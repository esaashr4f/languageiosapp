## The language of two small kingdoms

Biblical Hebrew is the language of most of the Hebrew Bible, the collection of books that Jews call the Tanakh and Christians the Old Testament. It was spoken and written in the southern Levant, in the hill country between the Jordan and the Mediterranean. In the first millennium BC that land held two small kingdoms, Israel in the north and Judah in the south, and later the Persian province of Yehud around Jerusalem.

Hebrew belongs to the Semitic family. Within it, it is a Northwest Semitic language of the Canaanite group, alongside Phoenician and Moabite. Aramaic is a near cousin in the same Northwest branch. Arabic is a more distant relative in the same Central Semitic group. If you know some Arabic, you will meet familiar machinery on almost every page: words built from consonant roots, a construct state like the *iḍāfa*, sentences with no verb "to be".

The earliest Hebrew writing yet found dates from about the tenth century BC. Hebrew went on being spoken after the biblical books were finished. It stopped being a mother tongue around AD 200, and lived on for seventeen centuries as a language of prayer, law, poetry and scholarship. Modern Hebrew is its revival as a spoken language, from the late nineteenth century. It is a separate language with its own guide in this app.

## One book, many centuries

The Hebrew Bible was not written at one time. Its texts took shape over roughly eight or nine centuries, from about the tenth century BC to the second. Scholars group its Hebrew into three broad layers.

**Archaic Hebrew** is the language of a few old poems, such as the Song of the Sea in Exodus 15 and the Song of Deborah in Judges 5.

**Classical Biblical Hebrew** (also called Standard Biblical Hebrew) is the prose of the great narrative, Genesis to 2 Kings, with Ruth. It is the most regular and the best described.

**Late Biblical Hebrew** is the language of books written after the Babylonian exile of the sixth century BC: Chronicles, Ezra, Nehemiah, Esther, Ecclesiastes (Qohelet) and the Hebrew parts of Daniel. It shows the influence of Aramaic.

A few chapters of the Bible (parts of Daniel and Ezra, one verse of Jeremiah) are not in Hebrew at all, but in Aramaic.

## Two layers on every page

Open any printed Hebrew Bible and you see two things at once. There are the letters, which are all consonants. Around and inside them sit small dots and dashes, which give the vowels and other details of pronunciation.

These two layers are not the same age.

The consonants are the text as the scribes wrote and copied it. They were fixed, with small variations, by the end of the biblical period.

The dots and dashes are called the **pointing** (in Hebrew *niqqud*). They were added by Jewish scholars called the **Masoretes**, "transmitters", working in the city of Tiberias in Galilee in the early Islamic period, roughly AD 750 to 950. Older grammars, including Gesenius, put the work a little earlier. Either way, the vowels were written down about a thousand years after most of the texts were composed.

So the pointing does not record how Isaiah or the scribes of Kings spoke. It records a **reading tradition**: how the text was recited aloud in the synagogue at Tiberias. That tradition was careful and old, and in most things it can be trusted. But it also contains features that arose after the biblical period, and this guide says so when they come up. Three examples you will meet in part 1:

- Six letters have two pronunciations, a hard one and a soft one (b and v, k and the *ch* of "Bach"). The soft forms probably arose under Aramaic influence, late in the biblical period or after it.
- Two letters, ḥet and ʿayin, each once stood for two different sounds. The pairs had merged by about 200 BC.
- One letter with a dot, sin, was read as plain s by the Masoretes. Earlier it was probably a different sound.

## Which Hebrew this guide teaches

This guide teaches Classical Biblical Hebrew, the prose of Genesis to Kings, read in the Tiberian pointing. It is a hybrid: a grammar of the first millennium BC, pronounced as it was read in the first millennium AD. There are three reasons for the choice.

First, it is what every printed Bible, every grammar and every dictionary uses, so every statement here can be checked.

Second, the pointing is the only full record of the vowels we have. Without it the grammar of the verb cannot be taught at all.

Third, where the reading tradition has added something of its own, the guide labels it. You learn the hybrid and, at the same time, where its seams are.

The text quoted is that of the Leningrad Codex, the oldest surviving manuscript that contains the whole Hebrew Bible, copied in about AD 1009 in the Tiberian tradition. The guide uses its digital transcription, the Westminster Leningrad Codex, and checks passages for reading against the maintained Unicode/XML edition.

Poetry, Late Biblical Hebrew, the old inscriptions and Biblical Aramaic are not ignored. Part 7 gives each its own lesson, and any form from them that appears earlier is labelled.

## What kind of language it is

Four facts shape everything that follows.

Words are built from **roots**: sets of usually three consonants that carry a core meaning. Vowels and small additions poured around the root say what kind of word it is. The next lesson is about this, because it is the organising idea of the grammar.

A story is told as a **chain of verbs**, each beginning with "and": "and he said... and it was... and he saw...". The verb usually comes first in its clause. The next lesson shows this too.

Nouns have no case endings. Who did what to whom is shown by word order, by small words and by prefixes.

The script is written right to left, and the vowels are marks added to the consonants. Part 1 teaches it from the beginning.

## What you already know

From Arabic you know roots and patterns, the pharyngeal consonants ḥ and ʿ, the definite article that doubles the next consonant, and sentences without a verb. From Spanish and French you know dozens of Hebrew words that passed through biblical translation: *amén*, *aleluya*, *sábado*, *sabbat*, *chérubin*. These are footholds. The grammar still has to be learnt on its own terms, and it starts on the next page.
