## The idea

Every language needs a way to make many words from a few pieces. English mostly glues pieces together in a row: *king*, *king-dom*, *king-ly*. Hebrew does something different.

A Hebrew word has a skeleton of consonants, usually three, called the **root**. The root carries a core of meaning. The three consonants מ ל ך, m-l-k, have to do with being king. The three consonants ש מ ר, š-m-r, have to do with guarding and keeping.

The root is not a word. You cannot say it. To make a word, you pour the root into a **pattern**: a fixed arrangement of vowels, sometimes with a doubled consonant or a prefix or suffix. The pattern says what kind of word it is and what job it does: "he did it", "he will do it", "someone doing it", "do it!", "the place or result of it".

That is the first half of the organising idea of Hebrew: **roots carry the meaning, patterns carry the grammar**. This guide writes a root with hyphens, m-l-k, and names the root of every new verb and noun, so that you learn to see roots as an Arabic speaker does.

## One root, many words

Here is the root m-l-k in five patterns. Do not try to learn the forms yet. The verb forms are taught in part 4. For now, watch the three consonants stay put while everything else moves.

:::example
orig: מֶלֶךְ
translit: meleḵ
morphemes: meleḵ
gloss: king
translation: king
note: The root m-l-k with e-vowels between the consonants: a noun.
source: Amos 7:13 (WLC)
confidence: attested
:::

:::example
orig: שְׁמֹר
translit: šəmōr
morphemes: šəmōr
gloss: guard.IMP.2SG.M

Put the two sets side by side and the logic shows. *Mālaḵ* and *šāmar* share a pattern, so they look alike and do the same job, though they mean different things. *Mālaḵ* and *mamlāḵâ* share a root, so they share a meaning, though they look unalike. Reading Hebrew is the habit of asking two questions of every word: which root, and which pattern?

## The narrative chain

The second half of the organising idea is about how a story is told.

English narrative strings past tenses together: "God said... there was light... God saw... God separated". Classical Hebrew prose uses one special verb form for this, again and again. It begins with *wa-* "and", and the next consonant is doubled. Grammars call it **wayyiqtol**, after its shape. Each wayyiqtol moves the story one event forward, and it stands first in its clause, before its subject.

:::example
orig: וַיֹּאמֶר אֱלֹהִים יְהִי אוֹר וַיְהִי־אוֹר׃
translit: wayyōʾmer ʾĕlōhîm yəhî ʾôr wayhî-ʾôr
morphemes: way-yōʾmer ʾĕlōhîm yəhî ʾôr way-hî ʾôr
gloss: WAYY-say.3SG.M God be.JUSS.3SG.M light WAYY-be.3SG.M light
literal: And-said God, "Let-be light"; and-was light.
translation: And God said, "Let there be light," and there was light.
note: Preview. Two wayyiqtol verbs, way-yōʾmer "and he said" and way-hî "and it was", each at the head of its clause.
source: Genesis 1:3 (WLC)
confidence: attested
preview: WAYY, JUSS, 3, SG, M
:::

:::example
orig: וַיַּרְא אֱלֹהִים אֶת־הָאוֹר כִּי־טוֹב וַיַּבְדֵּל אֱלֹהִים בֵּין הָאוֹר וּבֵין הַחֹשֶׁךְ׃
translit: wayyarʾ ʾĕlōhîm ʾeṯ-hāʾôr kî-ṭôḇ wayyaḇdēl ʾĕlōhîm bên hāʾôr ûḇên haḥōšeḵ
morphemes: way-yarʾ ʾĕlōhîm ʾeṯ hā=ʾôr kî ṭôḇ way-yaḇdēl ʾĕlōhîm bên hā=ʾôr û=ḇên ha=ḥōšeḵ
gloss: WAYY-see.3SG.M God OBJ DEF=light that good WAYY-separate.HIPH.3SG.M God between DEF=light and=between DEF=darkness
literal: And-saw God the light, that good; and-separated God between the light and-between the darkness.
translation: God saw that the light was good, and God separated the light from the darkness.
note: Preview. The chain continues: way-yarʾ "and he saw", way-yaḇdēl "and he separated". Each new event opens with way-.
source: Genesis 1:4 (WLC)
confidence: attested
preview: WAYY, OBJ, DEF, HIPH, 3, SG, M
:::

Read the left edge of each clause in those two verses: *way-*, *way-*, *way-*, *way-*. That is the rhythm of Hebrew storytelling. Older English translations kept it ("And God said... And God saw... And God divided"), which is why the King James Bible starts so many sentences with "And".

## Breaking the chain

When a writer wants to step off the main line of events, to describe a situation rather than add an event, he breaks the chain. The verse before the creation of light does exactly that.

:::example
orig: וְהָאָרֶץ הָיְתָה תֹהוּ וָבֹהוּ
translit: wəhāʾāreṣ hāyəṯâ ṯōhû wāḇōhû
morphemes: wə=hā=ʾāreṣ hāyəṯâ ṯōhû wā=ḇōhû
gloss: and=DEF=earth be.QTL.3SG.F formlessness and=emptiness
literal: And-the-earth was formlessness and-emptiness.
translation: Now the earth was formless and empty.
note: Preview. The noun comes first and the verb is not a wayyiqtol. This sets the scene; it does not move the story on.
source: Genesis 1:2, first clause (WLC)
confidence: attested
preview: DEF, QTL, 3, SG, F
:::

The verb here, *hāyəṯâ* "she was", is from the same family of patterns as *mālaḵ* and *šāmar*. Its subject, "the earth", stands in front of it. A Hebrew reader feels the difference at once: this is background, not the next event.

## What the guide does with this

Two things follow for the rest of the guide.

Part 4 teaches the verb by its patterns: one root, poured into a small number of shapes for person, and a few stems that change the meaning (for example "reign" and "make someone king" from the same m-l-k). Each new verb is given with its root, so you can see the root in other words.

Wayyiqtol is so common that you will meet a handful of wayyiqtol forms as words to learn, well before the lesson that explains their full system. Each time, the note says so and points ahead. *Way-yōʾmer* "and he said" alone occurs nearly two thousand times in the Hebrew Bible.

What exactly the verb forms mean (whether they mark time, completeness or the shape of a story) is one of the old debates of Hebrew grammar. This guide uses neutral names for them, explains the debate in part 4, and translates each form by its context. For narrative prose the practical meaning of wayyiqtol is not in doubt: "and then he did".
