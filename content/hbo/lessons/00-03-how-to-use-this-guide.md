## The route

The guide has nine parts after this one. Read them in order. Each lesson uses only what earlier lessons have taught, so skipping ahead will show you forms nobody has explained yet.

Part 1 teaches the script and the sounds: the letters, the vowel points and the other marks, in stages, ending with the first verses of Genesis read aloud. Part 2 gets you to short, complete sentences. Part 3 covers nouns and their machinery, and part 4, the longest, the verb. Parts 5 and 6 build longer sentences. Part 7 covers special topics, including poetry, Late Biblical Hebrew and the old inscriptions. Part 8 is real passages, read line by line, ending with the first chapter of Genesis. Part 9 is reference: tables, vocabulary and the book list.

Each lesson has the same shape. First the idea and the reason for it. Then how the form is built, step by step. Then examples, and from part 2 a map of where the construction applies. Then the traps. A table comes only after the pattern has been explained. The lesson ends with exercises and worked answers. Do the exercises before reading the answers.

## How to read an example

Here is the first verse of the Bible as this guide presents it. Most of its grammar is a preview: you will be able to build it yourself by the end of part 2.

:::example
orig: בְּרֵאשִׁית בָּרָא אֱלֹהִים אֵת הַשָּׁמַיִם וְאֵת הָאָרֶץ׃
translit: bərēʾšîṯ bārāʾ ʾĕlōhîm ʾēṯ haššāmayim wəʾēṯ hāʾāreṣ
morphemes: bə=rēʾšîṯ bārāʾ ʾĕlōhîm ʾēṯ ha=ššāmayim wə=ʾēṯ hā=ʾāreṣ
gloss: in=beginning create.QTL.3SG.M God OBJ DEF=heavens and=OBJ DEF=earth
literal: In-beginning created God (OBJ) the-heavens and-(OBJ) the-earth.
translation: In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth.
note: Preview, to show the layers of an example. Whether bərēʾšîṯ means "in the beginning" or "in the beginning of" (that is, "when God began to create") has been argued since the Middle Ages; the capstone reading in part 8 returns to it.
source: Genesis 1:1 (WLC)
confidence: attested
preview: QTL, 3, SG, M, OBJ, DEF
:::

The first line is the Hebrew, pointed, as printed. It runs **right to left**: the first word, *bərēʾšîṯ*, is the one on the right. The mark at the end, like a colon, closes the verse. The guide leaves out the musical accents printed in most Bibles, because they are cantillation and punctuation rather than grammar. Lesson 1.9 shows them and teaches the few that matter for reading.

The second line is the **transliteration**: the same words in Latin letters, left to right. The scheme is the academic one of the *SBL Handbook of Style*, which most English-language scholarship uses. It is nearly letter for letter, so you can turn it back into the pointed Hebrew. Part 1 builds it up sound by sound, and lesson 1.10 gives the whole scheme in one table.

The third line cuts each word at its joins, still in transliteration, so that it lines up with the line below. Two marks are used. An equals sign **=** joins a little word that is written as part of the next word: *wə=* "and", *ha=* "the", *bə=* "in". A hyphen **-** separates a word from an ending or prefix that belongs to it, such as a pronoun ending or the *way-* of the narrative verb.

The fourth line is the **gloss**. It labels each piece, word under word and piece under piece, in the same left-to-right order. Meanings are in lower case: *beginning*, *create*. Grammatical labels are in capitals: DEF for "the", OBJ for the little word *ʾēṯ* that marks a definite object. A full stop joins labels carried by a single piece: *bārāʾ* means "created" and "he" at once, so it is glossed create.QTL.3SG.M. Every label is explained in plain words in the lesson that introduces it, and all of them are in the glossary in part 9. Names keep their capitals: *God*, *Moses*.

A literal translation follows where it helps you see the Hebrew order. Then natural English, and a note on what the example is there to show. Translations are always this guide's own.

## Spelling and conventions

Two kinds of transliteration appear. Examples and new words always use the academic scheme: *šālôm*, *bərēʾšîṯ*. In running prose, familiar names and terms keep their ordinary English spelling: Torah, Shema, Masoretes, binyan.

The Hebrew consonants were written in two ways in different books. Some books write a vowel with an extra letter, others leave it out. The guide never corrects this. It copies the letters of the Leningrad Codex exactly, and lesson 1.4 explains the habit.

The name of the God of Israel is written with four consonants, YHWH. Jewish tradition does not pronounce it, and the Masoretes pointed it to be read *ʾăḏōnāy* "my Lord". The guide transliterates it **YHWH**, glosses it *Yhwh*, and translates it **the LORD**, as most English Bibles do. Lesson 1.9 explains how the pointing signals the substitute reading.

Stress is marked only in part 1 and in notes, with an acute accent on the transliteration: *méleḵ* "king" is stressed on its first syllable. The acute is the guide's, not part of the scheme.

## Real sentences and built ones

There are no living speakers of Biblical Hebrew. Everything known comes from texts. So the guide tells you where each example comes from. In the app, every example and table has a small coloured dot. Tap it to see its label and source.

- **attested**: real words from the Hebrew Bible, cited by book, chapter and verse. Most examples in this guide are attested, because the Bible is large and fully searchable.
- **verified**: a form, a single word or a table checked against a named source.
- **constructed**: a sentence built for this guide from rules and words that have each been checked. These appear where no verse shows a point cleanly with only the grammar taught so far.
- **uncertain**: the sources disagree, or the form is a scholarly reconstruction. The lesson says so in words as well.
- **unverified**: written but not yet checked against a source. You should not see this label in a finished guide.

Citations use English book names and the Hebrew chapter and verse numbers. These sometimes differ from English Bibles. In many Psalms the heading counts as verse 1 in Hebrew, so the English verse numbers run one behind. The last chapter of Malachi in English, 4:1–6, is Malachi 3:19–24 in Hebrew.

## Where the facts come from

The grammar follows *Gesenius' Hebrew Grammar*, edited by Kautzsch and translated by Cowley (1910), the standard reference grammar in English and free to read online. Lessons cite it by section, as "Gesenius §10".

Meanings follow the *Hebrew and English Lexicon* of Brown, Driver and Briggs (1906), known as BDB, which is arranged by root and so suits this guide's organising idea.

The text is the Westminster Leningrad Codex, as published with word-by-word analysis by the Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible project. Every attested example was checked word by word against it, and passages for reading against the Unicode/XML Leningrad Codex. The choice of words to teach follows counts from the same corpus, so that the commonest words come first.

The sounds follow Geoffrey Khan's account of the Tiberian pronunciation tradition (2020), the modern standard work on how the Masoretes read the text. Where a detail from it has not yet been checked against his chapters, the guide says so.

Wiktionary was used to check the pointing of individual words, never their meanings: its Hebrew entries mostly describe Modern Hebrew.

The annotated bibliography in part 9 gives full details and says what each source is good for.

## How much to memorise

Less than a school course would ask. The aim is that you understand why each form is what it is, so that you can rebuild it from its root and pattern. Tables are there to be consulted. What must become automatic, the letters and points, the commonest patterns and a few hundred words, will come through the exercises and the readings.
