## The idea

Each Hebrew letter stands for one consonant. The question is: as said by whom, and when?

There are three answers, and you will meet all three.

- **The Tiberian pronunciation** is the one the Masoretes recorded when they pointed the text, around AD 750 to 950. It has been reconstructed in detail by Geoffrey Khan from medieval descriptions. This is the guide's reference pronunciation, because it is the one the pointing was designed to write. When a later lesson says "the sound", it means this.
- **The biblical-period pronunciation** is older and only partly known. It is pieced together from spelling, from Greek transcriptions of Hebrew names, and from the related languages. The guide uses it only to explain why the system looks as it does.
- **The modern reading**, Israeli-style, is what most teachers and recordings use today. It is the pronunciation of Modern Hebrew. It merges several sounds that the Tiberian reading kept apart. You should recognise it, but it is not the subject of this guide.

Most of the twenty-two letters are easy for an English speaker. The interesting ones fall into four groups: the six letters with two sounds, the throat sounds, the "emphatic" sounds, and the letters whose value changed over time.

## How it is built

To pronounce a letter, ask four questions in order. Sounds are written in the International Phonetic Alphabet between square brackets; each symbol is one sound.

1. **Is it one of the six double letters, b g d k p t?** Each has a hard sound, a stop like English *b* or *t*, and a soft sound, a rubbing sound made by not quite closing the mouth. The soft ones are: ḇ [v], ḡ [ʁ] (a French or German *r*, made at the back of the mouth), ḏ [ð] (the *th* of "this"), ḵ [χ] (the *ch* of Scottish "loch"), p̄ [f], ṯ [θ] (the *th* of "thin"). A dot inside the letter marks the hard sound. Lesson 7 gives the rule for which one you hear. Hard k, p and t were said with a puff of breath, as in English.
2. **Is it a guttural?** The **gutturals** are the consonants made deep in the throat: alef ʾ [ʔ], he h [h], ḥet ḥ [ħ] and ʿayin ʿ [ʕ]. Alef is the catch in "uh-oh", a real consonant. Ḥet and ʿayin are the Arabic ح and ع, made by squeezing the throat below the tongue. Resh behaves partly like a guttural.
3. **Is it emphatic?** Ṭet ṭ [tˤ], ṣade ṣ [sˤ] and qof q [q] are the Arabic ط, ص and ق. Ṭ and ṣ are t and s said with the root of the tongue pulled back. Q is a k made further back, at the uvula.
4. **Otherwise**, the sound is close to English: z, y, l, m, n, s, h, and š as English "sh". Three details: waw was [v] in the Tiberian reading, though it is transliterated w because it was [w] earlier; resh was a uvular r like the French one; sin (ś) was said exactly like samekh, [s].

The gutturals matter far beyond pronunciation. Two facts about them explain a large share of what look like irregular forms later in the guide. First, they cannot be doubled: where another consonant would be pronounced twice, a guttural is pronounced once and the vowel before it changes. Second, they prefer a-vowels near them. Gesenius explains both from the nature of the sounds. You will meet them again in lessons 5 and 7, and constantly in parts 2 and 4.

## Watch out

**The older sounds.** The twenty-two letters were fewer than the consonants of early Hebrew, so some letters did double duty. Ḥet wrote both [ħ] and a sound like Arabic خ; ʿayin wrote both [ʕ] and a sound like Arabic غ. Greek transcriptions still keep them apart: Isaac (יִצְחָק) became Ἰσαάκ but Rachel (רָחֵל) Ῥαχήλ, and ʿazzâ became Γάζα. By about 200 BC each pair had merged. In the same way, ś was probably once a different sound, a lateral like Welsh *ll*, which is why it shares a letter with š and not with samekh.

**The soft letters are late.** The hard and soft pronunciations of b g d k p t probably developed under Aramaic influence, late in the biblical period. The authors of Genesis may well have said only [b], [g], [d]. Learn the soft sounds anyway: the pointing marks them, and the transliteration shows them.

**The emphatics' old sound is disputed.** The Tiberian ṭ and ṣ were pharyngealised, like Arabic. Whether they were so in the biblical period, or were ejectives as in Ethiopic, is not known. "Probably pharyngealised" is a fair summary.

**The modern reading merges.** In Israeli pronunciation ḥ is [χ], alef and ʿayin are usually silent, ṭ is t, q is k, ṣ is [ts], and soft g, d and t are said like the hard ones. If a teacher reads ʿam as *am*, that is why.

## Connections

If you read Arabic, the gutturals and emphatics are old friends: ح ع ط ص ق are the same sounds, and Arabic kept the خ and غ that Hebrew lost. BDB compares Hebrew *ṭôḇ* "good" with Arabic *ṭāba* "be pleasant", the same emphatic at the start. French *r* is a fair model for both Tiberian resh and soft gimel.

## Exercises

1. Say which consonant of *ṭôḇ* is emphatic and which is soft, and give the sound of each in IPA.
2. Which four consonants are the gutturals? Give one fact about their behaviour.
3. In śāḏeh and šānâ the first letter is the same shape. What tells you which sound it has?
4. A modern teacher reads עַם as *am* and חֹשֶׁךְ as *khóshekh*. Which Tiberian sounds has he replaced, and with what?
5. Why does the Greek Bible write Γάζα for ʿazzâ, with a g?
6. Samekh and sin sound the same in the Tiberian reading. Why does the script have two letters for them?

## Worked answers

1. Ṭet is emphatic, [tˤ], the Arabic ط. The final bet has no dot, so it is the soft [v].
2. Alef, he, ḥet and ʿayin. They cannot be doubled, and they prefer a-vowels near them. (Resh shares some of their behaviour.)
3. The dot. On the right arm (שׁ) it is shin, š; on the left arm (שׂ) it is sin, ś.
4. He has dropped ʿayin [ʕ] (silent in the modern reading) and replaced ḥet [ħ] with [χ]. The final soft kaf is [χ] in both readings.
5. Because when the translation was made, ʿayin still stood for two sounds, and in this name it was the one like Arabic غ, which Greek writers heard as a g. The two sounds merged later, by about 200 BC.
6. Because they were once different sounds. Sin was probably a lateral sound, and it was written with the same letter as shin. When sin merged with s, the old spelling stayed. The Tiberian reading pronounces both [s]; the spelling is an archaism.
