## The idea

For more than a thousand years the Hebrew Bible was copied with consonants only. Readers learnt the vowels by ear, from teachers, in the synagogue. After Hebrew stopped being spoken, around AD 200, that oral knowledge grew harder to keep. Scholars in several centres worked out ways of writing it down without changing a single letter of the sacred text. They added small marks around the letters: dots and dashes below, a dot above, a dot inside. These marks are the **vowel points**, and the whole system of marks is the **pointing**.

Three systems were invented. The Babylonian one put its vowel signs above the letters. A Palestinian one used yet other signs. The system made by the Masoretes of Tiberias, roughly between AD 750 and 950, displaced the others, and it is the one printed in every Hebrew Bible today.

What did the Tiberian Masoretes write down? Not the language of the biblical authors, but the way the text was read aloud in their own community. Their tradition was old and disciplined, and most of what it records is ancient. But it is a record of a reading, made a thousand years after the writing. The guide calls it the Tiberian reading.

The vowel signs record **quality**: which vowel it is. The Tiberian reading had seven full vowel qualities, and each has its own sign. The signs do not record **length**, how long the vowel is held. In Khan's reconstruction, length was automatic: a vowel was long when it carried the stress or stood at the end of an unstressed syllable, and short otherwise. Two vowels, ṣere and ḥolem, were always long.

Later grammarians, medieval and modern, described the signs differently. They sorted them into "long" vowels (qameṣ = long a, ṣere = long e, ḥolem = long o) and "short" ones (pataḥ = short a, segol = short e). That is a later analysis, not what the Masoretes meant. It is still useful, because it predicts how vowels change when a word grows, and this guide uses the words "long" and "short" in that sense from lesson 6. The macron in the transliteration (ā ē ō) follows the same tradition. Treat it as a label for the sign, not as a promise about length.

## How it is built

To read a pointed consonant, take three steps.

1. Find the consonant. The vowel sign under it, or the dot above its top left corner, belongs to it.
2. Say the consonant first and the vowel after it. A vowel sign written under a letter is always pronounced after that letter. בַּ is *ba*, not *ab*.
3. Name the sign and give it its value. This lesson teaches seven signs.

The seven, with the guide's transliteration and the Tiberian sound:

- **pataḥ**, a short line under the letter: *a* [a], the a of Spanish *casa*.
- **qameṣ**, a line with a small stem below it, like a tiny T: *ā* [ɔ], the vowel of British "law", held when long. This is the one that surprises most learners: the Masoretes said it as an open o, not as a.
- **segol**, three dots in a triangle: *e* [ɛ], as in "bed".
- **ṣere**, two dots side by side: *ē* [eː], French *é* held.
- **ḥireq**, one dot under the letter: *i* [i], as in "machine".
- **ḥolem**, one dot above the top left corner of the letter: *ō* [oː], French *eau* held.
- **qibbuṣ**, three dots in a slanting line: *u* [u], as in "rule". It is uncommon on its own; you will see it in lesson 7.

Three other vowel sounds are written with a consonant letter plus a dot (lesson 4), and a set of reduced vowels with a sign called shewa (lesson 5).

## Watch out

**Qameṣ is not a.** Most textbooks and nearly all modern readers say qameṣ as [a]. In the Tiberian reading it was [ɔ]. This is why the same sign also serves for a short o (lesson 6): the Masoretes heard one quality, open o, and wrote one sign. If you read qameṣ as a, your Hebrew will sound like modern Israeli reading; if you read it as open o, you will be closer to Tiberias.

**The macron is not length.** *Ā*, *ē*, *ō* name the signs qameṣ, ṣere and ḥolem. In the Tiberian reading, pataḥ, segol and ḥireq were also long when stressed or at the end of a syllable. *Máyim* has a long first vowel in the Tiberian reading though it is written *a*.

**Order on the page is not order in time.** The vowel sits under its consonant but is heard after it. With ḥolem the dot sits high on the left, sometimes over the next letter's right edge. Always attach it to the consonant before it in reading order.

**Not every dot is a vowel.** The dot inside a letter (lesson 7) and the dot on the arm of ש are not vowels. A vowel dot is under the letter (ḥireq) or above its left corner (ḥolem).

## Connections

Arabic writes its short vowels with marks too (fatḥa, kasra, ḍamma) and leaves them out in most printing, as Hebrew outside the Bible does. The Syriac Christians were pointing their texts before the Masoretes, and Gesenius held that the Hebrew system followed the Syriac pattern.

## Exercises

1. Read aloud and transliterate: לֵב, אֵשׁ, עֵץ. Which sign do all three share?
2. Transliterate זָהָב and say how the Tiberian reading of its vowels differs from the modern one.
3. In כֹּל, which mark is the vowel and which is not?
4. A student writes *mayim* and says its first vowel is short "because it has no macron". Correct him.
5. The Masoretes used one sign for open o in all positions. Explain from this why qameṣ can be read as *o* in some words.
6. Put these sounds in order of the marks for them in לֶחֶם: which consonant does each segol belong to?

## Worked answers

1. *lēḇ*, *ʾēš*, *ʿēṣ*. All three have ṣere, two dots side by side, [eː].
2. *zāhāḇ*. The Tiberian reading had open o [ɔ] for both qameṣ vowels. The modern reading says *zahav*, with a.
3. The dot above the top left corner is ḥolem, the vowel ō. The dot inside the kaf is not a vowel: it marks the hard k (lesson 7).
4. The macron is the guide's label for the signs qameṣ, ṣere and ḥolem; it does not mark length. In the Tiberian reading the stressed vowel of *máyim* was long, though it is pataḥ. The pointing records which vowel, not how long.
5. The sign records the quality [ɔ]. When that quality is long it is the qameṣ traditionally read *ā*; when it is short, in some unstressed syllables, it is the short o of qameṣ ḥaṭuf. The sound differed only in length, so the Masoretes saw no need for a second sign.
6. The first segol is under lamed: *le*. The second is under ḥet: *ḥe*. The mem at the end has no vowel. So the word is *leḥem*.
