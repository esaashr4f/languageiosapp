## The idea

Long before the Masoretes, scribes already had a partial way of writing vowels. Four letters, ה he, ו waw, י yod and sometimes א alef, could stand for a vowel instead of a consonant. Grammars call them **vowel letters**, or by their Latin name *matres lectionis*, "mothers of reading".

They began by accident, through sound change. Gesenius traces two routes.

The first was at the end of a word. When a final consonant or syllable dropped away in speech, a long vowel was left at the end. Scribes kept writing a letter there, and it came to mean "a vowel ends this word". Final ה became the usual sign for a final vowel.

The second was the **diphthong**: a vowel and a glide said in one syllable, like English "cow" or "my". Early Hebrew had *aw* and *ay*. In many words they contracted into single vowels: *aw* became *ô*, and *ay* became *ê*. The word for "day" was once *yawm*, as it still is in Arabic; it became *yôm*. The spelling did not change, so the ו that once wrote the consonant w now stood for the vowel ô. Once ו meant ô and י meant ê in such words, scribes began to use them for ô, û, ê and î elsewhere too. The oldest Hebrew inscriptions fit this account: they write vowel letters mainly for final vowels and for contracted diphthongs.

Use of vowel letters was never fixed by rule. Some writers and some periods used more, some fewer. A word spelled with its vowel letter is **full** (or *plene*); without it, **defective**. Both spellings can be the same word. In the Hebrew Bible the later books tend to spell more fully. The Masoretes did not tidy this up. They added their points to whatever letters the text had, so a pointed word shows both the old vowel letter and the Masoretic vowel sign.

## How it is built

When the Masoretes pointed a word with a vowel letter, the letter took no vowel of its own. The vowel sign stands on the consonant before it, and the vowel letter sits silently after. To read one, take three steps.

1. Find a ו, י or ה that has no vowel sign of its own and does not begin a syllable.
2. Look at the consonant before it. Its vowel sign and the vowel letter together make one vowel:
   - ḥolem with ו, written וֹ (the dot on top of the waw): *ô*;
   - a dot in the middle of ו, written וּ, called **shureq**: *û* (the same vowel as qibbuṣ);
   - ḥireq with י: *î*; ṣere or segol with י: *ê*;
   - at the end of a word, qameṣ with ה: *â*; segol with ה: *eh*; ṣere with ה: *ēh*; ḥolem with ה: *ōh*.
3. Write the vowel with a circumflex (*ô*, *û*, *î*, *ê*, *â*). The circumflex tells you that a vowel letter is there; the macron (*ō*, *ē*) tells you it is not. So the transliteration shows the spelling exactly.

Alef is a special case. When an alef that once was a consonant lost its sound next to a vowel, it stayed in the spelling. This guide still writes it, as ʾ: *rōʾš* "head", once *raʾš*.

## Watch out

**A vowel letter can also be a consonant.** ו and י are vowel letters only when they have no vowel of their own and follow a vowel. In *bayiṯ* the yod has ḥireq, so it is y. In *gôy* the final yod follows a full vowel and ends the word, so it is y again. Test each one: does it carry a vowel sign, or begin a syllable? Then it is a consonant.

**Shureq and a doubled waw look the same.** A dot in the middle of ו is shureq (*û*) when the letter before it has no vowel, or at the start of a word. If the letter before it has a vowel, the dot doubles the waw (lesson 7). Gesenius notes that printed texts cannot tell these apart by the dot's position.

**Final ה with a dot inside is a consonant.** Most words ending in ה end in a vowel. A few end in a real h, and then the ה carries a dot (lesson 7).

**Do not "correct" a spelling.** If one verse has לוֹא and another לֹא, both are right. The guide copies the Leningrad Codex exactly, and so should you when you quote.

## Connections

Arabic uses the same three letters, alif, wāw and yāʾ, to write its long vowels, and it went the same way from diphthong to spelling: Arabic *yawm* "day" and *bayt* "house" are the forms Hebrew contracted to *yôm* and *bêṯ*. The difference is that Arabic made the system regular, while Hebrew spelling stayed optional and uneven.

## Exercises

1. Transliterate תּוֹרָה and name each vowel letter in it.
2. In בַּיִת and בֵּית, is the yod a consonant or a vowel letter? Explain from the pointing.
3. Why does the transliteration write *ô* in *ʾôr* but *ō* in *kōl*?
4. Write the defective spelling of עוֹלָם and its transliteration.
5. Gesenius says the word for "day" was once *yawm*. Explain how its ו came to be read as a vowel.
6. A reader sees דָּוִיד in one book and דָּוִד in another and suspects two different men. What would you tell him?
7. Transliterate שָׂדֶה and פֹּה and say what the final ה does in each.

## Worked answers

1. *tôrâ*. The waw with a dot on top is ḥolem with waw, *ô*. The final he after qameṣ is the vowel letter for *â*. The taw and resh are consonants.
2. In *bayiṯ* the yod carries ḥireq, so it is the consonant y. In *bêṯ* the yod has no vowel of its own and follows ṣere, so it is a vowel letter; the two together are *ê*.
3. In *ʾôr* the vowel is written with ḥolem and a waw; the circumflex records the waw. In *kōl* the ḥolem stands alone, with no vowel letter; the macron records that. The vowel sound is the same.
4. עֹלָם, *ʿōlām*: ḥolem over ʿayin, with no waw.
5. The diphthong *aw* contracted into the single vowel *ô*. The scribes kept the old spelling with ו, which had written the w of the diphthong. Since the w was no longer heard, the letter came to stand for the vowel.
6. They are the same name. The second spelling is defective, the first full; the sound is the same, *dāwiḏ*. Later books such as Chronicles prefer the full spelling.
7. *śāḏeh* and *pōh*. In both, the final he is a vowel letter, not a consonant: it marks the final vowel (*eh* after segol, *ōh* after ḥolem).
