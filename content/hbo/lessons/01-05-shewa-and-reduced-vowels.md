## The idea

One more sign is needed to read Hebrew: two dots stacked vertically under a letter, ְ. It is called **shewa**, and it is the commonest mark in the Bible after the letters themselves.

Shewa says "no full vowel here". That covers two different situations, and the reader has to tell them apart.

- **Silent shewa** marks a consonant that closes a syllable. Nothing is pronounced after it. In מִדְבָּר *miḏ-bār* "wilderness" the shewa under dalet tells you the d ends the first syllable.
- **Vocal shewa** marks a consonant followed by a very short vowel, a mere flick of sound. In בְּרִית *bərîṯ* "covenant" you cannot say b and r together at the start; a short vowel comes between them.

Why would one sign do both jobs? Because the Masoretes were marking a fact about structure, not a sound: this consonant has no full vowel of its own. Whether a short vowel was heard depended on where the consonant stood, and a trained reader knew. Gesenius adds the history. Vocal shewa usually stands where the language once had a full short vowel in an open syllable. Hebrew reduced those vowels; Arabic kept them. The word for "blessing", *bərāḵâ* in Hebrew, still has a full first vowel in its Arabic counterpart.

What did vocal shewa sound like? European grammars say *ĕ* or *ə*, a neutral murmur. Khan's reconstruction of the Tiberian reading is more precise: normally a short [a]; before a guttural, a short copy of the vowel after that guttural; before yod, a short [i]. The old Greek translation supports this. It wrote Samuel, *šəmûʾēl*, as Σαμουήλ, with an a, and Sodom, *səḏōm*, as Σόδομα, copying the next vowel. This guide writes vocal shewa *ə* and silent shewa as nothing.

Gutturals add a twist. A guttural that begins a syllable cannot take a bare murmur; it needs a definite short vowel. So the Masoretes combined shewa with a vowel sign to make three **hatef** ("hurried") vowels: ḥaṭef pataḥ *ă*, ḥaṭef segol *ĕ* and ḥaṭef qameṣ *ŏ*. They are always pronounced, always short, and found mostly under ʾ, h, ḥ and ʿ.

## How it is built

To decide whether a shewa is vocal or silent, take these steps in order. They are the school rules set out by Gesenius, and the guide's transliteration follows them.

1. **Is it at the end of the word?** Then it is silent. This includes the shewa written in final kaf, ךְ, which is there only to tell the letter apart from final nun.
2. **Is it under the first consonant of the word?** Then it is vocal: *bərîṯ*.
3. **Is it the second of two shewas in a row?** Then the first is silent and the second vocal: *yiš-mə-rû*.
4. **Is the consonant doubled?** Then the shewa is vocal. (Doubling is lesson 7.)
5. **What vowel stands before it?** After a short vowel, in a closed syllable, it is silent: *miḏ-bār*. After a long vowel it counts as vocal: *šō-mə-rîm*.

A hatef vowel needs no test. It is always pronounced.

## Watch out

**The same two dots, two different jobs.** Never read every shewa aloud. *Miḏbār* said as *mi-də-bār* is wrong: the shewa closes a syllable. And never drop every shewa: *bərîṯ* said as *brîṯ* is wrong.

**After a long vowel the sources disagree.** The school rule (step 5) makes the shewa vocal after a long vowel, as in *šōmərîm*. Khan shows from medieval descriptions that the Tiberian reading generally said such a shewa silently, with a short echo of the long vowel before the consonant. It was vocal there only in certain cases, for example on a guttural, where the Masoretes wrote a hatef. The guide keeps the school transliteration, *šōmərîm*, because every grammar and dictionary uses it, but you should know that it is a convention.

**A hatef is not a full vowel.** *Ḥămôr* has a short, hurried first vowel.

**Final kaf has a shewa that means nothing.** The two dots in ךְ do not make a vowel. *Meleḵ* ends in a plain [χ].

## Connections

Arabic shows what shewa has worn away. Where Hebrew has a vocal shewa, Arabic often has a full short vowel, as Gesenius notes for *bərāḵâ* "blessing". Arabic also has a sign for "no vowel here", the *sukūn*, which does the job of silent shewa. Hebrew packs both jobs into one sign.

## Exercises

1. Divide מִדְבָּר into syllables and say whether its shewa is vocal or silent, with the rule.
2. In יִשְׁמְרוּ there are two shewas. Which is vocal and why?
3. Transliterate בְּרִית and שְׁמוּאֵל. What do the two words have in common?
4. Why does the word for "God" begin with ḥaṭef segol rather than with plain shewa?
5. How would the Tiberian reading have pronounced the first vowel of *bərîṯ*, according to Khan?
6. A student reads מַלְכֵי as *ma-lə-ḵê*. What is wrong, and what is the right reading?
7. Transliterate חֲמוֹר and חֳדָשִׁים and name the hatef vowel in each.

## Worked answers

1. *miḏ-bār*. The shewa under dalet follows the short vowel i and closes the first syllable, so it is silent (rule 5).
2. The second, under mem, is vocal: it is the second of two shewas in a row (rule 3). The first, under shin, is silent and closes the syllable *yiš*.
3. *bərîṯ* and *šəmûʾēl*. Both begin with a shewa under the first consonant, which is always vocal.
4. Alef is a guttural. A guttural that begins a syllable needs a definite vowel, not a bare murmur, so the Masoretes wrote a hatef. Here it is ḥaṭef segol, *ʾĕ*.
5. As a short [a], the default value of vocal shewa: roughly *ba-rîṯ*. (Before r no special rule applies.)
6. The shewa follows the short vowel a and closes the syllable, so it is silent. The word is *mal-ḵê*, two syllables.
7. *ḥămôr*, with ḥaṭef pataḥ (*ă*) under ḥet; *ḥŏḏāšîm*, with ḥaṭef qameṣ (*ŏ*) under ḥet.
