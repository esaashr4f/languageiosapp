## The idea

A **syllable** is one beat of a word: a vowel with the consonants that lean on it. *Dāḇār* has two, *dā* and *ḇār*.

Hebrew syllables are built to a strict plan. Gesenius states the first rule plainly: **every syllable begins with a consonant**, and with only one. A Hebrew syllable never starts with a bare vowel and never with two consonants together. Alef counts as a consonant, which is why a word like *ʾāḏām* begins with one.

A syllable can end in two ways. An **open** syllable ends in its vowel: *dā*. A **closed** syllable ends in a consonant: *ḇār*. (A vocal shewa is not a syllable of its own. It is a short lead-in to the next one, and grammars call it a half-syllable.)

Why does this matter? Because the vowels follow the syllables. In the school description that Gesenius gives, an open syllable has a long vowel, and a closed syllable without the stress has a short one. In Khan's account of the Tiberian reading, length was automatic: stressed and open syllables were long, others short. Both say the same thing from opposite ends. Where you know the syllable, you know what kind of vowel to expect, and where you see the vowel, you can tell what kind of syllable it is.

That gives you the key to the one ambiguous sign in the pointing. Qameṣ (ָ) normally stands for *ā*, open [ɔ] held long. But the same sign also stands for a short o, called **qameṣ ḥaṭuf**, "hurried qameṣ". The Masoretes used one sign because the quality was the same, [ɔ]; only the length differed, and length was predictable. So you can predict it too: a qameṣ in a closed syllable without the stress is short, *o*.

## How it is built

To divide a word and read its qameṣ, take five steps.

1. Find the full vowels (including the vowel letters of lesson 4 and the hatef vowels). Each full vowel is the centre of one syllable.
2. Give each vowel the one consonant before it. That consonant starts the syllable.
3. Any consonant left over, with silent shewa or at the end of the word, closes the syllable before it: *ḥoḵ-mâ*, *miḏ-bār*.
4. Find the stress. Most words are stressed on the last syllable; lesson 8 gives the details. In part 1 the notes tell you.
5. Look at each qameṣ. In a closed syllable without the stress, read *o*. Elsewhere read *ā*.

Two exceptions to "every syllable begins with a consonant" are worth knowing now. The word "and" is sometimes the bare vowel *û-* (part 2). And a ḥet or ʿayin at the end of a word, after a vowel other than a, takes a **furtive pataḥ**: a short a slipped in before the guttural so that it is clearly heard. It is written under the guttural but said before it: *rûaḥ*, not *rûḥa*.

There is one trap in step 5. In a printed Bible, a small vertical stroke called meteg sometimes stands beside a qameṣ. It shows that the syllable is open and the vowel long, even though a shewa follows. This guide strips meteg from the Hebrew to keep it legible, so the transliteration makes the decision for you: *ā* or *o*.

## Watch out

**Same letters, same points, different words.** *Ləʾoḵlâ* "for food" and *ʾāḵəlâ* "it consumed" are pointed alike in this guide's Hebrew. In WLC the verb has a meteg and the noun does not. When you meet such a pair in a printed Bible, look for the meteg. Where the guide's text has stripped it, trust the transliteration.

**Not every qameṣ before a shewa is o.** *Šāmərû*-type forms, where the qameṣ is open and the shewa vocal, keep ā. The test is whether the syllable is closed, not whether a shewa follows.

**Kol and kōl are one word.** כֹּל *kōl* "all" is spelled with ḥolem when it stands alone. Before maqqef it is written with qameṣ, כָּל־, and read *kol*. The vowel letter does not change, and the meaning does not either. The quality is the same [ɔ]-like vowel; only the length and the sign differ.

**Furtive pataḥ is not a second syllable.** Do not read *rûaḥ* as *rû-ḥa* or as *ru-a-ḥa*. The pataḥ is a glide into the guttural.

## Connections

Arabic has the same rule that a syllable starts with exactly one consonant, and fills a word-initial cluster with a helping vowel, as in *ibn* "son". Hebrew *šālôm* and Arabic *salām* are the same word, and the different first consonants (š and s) are the regular correspondence between the two languages.

## Exercises

1. Divide דָּבָר and שָׁלוֹם into syllables and say whether each is open or closed.
2. Read חָכְמָה aloud and explain the value of its first vowel.
3. Why is the qameṣ of כָּל־ read *o*, while the ḥolem of כֹּל is *ō*?
4. Divide וַיָּקָם and say which syllable has qameṣ ḥaṭuf, and why.
5. Transliterate רוּחַ and say where the pataḥ is heard.
6. In a printed Bible you see אָֽכְלָה with a small vertical stroke beside the first qameṣ. Is the word *ʾoḵlâ* or *ʾāḵəlâ*? Explain.
7. Give two exceptions to the rule that every syllable begins with a consonant.

## Worked answers

1. *dā-ḇār*: open, closed. *šā-lôm*: open, closed.
2. *ḥoḵ-mâ*. The shewa is silent and closes the first syllable. A closed syllable without the stress has a short vowel, so this qameṣ is the short o, qameṣ ḥaṭuf.
3. Maqqef joins כָּל־ to the next word, and the first word loses its stress. So its syllable is closed and unstressed, and the qameṣ is short o. Standing alone, the word is stressed and written with ḥolem, *kōl*.
4. *way-yā́-qom*. The stress is on *yā*. The last syllable, *qom*, is closed and unstressed, so its qameṣ is the short o. The *yā* is stressed and open, so it is ā.
5. *rûaḥ*. The pataḥ is written under the ḥet but heard before it, as a glide after the û.
6. *ʾāḵəlâ*. The stroke is meteg: it marks the syllable as open, so the qameṣ is long ā and the shewa after it is vocal. Without meteg, as in *ləʾoḵlâ*, the syllable would be closed and the qameṣ short o.
7. The conjunction "and" when it is the bare vowel *û-*, and a furtive pataḥ before a final guttural.
