## The idea

One small dot sits inside many letters in a pointed Bible. It is called **dagesh**, and it does two different jobs. Gesenius explains the name as "sharpening" a letter, which covers both.

**Dagesh forte** ("strong dagesh") **doubles** a consonant: the sound is held, closing one syllable and opening the next, as in Italian *fatto* or English "book-keeper". רַבִּים is *rab-bîm*, not *ra-bîm*. The transliteration writes the letter twice.

**Dagesh lene** ("weak dagesh") **hardens** one of the six letters b g d k p t, which grammars call the **begadkefat** letters after a made-up word that strings them together. It tells you to say the stop (b, k, t) rather than the soft sound (v, ch, th) you met in lesson 2.

Why do the six letters have two sounds? In the Tiberian reading, b g d k p t were softened whenever a vowel came right before them. The mouth, moving from an open vowel, did not close fully. After a consonant, or at the start of speech, there was nothing to soften them, and they stayed hard. The hard sound is the original. The softening probably came into Hebrew under Aramaic influence, during or after the biblical period. Lesson 2 called it a feature of the reading tradition that the language acquired late; here is its rule.

A third use of the same dot is **mappiq**, in a final ה. It tells you that the he is a real consonant h, not a vowel letter (lesson 4).

## How it is built

To decide what a dot inside a letter means, take four steps.

1. **Is the letter a final ה?** Then the dot is mappiq: say h. (A dot in ו with no vowel before it is shureq, lesson 4.)
2. **Does a full vowel come right before the letter, in the same word?** Then the dot is dagesh forte: double the letter. A doubled b g d k p t is always hard: *rabbîm*, not *raḇḇîm*. Gesenius gives this test: dagesh forte always has a vowel before it, dagesh lene never does.
3. **Otherwise, is it one of b g d k p t?** Then it is dagesh lene: say the hard sound. This happens at the start of a word, and after a closed syllable (after a silent shewa): *miḏ-bār*.
4. **A b g d k p t with no dot** is soft. It follows a vowel or a vocal shewa: *dāḇār*, *bərîṯ*.

Two refinements.

First, words run together. A begadkefat letter at the start of a word is soft if the word before ends in a vowel and the two are said together without a pause: *wayhî-ḵēn* "and it was so". At the start of a verse or after a pause it is hard. Lesson 9 shows how the accents mark those pauses.

Second, a few soft letters stand after a silent shewa, against step 3. *Malḵê* "kings of" has a soft k. Gesenius reports the explanation: the softening is older than the loss of the vowel. The form was once something like *malaḵê* (Gesenius writes *mălăkhê*, §21f); the k was softened after the second a, and only later did that vowel drop. History left the soft sound behind.

Where does dagesh forte come from? Gesenius lists three sources. A consonant can be absorbed into the next one: *yittēn* "he will give" was once *yintēn*. Some patterns double a root letter as part of their meaning: *lāmaḏ* "he learned" but *limmaḏ* "he taught". And a short vowel can be kept short by doubling the consonant after it. Gutturals and resh do not take dagesh forte (lesson 2; Gesenius lists a handful of exceptions with resh, §22s).

## Watch out

**Forte or lene? Look left of the dot, not at the dot.** The two look identical. What decides is the sound before the letter: a full vowel means forte; a consonant, a silent shewa or the start of a word means lene. In *kissēʾ* the same mark has both meanings within one word.

**A soft letter is not a different letter.** *Nāṯan* and *yittēn* share the root n-t-n. The t is soft in one and hard in the other because of what stands before it, not because the root has changed. Learn to see through the dot to the root.

**Gutturals refuse doubling.** Where the pattern calls for a doubled guttural or resh, there is no dagesh, and the vowel before it often changes instead. Part 2 shows this with the article.

**Do not over-apply the rule across words.** The softening at the start of a word depends on the word before and on the pause between them. Read the verse as a whole (lesson 9) before deciding.

## Connections

Arabic marks doubling with a sign of its own, the *shadda*, and has no hard and soft alternation: Arabic *b* and *k* never soften. The Hebrew pair *lāmaḏ* "learn" and *limmaḏ* "teach" works like Arabic *ʿalima* "know" and *ʿallama* "teach", where doubling the middle root letter makes someone else do the action.

## Exercises

1. In כִּסֵּא, name the job of each dot.
2. Transliterate מִשְׁפָּט and explain why the p is hard.
3. Why does יִתֵּן have a doubled t, when the root is n-t-n?
4. Transliterate בַּיִת and say which letters are hard and which soft, and why.
5. Explain the different endings of אַרְצָהּ and אַרְצָה.
6. In *wayhî-ḵēn*, why is the first letter of *kēn* soft?
7. Gesenius says dagesh forte always has a vowel before it. Use that to classify the dots in רַבִּים and מִדְבָּר.

## Worked answers

1. The dot in kaf, at the start of the word, is dagesh lene: hard k. The dot in samekh, after the vowel i, is dagesh forte: the s is doubled, *kis-sēʾ*.
2. *mišpāṭ*. The pe comes after a silent shewa, which closes the first syllable. After a consonant a begadkefat letter keeps its hard sound, so the dot is dagesh lene.
3. The n of the root has been absorbed into the following t. Two consonants became one doubled consonant, *yintēn* > *yittēn*, and dagesh forte marks the doubling.
4. *bayiṯ*. The bet is hard: it starts the word, and the dot is dagesh lene. The taw is soft: it follows the vowel i and has no dot.
5. In אַרְצָהּ the dot in the he is mappiq: the he is a consonant, *ʾarṣāh* "her land". In אַרְצָה there is no dot: the he is a vowel letter, *ʾarṣâ* "to the land".
6. The word before, *wayhî*, ends in a vowel, and maqqef joins the two words into one unit of speech. A begadkefat letter right after a vowel is soft.
7. In רַבִּים the bet has the vowel a before it, so its dot is forte: *rabbîm*. In מִדְבָּר the bet has a silent shewa before it, so its dot is lene: hard b, not doubled.
