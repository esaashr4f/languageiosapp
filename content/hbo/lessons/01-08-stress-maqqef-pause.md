## The idea

**Stress** is the extra weight one syllable of a word carries: the "TEN" of "ten-ta-tive". In Hebrew it is worth knowing for two reasons. It changes the sound of a word, and it moves the vowels. Many of the vowel changes you will meet in parts 3 and 4 happen because the stress has moved and the vowels around it have grown or shrunk to fit.

The basic fact is simple. **Most Hebrew words are stressed on the last syllable.** The Jewish grammarians called such a word *milraʿ*, "stressed below", meaning at the end. A smaller group is stressed on the next-to-last syllable, *milʿêl*, "stressed above". Almost without exception, Hebrew stress falls on one of the last two syllables.

Which words are stressed early? Mostly three kinds.

- Words of the type *méleḵ*, *léḥem*, *šémeš*, *báyiṯ*, *náḥal*, called **segolates** after the segol vowels most of them have. Gesenius explains them as old one-syllable words, such as *naḥl* and *bayt*, which took a helping vowel to break up the final pair of consonants. The helping vowel never takes the stress, so the stress stays where the old single vowel was.
- Words with certain endings: *láylâ* "night", the ending *-áyim* of *máyim*, and the "towards" ending *-â* of *ʾárṣâ* (lesson 7).
- Some verb forms, which part 4 will point out.

Stress also moves. When a word grows at the end, the stress moves to the new last syllable, and the vowels before it reduce: *dāḇā́r* "word" but *dəḇārî́m* "words". When two stressed syllables would collide across words, the first stress can step back. And the narrative form wayyiqtol often pulls the stress back from the last syllable: *yōʾmár* "he will say" but *wayyṓʾmer* "and he said". Gesenius lists all three movements.

Two further things affect stress across words.

**Maqqef** is a short hyphen-like stroke at the top of the line that joins two or more words into one unit of stress. Only the last word keeps its stress. The words before it are said lightly, like "of" in "cup of tea". Short words, especially little prepositions like עַל *ʿal* "on" and כָּל *kol* "all", almost always stand before a maqqef. The transliteration shows maqqef with a hyphen.

**Pause** is the strong stress on the last word before a major break in the verse: at the end of the verse, and at its main midpoint. Many words change their form in pause. A short stressed vowel becomes long, and a segol often becomes qameṣ: *máyim* becomes *mā́yim*, and *ʾéreṣ* becomes *ʾā́reṣ*. These **pausal forms** are a feature of the solemn reading of the text. They are not different words, and this guide glosses them like the normal form, with "pausal" in the note.

## How it is built

To find the stress of a word, take three steps.

1. Look at the last syllable. Unless you have a reason to think otherwise, it carries the stress.
2. Check for the early-stress types: a segolate pattern (two short e or a vowels with the first stressed), the endings *-áyim*, *-â* "towards", the word *láylâ*. Then the next-to-last syllable is stressed.
3. Check the context. Before maqqef, the word has no stress of its own. At the end of a verse or its main midpoint, expect a pausal form.

Printed Bibles mark the stress for you: most of the accents (lesson 9) stand on the stressed syllable. This guide has removed them from its Hebrew, so in part 1 the notes give the stress with an acute accent on the transliteration.

## Watch out

**Do not stress the helping vowel.** *Méleḵ*, not *meléḵ*; *náḥal*, not *naḥál*. The second vowel of a segolate was added to ease pronunciation and never takes the stress.

**A pausal form is not a new word.** *Mā́yim* and *máyim*, *ʾā́reṣ* and *ʾéreṣ*, are the same words read with the weight of a pause. Do not look them up separately, and do not use pausal forms in sentences you build yourself unless they end a verse.

**Not every qameṣ in ʾāreṣ is pausal.** With the article, the word is always *hāʾāreṣ*, in every position: the Leningrad Codex has it over nine hundred times and never with segol. Part 2 explains why.

**Maqqef is not a hyphen inside a word.** The words it joins are separate words with separate meanings. In the morpheme line of an example they are written apart.

## Connections

Arabic has no maqqef, but its short words also lean on the next word in speech, and Arabic too has special pause forms: a word at the end of a phrase drops its short final vowel (*kitābun* becomes *kitāb*). Hebrew pause works the other way, by lengthening, but the idea of a special form for the end of a unit is shared.

## Exercises

1. Mark the stress on *dāḇār*, *meleḵ* and *laylâ*.
2. Why does the first vowel of *dāḇār* become a shewa in *dəḇārîm*?
3. Explain why *náḥal* is not stressed on its last syllable.
4. In *ʿal-pənê*, which word has the stress?
5. Genesis 1:6 has מַיִם and then לָמָיִם. Why are the vowels different?
6. In Genesis 1:5, *qārāʾ* is stressed *qā́rāʾ*. Give the reason.
7. A student meets אָרֶץ at the end of a verse and looks for it in the dictionary under a new entry. What should he look up instead?

## Worked answers

1. *dāḇā́r* (last syllable); *méleḵ* (first: a segolate); *láylâ* (first: the final -â is unstressed).
2. The ending *-îm* takes the stress, and the stress moves forward. The first vowel is now two syllables before the stress. Vowels that far from the stress tend to reduce, and here it reduces to vocal shewa.
3. It is a segolate. Its second vowel is a helping vowel, added to an old one-syllable form *naḥl*. The helping vowel never takes the stress, so the stress stays on the first syllable.
4. *pənê*. Maqqef makes the two words one unit of stress, and only the last keeps its stress.
5. The second stands at the end of the verse, in pause. In pause the stressed short vowel becomes long: pataḥ becomes qameṣ, *mā́yim*. Even the prefix lengthens to *lā-*.
6. The next word, *lā́ylâ*, is stressed on its first syllable. Two stressed syllables would collide, so the stress of *qārāʾ* steps back one syllable.
7. *ʾereṣ* "earth, land". *ʾĀreṣ* is its pausal form.
