## The idea

A printed Hebrew Bible has a third layer of marks besides the letters and the vowel points: small signs above and below the words, about thirty kinds in all. They are the **accents** (in Hebrew *ṭəʿāmîm*, "tastes" or "senses"). The Masoretes added them with the vowels.

The accents do three jobs at once, as Gesenius explains.

1. They are **musical notes**. The text was chanted, and each accent stands for a short melody. This is their purpose in the synagogue, and the reason there are so many.
2. They mark the **stress**. Most accents stand on the stressed syllable of their word, which is how lesson 8's rules can be checked.
3. They are **punctuation**. Some accents separate a word from what follows, like a comma or a full stop. Others join a word to the next one. The separating accents are called **disjunctive** and the joining ones **conjunctive**.

For reading, the third job is the one that matters. The accents are the Masoretes' analysis of every verse: where the main break falls, which words belong together, how the sense runs. They are not always right, but they are an old and careful reading, and the pausal forms of lesson 8 depend on them.

This guide removes the accents from its Hebrew everywhere except in this lesson, for three reasons: they are chant and punctuation rather than grammar, they crowd a small screen, and without them the text is easier to handle. When you read a printed Bible you will see them, and this lesson teaches you the few that you need.

There is a fourth kind of Masoretic note on the page. Here and there the Masoretes were not willing to change a letter of the text, but they knew it was to be read differently. They left the written letters, called the **ketiv** ("what is written"), and put the vowels of the word to be read, the **qere** ("what is read"), under them, with the letters of the qere in the margin. In a few very common words the correction is so regular that it is never noted in the margin at all. This is called a **perpetual qere**.

## How it is built

To divide a verse with the accents, take four steps.

1. Find the end. The last word carries **silluq**, a short vertical stroke under its stressed syllable, and is followed by **sof pasuq**, the two dots ׃ that end the verse.
2. Find the main break. The strongest accent inside the verse is **atnaḥ**, a small caret under the stressed syllable (֑). It splits the verse into two halves, like a semicolon. In a short verse its place may be taken by **ṭipḥa** (֖).
3. Divide each half. The next strongest accents split the halves: **segolta**, three dots above the last letter (֒), and **zaqef qaton**, two dots one above the other over the word (֔). Below them come **rebiaʿ**, a single dot above (֗), and **ṭipḥa**, a slanting stroke below (֖), which often marks the word just before silluq or atnaḥ.
4. Treat every other accent, for now, as "joining". The commonest joining accents are **munaḥ** (֣), an angle below, and **mereḵa** (֥), a slanting stroke below. A maqqef (lesson 8) is the strongest join of all.

To read a word with a ketiv and qere, take two steps.

1. Read the vowels, not the letters. The points belong to the qere.
2. If the letters do not fit the vowels, look at the margin (or, in this guide, the note) for the letters of the word to read. The guide's transliteration and gloss always follow the qere.

## Watch out

**Do not read the letters of the divine name.** In the Masoretic reading, יְהוָה is never pronounced as written. The vowels you see are borrowed, and the WLC pointing does not even give a full set. Readings like "Jehovah" came from combining the consonants with the borrowed vowels; that combination was never a Hebrew word.

**Two vowels under one letter are not a mistake.** In יְרוּשָׁלַ͏ִם the pataḥ and ḥireq together give *-ayim*. On some screens the two marks may be drawn in the wrong order; the transliteration settles it.

**The accents are not grammar.** A verse divided by atnaḥ in one way could often be divided in another. The accents record one reading, and modern translations sometimes depart from it. Use them as a guide to the Masoretes' understanding, and as the reason for pausal forms.

**Not every unusual spelling is a ketiv.** Lesson 4's full and defective spellings are both read as written. A ketiv is a case where the text's letters and the vowels do not match.

## Connections

Arabic has its own tradition of marks for recitation in the Qurʾān: signs for pauses (where one must, may or must not stop) and for melodic features, added to the consonants and vowel marks. The Hebrew accents are the same kind of thing, a recitation tradition written into the text, though much more elaborate.

## Exercises

1. In Genesis 1:1, which word carries atnaḥ, and what are the two halves of the verse?
2. Which two marks end every verse, and where do they stand?
3. In Genesis 1:2, name the accent that divides the whole verse and the word it stands on.
4. Explain why יְהוָה has the vowels it has, and how the guide writes and translates it.
5. Why does Genesis 15:2 point the divine name differently, as יֱהוִה?
6. What does the guide's transliteration follow when a word has a ketiv and a qere?
7. A friend says that "Jehovah" is the Hebrew pronunciation of the name. Correct him.

## Worked answers

1. *ʾĕlōhîm*. The halves are "In the beginning God created" and "the heavens and the earth".
2. Silluq, under the stressed syllable of the last word, and sof pasuq, the two dots ׃ after it.
3. Atnaḥ, on *ṯəhôm*: it separates the darkness over the deep from the wind of God over the water.
4. The letters are the divine name, which is not pronounced. The vowels are those of the word read instead, *ʾăḏōnāy* "Lord" (WLC gives only shewa and qameṣ). The guide writes YHWH and translates "the LORD".
5. There *ʾăḏōnāy* already stands before it. To avoid saying *ʾăḏōnāy ʾăḏōnāy*, the name is pointed with the vowels of *ʾĕlōhîm* and read *ʾĕlōhîm*.
6. The qere: the guide gives the word that is read, and the note records the written letters.
7. "Jehovah" puts the consonants of the name together with vowels borrowed from *ʾăḏōnāy*. The Masoretes meant those vowels as a signal to read *ʾăḏōnāy*, not as part of the name, so the combination was never pronounced in Hebrew.
