## The idea

English cannot say "Samuel a prophet" and mean a full sentence. It needs "is". Spanish needs *es* or *está*, French *est*. Biblical Hebrew does not. To say that something is something, you put the two words side by side, and that is the sentence:

*šəmûʾēl nāḇîʾ*, "Samuel is a prophet".

A few terms first. A **clause** is a group of words that makes one statement. The **subject** is what the clause is about: *šəmûʾēl*. The **predicate** is what the clause says about it: *nāḇîʾ*. A clause whose predicate is not a verb is a **verbless clause**. Gesenius calls it a noun clause, and says that the link between subject and predicate "is as a rule expressed by simple juxtaposition, without a copula of any kind" (Gesenius §141f). A *copula* is a linking word like English "is".

This is not a gap in the language. The verbless clause is one of the basic types of Hebrew clause, beside the verbal clause, and Gesenius gives it its own section (§141). A verbal clause tells you that something happened. A verbless clause describes a state: who someone is, what something is like, where it is.

Because there is no verb, there is no tense. The clause does not say whether the state is past, present or future. You read that from the story around it (Gesenius §141f). In a conversation a verbless clause is usually present. In a narrative it is usually past. Where the time must be made explicit, Hebrew can use the verb *hāyâ* "be, become", but that is a different kind of clause, taught in part 4.

## How it is built

The predicate can be one of four things (Gesenius §141b):

- a noun: *šəmûʾēl nāḇîʾ* "Samuel is a prophet";
- an adjective, a word that describes, such as *ṭôḇ* "good" or *gāḏôl* "big";
- a number, such as *ʾeḥāḏ* "one";
- a phrase that says where, such as *ʿal-pənê ṯəhôm* "on the surface of the deep".

To build a verbless clause, work in three steps.

1. Decide what you are talking about. That is the subject: "the word".
2. Decide what you say about it. That is the predicate: "good".
3. Put them side by side. The order is free, but it is not meaningless (below).

So "the word is good" is *ṭôḇ haddāḇār*, as 1 Kings 2:38 has it. The *ha-* is the word "the", taught in the next lesson.

**Order.** Gesenius calls subject then predicate the natural order for describing a state (§141l). But predicate then subject is very common, and it is the rule when the predicate carries the weight of the sentence (§141l, §141n). An adjective predicate in particular often comes first: *ṭôḇ haddāḇār*, "good is the word". As a working rule: the first word is the one the speaker wants you to hear. English shows the same effect with stress: "the word is GOOD".

**Definite or not.** A predicate adjective never takes "the" (Gesenius §126i). The subject usually does, because you normally describe something already known. Lesson 2.2 shows why that difference is how you tell a sentence from a phrase.

**No dummy words.** English says "it is good" and "there was darkness", with an empty *it* or *there* to fill the subject slot. Hebrew has no such slot to fill. If the subject is already clear, it is left out: *kî-ṭôḇ*, "that (it was) good" (Genesis 1:4).

## Where it applies

Use a verbless clause wherever English uses "is", "are", "was" or "were" to say what something is like, what it is, or where it is.

- **Classifying: X is a Y.** *šəmûʾēl nāḇîʾ*. The predicate noun has no article: it names a class the subject belongs to.
- **Identifying: X is the Y.** *YHWH hāʾĕlōhîm* (Joshua 22:34). Both nouns are definite, and the clause equates them. This is the one place a predicate takes the article, because it is not describing but pointing to a known person (Gesenius §126k).
- **Describing with an adjective.** *ṭôḇ haddāḇār*, *ʿôḏ hayyôm gāḏôl*. With an adjective, the order is free; first position gives weight.
- **Location.** *wəḥōšeḵ ʿal-pənê ṯəhôm*. The predicate is a phrase with a preposition. Lesson 2.5 adds "in", "to", "like" and "from".
- **Number.** *ʿam ʾeḥāḏ*. A number can be the predicate, or part of it.
- **Material.** A noun can say what something is made of: Gesenius quotes "the altar was wood" from Ezekiel 41:22 (§141b). English needs "wooden" or "of wood"; Hebrew is content with the noun.
- **Any time.** Present in talk (Genesis 29:7), past in narrative (Genesis 25:27, 1:2). A verbless clause can also refer to the future when the context is a promise. Genesis 18:10 is one, met in lesson 2.5.
- **With the subject left out.** When the subject is obvious, the predicate alone is the clause: *kî-ṭôḇ*, *hēn ʿam ʾeḥāḏ*.
- **Inside other sentences.** After *kî* "that, because" (Genesis 1:4, Joshua 22:34), and, in later lessons, in questions (2.8), after "not" (2.7), and in clauses beginning "who" or "which" (part 6).
- **With "I", "you", "he".** When the subject is a pronoun, the clause is still verbless: "you are the man". Lesson 2.4.
- **Background in a story.** A verbless clause joined with "and" often describes the scene while the story moves on: *wəyaʿăqōḇ ʾîš tām* sets Jacob against Esau. Gesenius notes that such a clause after "and" "always describes a state contemporaneous with the principal action" (§141e).

## Watch out

**Do not add a word for "is".** By analogy with *es*, *est* or "is", you may want a verb in the middle. *\*šəmûʾēl hāyâ nāḇîʾ* is not "Samuel is a prophet". *hāyâ* means "was, became, came to be": it adds time or change. For a plain present statement, say *šəmûʾēl nāḇîʾ*.

**Do not use *yēš* for "is".** Spanish *hay* and French *il y a* have a Hebrew counterpart, *yēš* "there is" (lesson 2.7). It says that something exists. It is not a copula. *\*šəmûʾēl yēš nāḇîʾ* is not Hebrew. Say *šəmûʾēl nāḇîʾ*.

**Do not put "the" on a predicate adjective.** In *ṭôḇ haddāḇār* only the subject has the article. If both have it, *haddāḇār haṭṭôḇ*, you no longer have a sentence but a phrase: "the good word" (part 3). *\*haṭṭôḇ haddāḇār* for "the word is good" is wrong for the same reason (Gesenius §126i).

**Do not add "it" or "there".** *Kî-ṭôḇ* is "that it was good"; the light is the understood subject. Do not hunt for a missing pronoun, and do not supply one when you build a clause yourself unless the subject would otherwise be unclear.

**Do not assume present time.** English translations insert "is" or "was" by guessing from context, and so must you. *wəyaʿăqōḇ ʾîš tām* is "Jacob was a steady man", not "is": the story is in the past. *Hēn ʿôḏ hayyôm gāḏôl* is "it is still day", not "was": Jacob is speaking at that moment.

**Do not read word order as English.** In English the subject comes first. In *ṭôḇ haddāḇār* the first word is the predicate. Find the subject by asking which word is the thing described, usually the definite one, not by its position.

**Do not use *ʿal* for "to".** *ʿal* means "on, over, above", and by extension "about, against". It is not the preposition of direction. "To" is a different word, taught in lesson 2.5.

**Two nouns are not always a sentence.** *ʿam ʾeḥāḏ* in Genesis 11:6 is the predicate "(they are) one people". In another context the same two words could be a phrase, "one people", inside a longer sentence. Context, and above all the article, decides. Lesson 2.2 gives the test.

## Connections

Arabic has the same sentence type, the "nominal sentence" (*jumla ismiyya*): *al-baytu kabīrun* "the house is big", with no verb and the predicate adjective without the article, exactly as in *ʿôḏ hayyôm gāḏôl*. Spanish and French cannot leave the verb out; their *ser*/*estar* and *être* have no Hebrew counterpart in this clause type.

## Exercises

1. Translate *qāḏôš YHWH* and say which word is the predicate.
2. Build "Samuel is a priest" in Hebrew, in transliteration.
3. Build "The LORD is righteous" with the adjective first.
4. Translate *hēn ʿam ʾeḥāḏ* and explain why there is no subject word.
5. *Wəyaʿăqōḇ ʾîš tām* comes from a story. Translate it and explain how you chose the tense.
6. A student writes *\*šəmûʾēl yēš kōhēn* for "Samuel is a priest". Correct it and explain.
7. In *kî YHWH hāʾĕlōhîm*, why does the predicate have the article, when *ṭôḇ* in *ṭôḇ haddāḇār* has none?
8. Build "David is a servant" and "the servant is David", and say how the two differ in meaning.

## Worked answers

1. "The LORD is holy." *Qāḏôš* is the predicate: it is the description, and it has been put first for weight. *YHWH* is the subject, the one described.
2. *šəmûʾēl kōhēn*. The subject is Samuel; the predicate is the class he belongs to, a priest. No "is", no "a".
3. *ṣaddîq YHWH*. Predicate adjective first, subject second, nothing between them.
4. "Look, they are one people." The people building the tower have just been described, so the subject is understood. Hebrew leaves it out rather than filling it with a pronoun.
5. "But Jacob was a steady man." Nothing in the Hebrew marks time. The verse narrates the boyhood of Jacob and Esau, so English needs the past.
6. *šəmûʾēl kōhēn*. *Yēš* says that something exists ("there is"); it does not link a subject to a predicate. A verbless clause needs no linking word.
7. *Ṭôḇ* describes the word: it is a general quality the word has, so it takes no article (Gesenius §126i). *Hāʾĕlōhîm* does not describe the LORD: it identifies him with a known person, "the God", so it is definite like the subject.
8. *dāwiḏ ʿeḇeḏ* "David is a servant" classifies David. *Hāʿeḇeḏ dāwiḏ* "the servant is David" (with the article from lesson 2.2) tells you which person the servant is. In the first David is known and you learn what he is; in the second the servant is known and you learn who he is.
