## The idea

A noun is **definite** when speaker and hearer both know which one is meant: "the king", "the sun", "the sword I just mentioned". It is **indefinite** when it is any member of a class: "a king", "a sword".

Hebrew marks a definite noun with a prefix, the **article** *ha-*, glossed DEF. It is never a separate word; it is written as part of the noun (Gesenius §35a). It does not change for gender or number: *hammeleḵ* "the king", *hāʾiššâ* "the woman", *hehārîm* "the mountains" all have the same article. There is no word for "a". An indefinite noun is the bare noun: *meleḵ* "a king" (lesson 2.1).

The article has a striking shape. It is *ha-* plus a doubling of the next consonant: *meleḵ* → *ham-meleḵ*, *yôm* → *hay-yôm*. In the gloss the doubled letter stays with the noun, because it is the noun's own first consonant: *ha=mmeleḵ*.

Why the doubling? Gesenius reports two views (§35l). The older one took the article to be *hal-*, like Arabic *al-*, with the *l* assimilated to the next consonant, as Arabic does in *aš-šams* "the sun". The view he prefers, following Barth, connects it with an old Semitic pointing word *hā* "this" (compare Arabic *hāḏā* "this"). On that view the doubling comes from the close joining of *ha* to its noun, exactly as after the *wa-* of the narrative verb (*way-yōʾmer*). Gesenius also notes that the article "is by nature a kind of demonstrative pronoun" (§35a), and traces of that meaning survive: *hayyôm* is "this day, today" (§126b).

## How it is built

Start from the noun, look at its first consonant, and follow these steps (Gesenius §35).

1. **An ordinary consonant** (not a guttural or *r*): write *ha-* with pataḥ and double the consonant. *ha=mmeleḵ*, *ha=yyôm*, *ha=ššāmayim*, *ha=ddereḵ*. A doubled b g d k p t is always hard: *ha=bbayiṯ*.

2. **A guttural or *r*.** These letters cannot be doubled (lesson 1.2). So the doubling is lost, and something happens to the vowel of the article instead.

   - Before **ʾ** and **r**, the doubling is dropped entirely, and the article's vowel lengthens to qameṣ: *hā=ʾîš*, *hā=ʾôr*, *hā=rōʾš* (§35c). This is called compensatory lengthening: the vowel grows to make up for the lost consonant.
   - Before **h** and **ḥ**, the pataḥ normally stays, as if the letter were doubled: *ha=ḥōšeḵ*. Grammarians call this **virtual doubling**: the syllable behaves as if closed by a double consonant that is not pronounced (§35e–f, §22c). Khan describes the vowel in such words as spreading to fill the lost length (§I.2.3.2, on *hahûʾ* "that").
   - Before **ʿ**, the article is usually *hā-*: *hā=ʿîr*, *hā=ʿeḇeḏ* (§35g).

3. **A guttural that itself has qameṣ.** Here the stress decides (§35h–i):
   - before a *stressed* *hā* or *ʿā*, the article is *hā-*: *hā=ʿām*, *hā=hār*;
   - before an *unstressed* *hā* or *ʿā*, it is *he-*: *he=hārîm* "the mountains", *he=ʿārîm* "the cities";
   - before *ḥā*, always *he-*: *he=ḥāḵām* "the wise man".

4. Nothing else changes. Gender and number make no difference (§35k).

To make "the sword" from *ḥereḇ*: the first letter is ḥ, a guttural; its vowel is segol, not qameṣ; ḥ takes virtual doubling, so the article stays *ha-*: *haḥereḇ*.

### Why "the land" is always hāʾāreṣ

The word for land is *ʾereṣ*. With the article you might expect *\*hāʾereṣ*: alef, so *hā-*, then the noun unchanged. The text never has that. With the article the noun is always *hāʾāreṣ*, 934 times in the Leningrad Codex, with qameṣ in the first syllable as well.

Gesenius lists this as a fact about five words: *ʾereṣ* "land", *har* "mountain", *ḥaḡ* "festival", *ʿam* "people" and *par* "bull" "always appear after the article with a long vowel (as in pause)": *hāʾāreṣ*, *hāhār*, *heḥāḡ*, *hāʿām*, *happār* (§35o). BDB says the same of *par*: "always *happār*".

"As in pause" is the key. Lesson 1.8 showed that a word at a major break takes its **pausal form**, often with a longer vowel, and Gesenius gives exactly this pair: *ʾéreṣ*, in pause *ʾā́reṣ* (§29k). He explains there that the segol of such nouns stands for an earlier *a* (his "ground-form" for this type is a single syllable with *a*, like *qašr* behind *qešer*), and that in pause the *a* returns as *ā*. So *hāʾāreṣ* is the article plus the noun in its pausal shape. Compare Arabic *ʾarḍ*, with its *a*.

What the sources consulted do not give is *why* the article calls up the pausal shape in these five words and not in others. Gesenius records it without a reason. Do not invent one. Learn the five as a list; *hāʾāreṣ* and *hāʿām* are among the commonest words in the Bible.

## Where it applies

- **Something already mentioned.** *ḥāreḇ*, then *haḥereḇ* (1 Kings 3:24); *ʾôr* in Genesis 1:3, then *hāʾôr* in 1:4 (Gesenius §126d).
- **Something unique, or unique in the speaker's world.** *haššemeš* "the sun", *hāʾāreṣ* "the earth", *haššāmayim* "the heavens"; *hammeleḵ* "the king" when there is one king in the story (§126d).
- **A title that identifies a known person.** *hammeleḵ šəlōmōh* "King Solomon" (§126d): the title takes the article, the name does not.
- **A word narrowed to one famous instance.** *hāʾĕlōhîm* "the (one true) God", *hāʾāḏām* "the man, Adam" (§126e). Compare *YHWH hāʾĕlōhîm*, lesson 2.1.
- **"This" in time phrases.** *hayyôm* "today", *hallaylâ* "tonight", *happaʿam* "this time" (§126b).
- **Calling someone.** *hammeleḵ* "O king!" (1 Samuel 24:9; also *hôšiʿâ hammeleḵ* "Help, O king!", 2 Samuel 14:4). Hebrew treats the person addressed as definite (§126e).
- **An adjective used as a noun.** *heḥāḵām* "the wise man", *hārāšāʿ* "the wicked one".
- **The subject of a verbless clause**, which is usually something known: *kî-ʿaz hāʿām*, *ṭôḇâ hāʾāreṣ*.
- **A whole class.** Hebrew often puts the article on a noun meaning "the kind of thing", where English uses a plural or no article. Gesenius treats this use at §126l; you will meet it in reading.
- **After the prefixed prepositions,** where the *h* drops out and its vowel moves forward: *lāʾôr* "to the light" (Genesis 1:5). Lesson 2.5.
- **Later in the guide:** the article on adjectives that follow a definite noun ("the good word", part 3), the article on the last noun only of a chain ("the word of the king", part 3), and a rare use of the article as "who, which" (part 6).

## Watch out

**No doubling before gutturals and r.** By analogy with *hammeleḵ* you might write *\*haʾʾîš* or *\*harrōʾš*. These letters cannot be doubled. Write *hāʾîš*, *hārōʾš*.

**The land is never *\*hāʾereṣ*.** The article rule alone predicts it, but the five words of Gesenius §35o take their pausal vowel: *hāʾāreṣ*, *hāhār*, *hāʿām*, *heḥāḡ*, *happār*. Without the article they keep their normal shape: *ʾereṣ*, *har*, *ʿam*.

***hā-* and *he-* before the same letter.** *hāhār* but *hehārîm*. It is not a different article; the stress has moved. Check where the stress falls before you choose.

**No article on names.** English says "the Jordan" and Spanish "el Perú", but Hebrew personal names never take the article: *\*haddāwiḏ*, *\*haššəmûʾēl* are wrong. Say *dāwiḏ*, *šəmûʾēl*. A few place names and gentilic words do carry it, and these are learned one by one.

**No article on a noun with "my", "your", "his".** A suffix makes the noun definite already: *ʾăḏōnî* "my lord", not *\*hāʾăḏōnî*. Part 3 explains the suffixes.

**No article on a predicate adjective.** *ṭôḇâ hāʾāreṣ* is a sentence. Put the article on the adjective too and you have a phrase, "the good land" (lesson 2.1, Gesenius §126i).

**The article is not a separate word.** Writing it apart, *\*ha meleḵ*, breaks the word. It is one word with its noun, and the doubling shows why.

**Do not confuse it with other *h-* prefixes.** A *hă-* with ḥaṭep̄ pataḥ before a question is the question marker (lesson 2.8). The *ha-* that opens some verb forms belongs to the verb (part 4). The article has pataḥ (or qameṣ, or segol) and a doubled or "compensated" consonant after it.

**Doubling can vanish before shewa.** After the article, *y*, *l* and *m* with shewa often lose their doubling: *hayʾōr* "the Nile", *halwiyyim* "the Levites" (Gesenius §35b; Khan §I.2.5.8.1 describes the variation). The article is still there; only the dot is missing.

## Connections

Arabic *al-* behaves like Hebrew *ha-* before the "sun letters": *aš-šams* "the sun" doubles the *š* just as *haššemeš* does. The likeness is real, but Gesenius doubts that the two articles have the same origin (§35l). Spanish *el*, *la* and French *le*, *la* came from Latin demonstratives (*ille*, *illa*), which matches the demonstrative origin Gesenius proposes for *ha-*.

## Exercises

1. Add the article to *ʿîr*, *yām* and *ʾōhel*, and explain each form.
2. Add the article to *ḥereḇ* and to *ʾiššâ*.
3. Why is "the people" *hāʿām*, but "the cities" *heʿārîm*?
4. A student writes *\*hāʾereṣ* for "the land". What is wrong, and what is the explanation?
5. Translate *kî-ʿaz hāʿām*, and say which word is the predicate and how you know.
6. Build "The king is wise" as a verbless clause.
7. Build "the wise king", and say how it differs from your answer to 6.
8. Translate *hayyôm*, and say what it shows about the article's origin.

## Worked answers

1. *hāʿîr*: ʿayin cannot double, and before ʿ the article usually lengthens to *hā-*. *hayyām*: an ordinary consonant, so *ha-* with doubled *y*. *hāʾōhel*: alef cannot double, so *hā-* with no doubling.
2. *haḥereḇ*: ḥet has virtual doubling, so *ha-* stays; the ḥ has segol, not qameṣ, so the *he-* rule does not apply. *hāʾiššâ*: alef, so *hā-*.
3. In *ʿām* the ʿā is stressed, so the article is *hā-* (and the noun takes its pausal ā). In *ʿārîm* the stress is on *-rîm*, so the ʿā is unstressed and the article is *he-* (Gesenius §35h).
4. The noun must take its pausal vowel after the article: *hāʾāreṣ*. Gesenius lists five words that do this (§35o). The segol of *ʾereṣ* goes back to an *a*, which comes back as *ā* in pause (§29k). The sources do not say why the article triggers the pausal shape.
5. "But the people are strong." *ʿaz* is the predicate: it is the description, it has no article, and it stands first for weight. *hāʿām* is the subject: definite, the thing described.
6. *ḥāḵām hammeleḵ*, or *hammeleḵ ḥāḵām*. Only the subject has the article.
7. *hammeleḵ heḥāḵām* "the wise king": both words are definite, so it is a phrase, not a sentence. In 6 the adjective has no article, and that makes it the predicate.
8. "Today." The article keeps its old sense of "this": "this day". Gesenius takes such phrases as evidence that the article was originally a demonstrative (§126a–b).
