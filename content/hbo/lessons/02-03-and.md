## The idea

A **conjunction** is a word that joins words or clauses: "and", "but", "or". Hebrew's main conjunction is a single consonant, *w*, written as a prefix on the next word, like the article. You have already met it in Genesis 1: *wəhāʾāreṣ* "and the earth", *tōhû wāḇōhû* "formless and empty". It is glossed by the plain English word *and*, joined with `=`: *wə=hā=ʾāreṣ* `and=DEF=earth`.

Two facts shape how you read it.

First, *wə-* does far more work than English "and". Hebrew prose joins clause to clause with it where English would start a new sentence, or use "but", "so", "then", "while". English translators drop many of them. The meaning comes from the clauses it joins, not from the prefix.

Second, its pronunciation changes with the word it is attached to. That is why you see *wə-*, *û-*, *wî-*, *wa-*, *we-* and *wā-*. These are one word. The changes follow from two things you already know: Hebrew does not allow a syllable to begin with two shewas, and a vowel shifts to suit what comes next.

Gesenius says the prefix was originally *wa-*, "as still before ḥaṭeph pathaḥ and, with a following dagesh forte, in waw consecutive of the imperfect" (§104d). That older *wa-* survives in the narrative verb *way-yōʾmer*, which is a separate construction (part 4). In ordinary use the short *a* has weakened to shewa, *wə-*, just as the *ba-* of the preposition "in" weakened to *bə-* (Gesenius §102d).

## How it is built

Look at the first sound of the word you are joining, and apply the first rule that fits (Gesenius §104d–g).

1. **Before a consonant with shewa** (other than *yə*), *wə-* becomes **û-**: *û=šəp̄āḥōṯ* "and maidservants". Two vocal shewas cannot begin a word (lesson 1.5). So the *w* gives up its shewa and is pronounced as the vowel *u*, written with a shureq dot (§104e). This is one of the few places where a Hebrew word begins with a vowel (lesson 1.6).

2. **Before b, m, p**, the lip consonants, it is also **û-**: *û=ḇāqār* "and cattle", *û=meleḵ* "and a king of". Gesenius calls them "the cognate labials" (§104e): *w* is made with the lips too, and the sequence *wə* + lip sound was avoided. Note the soft *ḇ* in *ûḇāqār*: the *û* is a vowel, so a begadkefat letter after it is soft.

3. **Before yə**, the two merge into **wî-**: *wə* + *yəhûḏâ* → *wîhûḏâ* "and Judah" (§104f, §24b). The yod's shewa is lost and the yod becomes a vowel letter for *î*.

4. **Before a guttural with a ḥaṭep̄ vowel**, it takes the full vowel of that ḥaṭep̄: *wa=ʿăḇāḏim*, *wa=ʾănî*, *we=ʾĕ-*. With *ʾĕlōhîm* "God" the two syllables contract: *wēʾlōhîm* (§102d). Before the divine name it is pointed *wa-*, because the name is read *ʾăḏōnāy*: *waYHWH* (§102m).

5. **Before a stressed syllable, at a pause**, it is often **wā-**: *tōhû wāḇōhû*, *yôm wālaylâ*. Gesenius explains this as rhythm: it happens chiefly in pairs of related words, where the *wā-* stands right before the stressed syllable and the word carries a dividing accent (§104g). The short words *zeh*, *ʾēṯ*, *gam*, *lōʾ* keep *wə-* even so (§104g).

6. **Everywhere else**, **wə-**: *wə=ḵesep̄*, *wə=qayin*, *wə=hāʾāreṣ*.

The prefix never takes the place of the article. *wə* + *hāʾāreṣ* stays *wəhāʾāreṣ*, with the *h* kept (Gesenius §35n). The prepositions of lesson 2.5 behave differently, and that contrast is worth remembering.

To join "silver and gold": *kesep̄* + *wə* + *zāhāḇ*. *Z* has a full vowel and is not a lip consonant, so *wə-*: *kesep̄ wəzāhāḇ*. To join "gold and silver": *wə* + *kesep̄*. After the vowel *ə* the *k* is soft: *zāhāḇ wəḵesep̄*.

## Where it applies

- **Joining words:** *kesep̄ wəzāhāḇ*. In a list, Hebrew may put *wə-* before every item after the first (Genesis 35:23), or only before the last (Genesis 13:2). English normally keeps only the last.
- **Pairs that belong together**, usually with *wā-*: *tōhû wāḇōhû*, *yôm wālaylâ*, *ʿāp̄ār wāʾēp̄er*. A list of pairs uses *wə-* between them (Genesis 8:22).
- **Joining clauses, "and":** *waYHWH hāyâ ʿimmô*.
- **Contrast, "but":** *wəqayin hāyâ ʿōḇēḏ ʾăḏāmâ* (Genesis 4:2), *wəyaʿăqōḇ ʾîš tām* (Genesis 25:27, lesson 2.1). The contrast comes from putting a new subject first after *wə-*.
- **Background, "now", "while", "although":** *wə-* + a verbless clause describes a state that holds while the story goes on, or one that clashes with it (Gesenius §141e): *wəʾaḇrām kāḇēḏ məʾōḏ* "now Abram was very rich", *wəʾānōḵî ʿāp̄ār wāʾēp̄er* "though I am dust and ashes".
- **Opening a book or a section:** *wə-* or the narrative *way-* can stand at the very start of a text, as in Genesis 1:2 or the first word of Leviticus and Numbers, linking it to what went before. Translators often leave it out.
- **In the verb chain:** the narrative past *way-yōʾmer* and the future-continuing *wə-qatal* (part 4) are built on the same prefix, but they are separate forms with their own meanings. When *w* is followed by a verb, check which one it is.

## Watch out

**One word, six spellings.** Do not take *û-*, *wî-*, *wa-* or *wā-* for different words. They are all "and". Check the first sound of the next word: it tells you which shape to expect.

**Not *\*wəḇāqār*.** By analogy with *wəḵesep̄* you might write *wə-* everywhere. Before *b*, *m*, *p* and before shewa, the form is *û-*: *ûḇāqār*, *ûmeleḵ*, *ûšəp̄āḥōṯ*.

**Not *\*wəyəhûḏâ*.** Two shewas cannot start a word, and with *yə* the prefix merges: *wîhûḏâ*.

**Not *\*wəʾănî*.** Before a ḥaṭep̄ vowel, take its full vowel: *waʾănî*, *waʿăḇāḏim*.

**Do not drop the article's *h* after *wə-*.** By analogy with *ba-* "in the" (lesson 2.5), you might write *\*wāʾāreṣ* for "and the land". The conjunction does not swallow the article: *wəhāʾāreṣ* (Gesenius §35n).

**Do not take *wa-* before YHWH for the narrative verb.** *waYHWH* is the conjunction, pointed for the reading *waʾḏōnāy*. The narrative *way-* is followed by a doubled consonant and a verb.

**Do not translate every *wə-* as "and".** Fifteen "and"s in a row is not English, and often not the meaning. Read *wə-* + new subject as "but" or "now"; read *wə-* + verbless clause as "while" or "although" where the sense needs it; leave it out where English starts a new sentence.

**Do not use *wə-* for "or".** Spanish *y* and *o* map to two Hebrew words: *wə-* "and" and *ʾô* "or". *\*kesep̄ wəzāhāḇ* for "silver or gold" says "silver and gold". Say *kesep̄ ʾô zāhāḇ*.

**Do not write it as a separate word.** Like the article, it is a prefix: *\*wə ʾāreṣ* is wrong.

## Connections

Arabic has the same single-letter conjunction, *wa-*, written joined to the next word: *wa-l-arḍu* "and the earth". Its vowel is the older *a* that Gesenius posits for Hebrew (§104d). Arabic also has a second prefix, *fa-* "and so", which Hebrew lacks; Hebrew makes *wə-* do both jobs.

## Exercises

1. Join *ṣōʾn* and *bāqār* with "and", and explain the form.
2. Join *zāhāḇ* and *kesep̄* ("gold and silver"), and say how the *k* is pronounced.
3. Write "and Judah" and "and Levi", and explain the difference.
4. Write "and I" using *ʾănî*.
5. Why does Genesis 1:2 have *wəhāʾāreṣ*, while "in the land" is *bāʾāreṣ*?
6. Translate *wəʾaḇrām kāḇēḏ məʾōḏ* and explain how "and" is best rendered.
7. Find the error: *\*yôm wəlaylâ* at the end of a clause, in a pair. What did the Masoretes write in Genesis 8:22, and why?
8. Translate *wəqayin hāyâ ʿōḇēḏ ʾăḏāmâ* after a clause about Abel, and explain the choice of conjunction in English.

## Worked answers

1. *ṣōʾn ûḇāqār*. *B* is a lip consonant, so *wə-* becomes *û-* (Gesenius §104e). After the vowel *û* the *b* is soft: *ḇ*.
2. *zāhāḇ wəḵesep̄*. *K* is not a lip consonant and has a full vowel, so the prefix is *wə-*. After the vowel *ə* the begadkefat *k* is soft: *ḵ*.
3. *wîhûḏâ*: *yəhûḏâ* begins with *yə*, and *wə* + *yə* merges into *wî* (§104f). *wəlēwî*: *l* has a full vowel, so plain *wə-*.
4. *waʾănî*. The alef has ḥaṭep̄ pataḥ, so the prefix takes its full vowel, *a* (§104d).
5. The conjunction never absorbs the article: *wə* + *hāʾāreṣ* (§35n). The prepositions do: *bə* + *hāʾāreṣ* → *bāʾāreṣ*, with the *h* lost and its vowel moved forward (lesson 2.5).
6. "Now Abram was very rich." It is a verbless clause after *wə-* that gives a background fact in the middle of a story, so "now" fits better than "and" (Gesenius §141e).
7. *wəyôm wālaylâ*. In a pair, right before the stressed syllable of *láylâ* and under a dividing accent, the prefix lengthens to *wā-* (§104g). The *wə-* before *yôm* joins this pair to the one before.
8. "But Cain worked the soil." *Wə-* followed by a new subject sets him against Abel. Hebrew has no separate word for "but" here; the contrast is in the order.
