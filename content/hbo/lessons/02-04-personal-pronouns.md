## The idea

A **pronoun** is a word that stands for a person or thing without naming it: "I", "you", "she". The pronouns in this lesson are the **independent** ones, written as separate words. Hebrew also has short pronoun endings, attached to nouns, prepositions and verbs ("my", "to him", "he guarded"); those come in parts 3 and 4.

Three ideas organise them, and the gloss labels them.

- **Person.** First person (1) is the speaker, "I, we". Second person (2) is the one spoken to, "you". Third person (3) is anyone or anything else, "he, she, it, they".
- **Number.** Singular (SG) is one; plural (PL) is more than one.
- **Gender.** Masculine (M) and feminine (F). Hebrew marks gender not only in "he" and "she" but also in "you" and "they": a man is addressed *ʾattâ*, a woman *ʾatt*. Arabic does the same, *ʾanta* and *ʾanti*. Only the first person has one form for both, because, as Gesenius puts it, the person speaking "needs no further indication of gender" (§32e). A mixed group takes the masculine.

So *ʾattâ* is glossed `2SG.M`, *hîʾ* `3SG.F`, *ʾănaḥnû* `1PL`.

What are these words for? In a verbal clause, the verb already says who acts (lesson 2.6), and a pronoun is added only for emphasis. Gesenius says the independent pronouns serve "almost exclusively to emphasize the nominative-subject" (§32b). But a verbless clause has no verb to carry the person. There, the pronoun is the only way to say who the subject is: "you are the man" needs *ʾattâ*. That is why the pronouns come now.

## How it is built

The forms come down from old Semitic words that Arabic still shows clearly (Gesenius §32f): Hebrew *ʾattâ* "you" is contracted from *ʾantā*, as Arabic *ʾanta* keeps the *n*. The doubled *t* is the trace of the lost *n*.

To build a verbless clause with a pronoun subject, choose the pronoun by person, number and gender, then add the predicate.

1. Who is the subject? You, speaking to one man: second person, singular, masculine: *ʾattâ*.
2. What do you say about him? He is the man: *hāʾîš*, definite, because it identifies.
3. Put them side by side: *ʾattâ hāʾîš* (2 Samuel 12:7).

**Order.** With a pronoun subject, the predicate very often comes first: *ʿāp̄ār ʾattâ* "dust you are" (Genesis 3:19), *ʾăḥōṯî hîʾ* "she is my sister". Gesenius explains that a pronoun names someone "assumed to be generally known", and so "does not excite the same interest as that which is stated about him" (§141n). The new information goes first. When the pronoun itself is the point, "*you* are the man", it comes first.

**Pause.** Several pronouns change at a pause (lesson 1.8; Gesenius §32b): *ʾănî* → *ʾā́nî*, *ʾattâ* → *ʾā́ttâ*, *ʾănaḥnû* → *ʾănā́ḥnû*, *ʾatt* → *ʾā́tt*. They are the same words.

**Two words for "I".** *ʾănî* (587 times, plus 106 in pause) and *ʾānōḵî* (293). They mean the same. Gesenius notes that *ʾānōḵî* is the rarer, that it is shared with Phoenician and Moabite, and that the pronoun endings are built from *ʾănî* (§32c). Both appear in the same stories, even in the same mouth.

**"She" in the Torah.** In the five books of Moses the consonants usually write הוא for "she" as well as "he". The Masoretes point it הִוא, with ḥireq, to tell you to read *hîʾ* (lesson 1.9). Gesenius takes this to be a spelling habit of that text, not an old genderless pronoun (§32l).

**The third person as a link.** A third-person pronoun can stand between a subject and its predicate, picking the subject up again: *YHWH hûʾ hāʾĕlōhîm* "the LORD, he is God" (1 Kings 18:39). Gesenius describes it as "expressly resuming and therefore strengthening the subject" (§141g–h). It looks like a copula, and English translates it "is", but it is still a pronoun, agreeing with the subject in gender and number.

## Where it applies

- **Subject of a verbless clause**, the main use at this stage: *ʾănî YHWH*, *ʾattâ hāʾîš*, *kēnîm ʾănaḥnû*. Without a pronoun the person would be unclear.
- **Predicate first, pronoun second**, when the description is the news: *ʿāp̄ār ʾattâ*, *məraggəlîm ʾattem*, *ʾăḥōṯî hîʾ* (Gesenius §141l, n).
- **Pronoun first**, when the person is the point: *ʾattâ hāʾîš*, "*you* are the man".
- **"It" and "they" for things.** The third person covers things as well as people (Gesenius §32o), with the gender of the noun: a feminine noun such as *ʾereṣ* or *ʿîr* is referred to as *hîʾ*.
- **Resuming a subject.** *YHWH hûʾ hāʾĕlōhîm*. With "I" and "you" this is rare and emphatic: *ʾattâ-hûʾ hāʾĕlōhîm* "you are God" (2 Samuel 7:28; Gesenius §141h).
- **Emphasis with a verb.** "*I* did not know" sets the speaker against someone else. The verb already has the person, so the pronoun adds contrast (Gesenius §32b). You will see this from lesson 2.6.
- **After "and"**: *waʾănî wəʿammî hārəšāʿîm* "and I and my people are in the wrong" (Exodus 9:27, lesson 2.3).
- **Later in the guide:** *hûʾ* and *hîʾ* with the article mean "that": *hayyôm hahûʾ* "that day" (part 3). Their short forms are the pronoun endings (part 3).

## Watch out

**Do not leave out the pronoun in a verbless clause.** Spanish and Korean drop subject pronouns freely, and so does Hebrew with verbs. But *\*hāʾîš* alone cannot mean "you are the man". With no verb, the pronoun is the subject: *ʾattâ hāʾîš*.

**Do not add a pronoun to every verb.** The opposite mistake, by analogy with English or French. Once you meet verbs (lesson 2.6), *bārāʾ* is already "he created". *Hûʾ bārāʾ* means "*he* created", with contrast.

**Do not take *hûʾ* for "is".** In *YHWH hûʾ hāʾĕlōhîm* it resumes a third-person subject. *\*ʾănî hûʾ nāḇîʾ* for "I am a prophet" does not work: *hûʾ* is third person. Say *ʾănî nāḇîʾ*.

**Do not use these pronouns as objects.** English "him" and French *le* have no independent counterpart here. *Hûʾ* is only a subject (or a predicate). "Him" as an object is an ending on the object marker, *ʾōṯô* (lesson 2.9 and part 3).

**Choose "you" by gender.** Addressing a woman, use *ʾatt*, not *ʾattâ*: Abram to Sarai says *ʾātt* (Genesis 12:11). Addressing a mixed group, use *ʾattem*.

**No polite plural "you".** French *vous* and Spanish *usted* do not exist. A subject speaks to a king as *ʾattâ*; Nathan says *ʾattâ hāʾîš* to David. Courtesy is shown by other means, such as calling oneself "your servant" (part 7). *\*ʾattem* to one person is wrong.

**Do not confuse *hēnnâ* "they (f.)" with *hēnnâ* "here, hither".** In the Leningrad Codex 44 of its 70 occurrences are the adverb. Context decides.

**The Torah's הִוא is "she".** Read it *hîʾ*, as the pointing says, not *hûʾ*.

**Do not mistake *ʾănî* and *ʾānōḵî* for formal and informal.** No such difference is known; Gesenius treats them as two forms of one word (§32c).

## Connections

Arabic pronouns are close cousins: *ʾanā*, *ʾanta*, *ʾanti*, *huwa*, *hiya*, *naḥnu*, *ʾantum*, *hum*. Gesenius compares several of them directly (§32c, f, k). Korean and Japanese, like Hebrew, leave the subject pronoun out when a verb makes it clear, and use it for contrast; but unlike Hebrew they can also omit it in a sentence of the type "X is Y".

## Exercises

1. Translate *ʾănî YHWH* and *ʾattâ hāʾîš*, and say why the second has the article on its predicate.
2. Build "We are servants" using *ʿeḇeḏ* in the plural *ʿăḇāḏîm*, with the predicate first.
3. Build "You are a woman", speaking to a woman.
4. Gloss *hēmmâ* and *hîʾ*, and give the label for each.
5. Why does Genesis 3:19 put *ʿāp̄ār* before *ʾattâ*?
6. A student writes *\*ʾănî hûʾ kōhēn* for "I am a priest". Correct it.
7. In Genesis 20:2 the consonants spell הוא. Why is the word read *hîʾ*?
8. Translate *YHWH hûʾ hāʾĕlōhîm*, and explain what *hûʾ* does.
9. Build "He is holy" with the predicate first.

## Worked answers

1. "I am the LORD"; "You are the man". The predicate *hāʾîš* identifies David with the man in Nathan's story, a known person, so it is definite (Gesenius §126k).
2. *ʿăḇāḏîm ʾănaḥnû*. First person plural, *ʾănaḥnû*; the description, being the news, comes first.
3. *ʾiššâ ʾatt*. The person addressed is a woman, so second person singular feminine, *ʾatt*. The predicate is indefinite, "a woman".
4. *hēmmâ*: `3PL.M` "they" (masculine). *hîʾ*: `3SG.F` "she, it".
5. With a pronoun subject the new information usually comes first: the pronoun names someone already known, the predicate says something new (Gesenius §141l, n).
6. *ʾănî kōhēn* (or *kōhēn ʾănî*). *Hûʾ* is third person and cannot link "I" to anything; a verbless clause needs no link.
7. In the Torah the consonantal text normally writes הוא for both genders. The Masoretes pointed it with ḥireq to show the reading *hîʾ* (a perpetual qere, lesson 1.9; Gesenius §32l).
8. "The LORD is God" (or "the LORD, he is God"). *Hûʾ* repeats the subject and strengthens it; it agrees with *YHWH* as third person singular masculine (Gesenius §141h).
9. *qāḏôš hûʾ*. Predicate adjective first, no article, then the pronoun.
