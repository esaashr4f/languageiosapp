## The idea

So far every sentence has been a description. Now something happens. "God created", "the waters rose", "men came here". For that you need a verb.

Hebrew verbs are built from a root and a pattern (lesson 0.2). The root *š-m-r* carries the idea "guard, keep"; a pattern of vowels and endings around it says who did it and how the event is viewed. This lesson teaches one pattern, the **qatal**, and only its third person: "he", "she", "they".

The name *qatal* is a label taken from a model verb, *q-ṭ-l*; it means nothing more. Older books call the form "the perfect". This guide uses the form name, because what the form means is debated and part 4 sets out the views. For now one fact is enough. Gesenius says the qatal presents an event "from the point of view of completion" (§106a). In practice, in a story or a report, it is translated by the English past: "he guarded", "he has guarded", "he had guarded".

The qatal marks person with **endings** on the stem. Gesenius explains that the endings are "certain forms of the personal pronoun" attached to a stem that is "in meaning if not in form" a verbal adjective (§44a). The third person singular masculine has no ending at all: the bare stem *šāmar* already means "he guarded", the way *ṣaddîq* alone can mean "(he is) righteous". That bare form is also the form under which dictionaries and this guide list a verb.

## How it is built

The strong verb, one whose three root letters are all ordinary consonants, has the stem *qāṭal*: qameṣ after the first root letter, pataḥ after the second. With *š-m-r*:

- **3SG.M "he"**: the bare stem, *šāmar* "he guarded".
- **3SG.F "she"**: add *-â*: *šāmərâ* "she guarded".
- **3PL "they"**: add *-û*: *šāmərû* "they guarded". One form for both genders.

Why does the pataḥ disappear in *šāmərâ* and *šāmərû*? The ending begins with a vowel, so the *r* moves into the next syllable: *šā-ma-râ*. The stress moves onto the ending, and the pataḥ, now in an open syllable just before the stress, is reduced to shewa (Gesenius §44b; lesson 1.5 on reduction). At a pause the old vowel comes back, lengthened under the stress: *šāmā́rû* (§44b).

The feminine *-â* was originally *-at*, Gesenius notes, like the feminine ending of nouns (§44a; compare Arabic *katabat* "she wrote"). The plural *-û* matches the plural endings elsewhere in Semitic.

In the gloss, the stem takes the meaning and QTL, and the ending is its own piece: *šāmar* `guard.QTL.3SG.M`, *šāmər-â* `guard.QTL-3SG.F`, *šāmər-û* `guard.QTL-3PL`.

To build "God heard":

1. The root is *š-m-ʿ* "hear"; the event is complete, so qatal.
2. The subject is God, third person singular masculine: no ending, *šāmaʿ*.
3. The verb normally comes first, the subject after: *šāmaʿ ʾĕlōhîm*.

### Word order

In Hebrew prose the verb usually stands before its subject: *bārāʾ ʾĕlōhîm*, *kōh ʾāmar YHWH*. This is an observation about the texts. Whether verb-first is the "basic" order of the language or a result of how stories are told is debated among grammarians; the guide takes no side.

But the qatal has a particular place in a story. The main line of a narrative is carried by another form, the *wayyiqtol* ("and he did", part 4). Gesenius notes that a qatal in narrative stands at the head of a narrative or of an independent sentence, and that after "and" it appears "only when the verb is separated from the copulative *wə* by one or more words" (§106e). So when you see *wə* + subject + qatal, as in *wəhāʾāreṣ hāyəṯâ* or *wəlāḇān hālaḵ*, the writer has stepped out of the chain to give background, contrast or an earlier event: "now the earth was", "Laban had gone". When the qatal begins its clause, it reports the event plainly.

### Weak verbs

Some roots contain a weak letter, and their forms differ: *bāʾ* "he came" (root *b-w-ʾ*), *bāʾû* "they came"; *hāyâ* "he was", *hāyəṯâ* "she was"; *ʿāśâ* "he did", *ʿāśû* "they did"; *mēṯ* "he died". Learn these as words for now. Why they look like this is part 4.

## Where it applies

- **A past event, reported plainly:** *bārāʾ ʾĕlōhîm*; *kî-šāmaʿ ʾĕlōhîm* "for God has heard".
- **A past event as background, after a fronted subject:** *wəhāʾāreṣ hāyəṯâ*, *wəhammayim gāḇərû* (Gesenius §106e).
- **An earlier event, "had done":** *wərāḥēl lāqəḥâ*, *wəlāḇān hālaḵ* (Gesenius §106f). The context, not the form, tells you it was earlier.
- **"Has done", with results now:** *ʾănāšîm bāʾû hēnnâ* "men have come here (and are here)" (Gesenius §106g).
- **Words just spoken:** *kōh ʾāmar YHWH* "thus says the LORD". English uses the present for what Hebrew views as said.
- **States:** with verbs that name a state, such as *zāqēn* "be old" or *yāḏaʿ* "know", the qatal often means the state now holds: "he is old", "he knows". Part 4 covers these.
- **After *kî* and other linking words** the qatal normally comes first in its clause: *kî-šāmaʿ ʾĕlōhîm*.
- **With "not"** (lesson 2.7) and **in questions** (lesson 2.8).
- **Later:** the qatal in first and second person ("I did", "you did", part 4), and the *wə* + qatal of instructions and the future (part 4), which is a different construction.

## Watch out

**Do not add a pronoun.** By analogy with English "he guarded", you might write *\*hûʾ šāmar*. The form already says "he". *Hûʾ šāmar* means "*he* guarded", in contrast to someone else. For plain "he guarded", write *šāmar*.

**Do not add a feminine ending for a masculine plural.** The 3PL *-û* is used for both genders: *šāmərû* is "they guarded", whether the subjects are men or women.

**Do not keep the pataḥ before the ending.** *\*šāmarâ* and *\*šāmarû* are wrong outside pause. The vowel reduces: *šāmərâ*, *šāmərû*.

**Do not read *šāmərâ* as a noun.** The ending *-â* is also the feminine ending of nouns (*tôrâ*, *šānâ*). Context and the root pattern tell you which.

**Do not make every qatal a plain past.** Spanish *pretérito* and French *passé simple* do not cover "has come" or "had gone" or "thus says". The qatal views the event as complete; English needs the tense that fits the context.

**Do not translate *wə* + subject + qatal as "and then".** That is the job of the wayyiqtol. *Wəlāḇān hālaḵ* is not the next event; it is "now Laban had gone" (Gesenius §106e–f).

**Do not use qatal for a description.** "The day is big" is *hayyôm gāḏôl*, verbless (lesson 2.1). Adding a qatal of *hāyâ*, *\*hayyôm hāyâ gāḏôl*, turns it into "the day was big" or "had become big".

**Verb first means verb before the subject, not first in the sentence.** In *bərēʾšîṯ bārāʾ ʾĕlōhîm* and *kōh ʾāmar YHWH* a time word and "thus" come before the verb. That is normal: the subject still follows the verb.

## Connections

The Arabic suffix conjugation is the same system: *kataba* "he wrote", *katabat* "she wrote", *katabū* "they wrote", with the person in the ending and the bare stem for "he". The Hebrew root *k-t-b* is the same root. Spanish and French also put the person in the verb ending (*escribió*, *écrivit*), and drop the pronoun, so the Hebrew habit of *šāmar* without *hûʾ* will feel natural.

## Exercises

1. Give the qatal "she remembered" and "they remembered" from *zāḵar*, and explain the vowel change.
2. Build "Jacob heard" with the verb first.
3. Build "The king wrote".
4. Translate *kōh ʾāmar YHWH* and explain the English tense.
5. Gloss *šāmərû* piece by piece.
6. Translate *wərāḥēl lāqəḥâ* and say why the subject comes first.
7. A student writes *\*hûʾ lāqaḥ* for "he took" in a plain report. What does that mean, and what is the plain form?
8. Why is *gāḇərû* used for "the waters", not *gāḇar*?
9. *Wəhammeleḵ dāwiḏ zāqēn*: why is *zāqēn* best rendered "was old" rather than "grew old and then ..."?

## Worked answers

1. *zāḵərâ*, *zāḵərû*. The ending begins with a vowel, the stress moves to it, and the pataḥ of the second syllable, now in an open syllable just before the stress, reduces to shewa (Gesenius §44b).
2. *šāmaʿ yaʿăqōḇ*. Third person singular masculine, no ending; verb before subject.
3. *kāṯaḇ hammeleḵ*. "The king" is definite, *hammeleḵ*.
4. "Thus says the LORD." The words have been spoken, so Hebrew uses the qatal; English uses the present for a message being delivered.
5. *šāmər-û*: *šāmər* `guard.QTL`, the qal qatal stem of *š-m-r*; *-û* `3PL`, "they".
6. "Now Rachel had taken the household gods." After *wə-* the subject comes before the qatal: the writer steps out of the story's chain to give background, here an earlier event (Gesenius §106e–f).
7. "*He* took", with emphasis, as against someone else. The plain report is *lāqaḥ*: the verb already says "he".
8. *Mayim* is plural in form, and the verb agrees with it: third person plural, *gāḇərû*.
9. *Zāqēn* is a stative verb. Its qatal states a condition reached, and the clause, with the subject first, describes David at the start of the story, so "King David was old" (Gesenius §106e).
