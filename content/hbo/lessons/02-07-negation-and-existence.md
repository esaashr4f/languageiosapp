## The idea

You now have two kinds of clause: the verbless clause, which describes (lesson 2.1), and the verbal clause, which reports an event (lesson 2.6). Hebrew negates them with different words, and that is the organising fact of this lesson. Gesenius puts it in one sentence: verbal clauses "are regularly negatived by *lōʾ*", while *ʾên* "is used exclusively with noun-clauses" (§152a).

- **lōʾ** "not" goes in front of a verb: *lōʾ yāləḏâ* "she did not bear".
- **ʾên** "there is not" goes in front of the noun whose existence or presence it denies: *ʾên meleḵ* "there was no king".

The second needs a partner. **yēš** "there is" says that something exists or is present: *yēš YHWH bammāqôm hazzeh* "the LORD is in this place". Gesenius says *ʾên* "is the negative of *yēš*" (§152i).

Where do these two words come from? Gesenius describes both as originally nouns: *yēš* "existence" and *ʾayin* "non-existence" (§141k, §152i). So *ʾên meleḵ* is, at root, "non-existence of a king". That is why *ʾên* stands before a noun, as a construct form does, and why, like a noun, it has two shapes: *ʾên* before what it negates, and *ʾayin* when it stands alone at the end of a clause (§152k–l). Spanish *hay* / *no hay* and French *il y a* / *il n'y a pas* do the same job, but with a verb. Hebrew's words have no tense: Gesenius notes that *yēš* "includes the idea of being in all tenses", and *ʾên* the idea of not being (§152i).

## How it is built

### lōʾ with a verb

1. Build the verbal clause: *yāləḏâ lô* "she bore (children) to him".
2. Put *lōʾ* immediately before the verb (Gesenius §152e): *lōʾ yāləḏâ lô*.
3. Everything else stays as it was. The subject may stand first: *wəśāray ... lōʾ yāləḏâ lô*.

### yēš and ʾên

1. Name the thing that exists or does not: "a king".
2. Put *yēš* or *ʾên* before it: *ʾên meleḵ*.
3. Add where or for whom, if needed: *bəyiśrāʾēl* "in Israel".

Both words often combine with *lə-* to say what someone has or lacks (lesson 2.5): *yeš-lānû ʾāḇ* "we have a father", *ʾên lāh wālāḏ* "she had no child". *Yēš* becomes *yeš-* when joined by maqqef, because it loses its stress (lesson 1.8).

### ʾên with a whole verbless clause

*ʾên* can also negate a complete verbless clause, standing before its subject: *ʾên-yôsēp̄ babbôr* "Joseph was not in the pit" (Gesenius §152l). When the subject is a pronoun, it becomes an ending on *ʾên*: *ʾênennû* "he is not (there), he is no more" (§152m; the endings are part 3).

### ʾayin at the end

After the noun, alone, the form is *ʾayin*: *wəʾāḏām ʾayin* "and there was no man" (Genesis 2:5). Gesenius notes that this form "always follows the word negatived" (§152k). As an answer it means "no", "there is none" (lesson 2.8).

## Where it applies

**lōʾ**

- **Before a verb** in a statement: *lōʾ yāləḏâ*, *lōʾ ʾāḵal*, *lōʾ yāḏāʿtî*.
- **"Not any", "nothing", "no one"**, with an indefinite noun: *lōʾ ʾāḵal leḥem* "he ate nothing". With *kōl* or *ʾîš*, *lōʾ* means "none at all" (Gesenius §152b).
- **Before a single word**, to negate that word: *lōʾ-ṭôḇ* "not good" (Genesis 2:18). Gesenius notes this as a use besides the negation of clauses (§152a).
- **In a verbless clause, for emphasis**, especially when the subject is a pronoun: *lōʾ ʾîš dəḇārîm ʾānōḵî* (§152d).
- **As an answer, "no!"**, a clause cut down to one word (§152c; lesson 2.8).
- **With a future form, as an absolute ban**: "you shall not ...", as in the Ten Commandments (Gesenius §152b; part 4).

**yēš**

- **Existence or presence**: *yēš YHWH bammāqôm hazzeh*.
- **Possession**, with *lə-*: *yeš-lānû ʾāḇ zāqēn*.
- **In questions**: "is there ...?", "do you have ...?" (lesson 2.8).

**ʾên / ʾayin**

- **Non-existence or absence**: *ʾên meleḵ bəyiśrāʾēl*.
- **Not having**, with *lə-*: *ʾên lāh wālāḏ*.
- **A whole verbless clause**: *ʾên-yôsēp̄ babbôr*.
- **With a pronoun ending**: *ʾênennû* "he is not".
- **Absolute, after the noun or alone**: *wəʾāḏām ʾayin*; as an answer, "no".
- **Before a participle**, "there was no one seeing" (Gesenius §152l): part 4.

**ʾal** is a third negative. It goes with a wish or command, "do not!", and is taught with the command forms in part 4 (Gesenius §152f).

## Watch out

**Do not use *ʾên* with a verb.** By analogy with Spanish *no* before any verb, you might write *\*ʾên yāləḏâ*. *ʾên* belongs to noun clauses (Gesenius §152a). Before a qatal, use *lōʾ*: *lōʾ yāləḏâ*.

**Do not use *lōʾ* for "there is no".** *\*lōʾ meleḵ bəyiśrāʾēl* is not the classical way to deny existence. Use *ʾên*: *ʾên meleḵ bəyiśrāʾēl*.

**Do not put *lōʾ* after the verb.** French *ne ... pas* puts part of the negation after the verb. Hebrew *lōʾ* comes first: *lōʾ ʾāḵal*, not *\*ʾāḵal lōʾ*.

**Do not add *yēš* to an ordinary description.** *Yēš* says that something is there. "Joseph is good" is *yôsēp̄ ṭôḇ*; *\*yôsēp̄ yēš ṭôḇ* is not Hebrew (lesson 2.1).

**Do not translate *yēš* and *ʾên* with a fixed tense.** They have none. *ʾên meleḵ* is "there was no king" in Judges, but could be "there is no king" in a speech.

**Do not confuse *ʾal* with *ʿal*.** *ʾal* (alef) is "do not". *ʿal* (ʿayin) is "on, over". The vowels are the same; the first letter tells them apart.

**Do not use *lōʾ* for a request.** "Do not go!" said as a wish or plea takes *ʾal*, not *lōʾ* (Gesenius §152b, f; part 4).

***ʾên* or *ʾayin*?** Before the noun, *ʾên*; after it or alone, *ʾayin*. *\*ʾayin meleḵ* and *\*meleḵ ʾên* are both wrong.

**A double negative is not a positive.** *Lōʾ* with *kōl* means "none at all" (Gesenius §152b), like Spanish *no ... nada*, not "not everything".

## Connections

Arabic splits negation in a similar way: *lā* and *lam* with verbs, *laysa* "is not" for noun clauses. Modern Hebrew keeps *yeš* and *ʾeyn* and the possession pattern *yeš li* "I have", exactly as in Genesis 44:20.

## Exercises

1. Negate *šāmaʿ ʾĕlōhîm*.
2. Build "There is a prophet in Israel" and "There is no prophet in Israel".
3. Build "Laban has a daughter" and "Laban has no daughter".
4. Translate *ʾên-yôsēp̄ babbôr* and explain why it is *ʾên* and not *lōʾ*.
5. Why does Exodus 4:10 have *lōʾ* in a verbless clause?
6. A student writes *\*ʾên ʾāḵal leḥem* for "he did not eat bread". Correct it.
7. When is the form *ʾayin* used instead of *ʾên*? Give an example.
8. Translate *yeš-lānû ʾāḇ zāqēn*, and explain *yeš-*.
9. Build "The king did not remember".

## Worked answers

1. *lōʾ šāmaʿ ʾĕlōhîm* "God did not hear". *Lōʾ* goes immediately before the verb.
2. *yēš nāḇîʾ bəyiśrāʾēl*; *ʾên nāḇîʾ bəyiśrāʾēl*. Existence and non-existence take *yēš* and *ʾên*, placed before the noun.
3. *yēš ləlāḇān baṯ*; *ʾên ləlāḇān baṯ*. Hebrew "has" is "there is to": *yēš/ʾên* + *lə-* + the owner + the thing owned.
4. "Joseph was not in the pit." It is a verbless clause ("Joseph is in the pit"), and noun clauses are negated by *ʾên* (Gesenius §152a, l).
5. With a pronoun subject and a noun predicate, *lōʾ* negates the predicate: "a man of words is what I am not" (Gesenius §152d).
6. *lōʾ ʾāḵal leḥem*. *ʾāḵal* is a verb, and verbs take *lōʾ*.
7. When it stands after the noun it negates, or alone: *wəʾāḏām ʾayin* "and there was no man" (Genesis 2:5; Gesenius §152k). As an answer, *ʾayin* "no, there is none".
8. "We have an old father." *Yēš* is joined by maqqef to *lānû* and loses its stress, so its vowel shortens to segol (lesson 1.8).
9. *lōʾ zāḵar hammeleḵ*. Negative, verb, subject.
