## The idea

A **yes/no question** asks whether something is so: "Is he well?" "Is there a man here?" English turns the words round ("he is" → "is he") or adds "do". Spanish and French can keep the statement and change the voice: *¿Está bien?* Hebrew keeps the statement and puts a marker in front of it, the prefix **hă-**, glossed Q. It does the job of French *est-ce que* or Japanese *ka*, but at the front: *šālôm lô* "he is well" → *hăšālôm lô* "is he well?" (Genesis 29:6).

Nothing else changes. Word order, the verb, the negative all stay as in the statement. Gesenius calls *hă-* the regular mark of the simple question, "when the questioner is wholly uncertain as to the answer to be expected", and notes that it can stand before verbless and verbal clauses alike (§150c–d). He adds that it always stands at the very beginning of the clause (§100n).

A question can also be marked by tone alone, with no particle at all: *ʾattâ zeh bənî ʿēśāw* "you are really my son Esau?" (Genesis 27:24). Gesenius says this happens "frequently", where "the natural emphasis upon the words is of itself sufficient" (§150a). On the page you can only tell it from context.

## How it is built

### The form of hă-

The prefix is a *h* with a short vowel. Which vowel depends on the next letter (Gesenius §100k–n):

1. **Before an ordinary consonant with a full vowel: ḥaṭep̄ pataḥ, hă-.** *hă=šālôm*, *hă=yēš*.
2. **Before a consonant with shewa: pataḥ, ha-, usually with no doubling.** Rarely, as in Genesis 37:32, the next consonant is doubled, *ha=kkəṯōneṯ*, so that the question marker looks exactly like the article (§100l).
3. **Before a guttural: pataḥ, ha-.** *ha=ʾattâ*, *ha=ʾên*, *ha=ʿôḏ* (§100m).
4. **Before a guttural with qameṣ: segol, he-.** "Am I ...?" with *ʾānōḵî* is *heʾānōḵî* (§100n).

So *hă-* is usually told from the article by its vowel: the article has a full pataḥ and doubles the next consonant; the question marker has a ḥaṭep̄ vowel, or a pataḥ with no doubling. Where they look the same, the sense decides.

### Building a question

1. Build the statement: *yēš pōh ʾîš* "there is a man here".
2. Put *hă-* on the first word: *hăyēš-pōh ʾîš* "is there a man here?" (Judges 4:20).
3. To ask "or not?", add *ʾim-lōʾ* at the end: *haʾattâ ... ʾim-lōʾ* "are you ... or not?" (§150c).

### Negative questions: hălōʾ

*hă-* + *lōʾ* gives *hălōʾ* "is it not ...?". Very often this does not ask at all. It reminds the hearer of something both sides accept, and so means "surely", "you know that ...". Gesenius notes that such questions serve "to express the conviction that the contents of the statement are well known to the hearer" (§150e). The formula of the books of Kings, *hălôʾ-hēm kəṯuḇîm* "are they not written ...?", means "they are written" (§150e).

### Answering

Hebrew has no single word for "yes". Gesenius: the yes answer "is generally expressed ... by repeating the emphatic word in the question", with "you" turned into "I" where needed (§150n). "Is he well?" — "Well." "Are you Esau?" — "I." For "no", *lōʾ* alone can serve, or *ʾayin* "there is none" (§150n, §152c).

## Where it applies

- **Verbless questions:** *hăšālôm lô*, *haʿôḏ ʾāḇî ḥāy*.
- **Existence and possession:** *hăyēš-pōh ʾîš*, *hăyēš lāḵem ʾāḥ*; negative, *haʾên pōh nāḇîʾ*.
- **Verbal questions:** before a qatal or any verb, the same marker: "have you set your heart ...?" (Gesenius §150d).
- **"Or not?":** *hă-* ... *ʾim-lōʾ* (Genesis 27:21, 37:32). A choice between two options is *hă-* ... *ʾim* ... "X or Y?" (§150c).
- **Questions expecting "no", or refusing:** *hăšōmēr ʾāḥî ʾānōḵî* (§150d).
- **"Surely", with *hălōʾ*:** *hălōʾ ḵol-hāʾāreṣ ləp̄ānêḵā*; the Kings formula *hălôʾ-hēm kəṯuḇîm* "they are written" (1 Kings 11:41; §150e).
- **Questions by tone alone:** *ʾattâ zeh bənî ʿēśāw* (§150a). Especially after *wə-*, a statement can be an indignant question: "and I should not have pity?" (Gesenius §150a, on Jonah 4:11).
- **Answers:** repeat the key word (*šālôm*), use a pronoun (*ʾānî*), or deny with *lōʾ* or *ʾayin*.
- **Not for question words.** "Who?", "what?", "where?" have their own words and need no *hă-* (part 6).

## Watch out

**Do not change the word order.** English "is he" and French *est-il* invert. Hebrew does not: *hăšālôm lô*, not some reordered form. Put *hă-* on the front of the statement as it stands.

**Do not put *hă-* anywhere but the start.** *\*šālôm hălô* is wrong. The marker stands at the beginning of the clause (Gesenius §100n).

**Do not read *hă-* as the article.** *Hăyēš* is not "the there-is". The article has full pataḥ and doubling (*hammeleḵ*); the question marker usually has ḥaṭep̄ pataḥ (*hăšālôm*) or pataḥ without doubling (*haʾên*). Where they coincide, as in *hakkəṯōneṯ* (Genesis 37:32), look for other signs, such as *ʾim-lōʾ*.

**Do not answer "yes" with a word for "yes".** Spanish *sí* and French *oui* have no Hebrew equivalent here. Repeat the word asked about: *šālôm*, *ʾānî*, *yēš*.

**Do not take every *hălōʾ* as a real question.** "Is it not written?" means "it is written". Translate by the sense: "surely", "as you know".

**Do not use *hă-* with a question word.** *\*hămî* for "who?" is wrong; *mî* "who" asks by itself (part 6).

**Questions without *hă-* exist.** If a clause seems odd as a statement, read it as a question marked by tone: *ʾattâ zeh bənî ʿēśāw* (Genesis 27:24). The English translations you compare against will show the question mark, but the Hebrew shows nothing.

**Do not confuse *ʾim* "or" with *ʾô* "or".** In a question with two options, the second is introduced by *ʾim*: *hă-* ... *ʾim-lōʾ*. In a statement, "or" between two words is *ʾô* (lesson 2.3).

## Connections

Arabic marks a yes/no question with a prefix too, *ʾa-* (*ʾa-ʾanta* "are you?"), or the word *hal*, and both stand at the head of the clause like Hebrew *hă-*. Japanese and Korean put their question markers at the end (*ka*, *-kka*), but share with Hebrew the habit of answering by repeating the verb rather than with a word for "yes".

## Exercises

1. Turn *šālôm lāh* "she is well" into a question.
2. Turn *yēš lāḵem ʾāḇ* "you have a father" into a question, and give a "yes" and a "no" answer.
3. Ask "Is the king still alive?"
4. Why is it *haʾên* but *hăyēš*?
5. Ask "Are you a prophet or not?", speaking to a man.
6. Translate *hălōʾ ḵol-hāʾāreṣ ləp̄ānêḵā* and explain what kind of question it is.
7. A student writes *\*hammeleḵ ḥay* for "Is the king alive?". What does it mean, and how is the question written?
8. How does Hebrew say "yes" in answer to *hăšālôm lô*?
9. Translate *haʿôḏ ʾāḇî ḥāy*.

## Worked answers

1. *hăšālôm lāh*. Add *hă-* to the first word; nothing else changes.
2. *hăyēš lāḵem ʾāḇ*. Yes: *yēš* "there is". No: *ʾayin* "there is none". The answer repeats or denies the key word (Gesenius §150n).
3. *haʿôḏ hammeleḵ ḥay*. The question marker is *ha-* before ʿayin; *hammeleḵ* keeps its article.
4. Before a guttural, alef, the marker takes full pataḥ: *ha-*. Before *y*, an ordinary consonant with a full vowel, it takes ḥaṭep̄ pataḥ: *hă-* (§100k, m).
5. *haʾattâ nāḇîʾ ʾim-lōʾ*. *ʾattâ* begins with alef, so *ha-*; "or not" is *ʾim-lōʾ*.
6. "Is not the whole land before you?" It is a *hălōʾ* question that does not ask: it reminds Lot of a fact, "the whole land lies open to you" (Gesenius §150e).
7. It means "the king is alive", a statement: the *ha-* there is the article, with doubling. To ask, put the question marker on the first word of the clause. The neatest way is to put the predicate first: *haḥay hammeleḵ* "is the king alive?", with *ha-* before the guttural ḥ (Gesenius §100m).
8. By repeating the key word: *šālôm* "(he is) well" (Genesis 29:6).
9. "Is my father still alive?" *Ha-* before ʿayin, the adverb *ʿôḏ* "still", the noun with "my", and the predicate *ḥāy*, pausal.
