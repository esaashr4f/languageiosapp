## The idea

In "God created the heavens", *God* does the creating and *the heavens* are what is created. The second is the **object**: the person or thing a verb acts on. Latin and Arabic mark the object with a case ending (*caelum*, *as-samāʾa*). English marks it by position: subject before the verb, object after. Hebrew has neither tool to hand. It lost its old case endings, and its word order is free enough that position alone does not settle who did what to whom.

What it has instead is a small word, **ʾēṯ**, placed before the object, glossed OBJ. It has no English translation. It does not mean "the"; it means "what follows is the object". You have seen it in the first verse of the Bible: *bārāʾ ʾĕlōhîm ʾēṯ haššāmayim* "God created the heavens".

The striking fact is that *ʾēṯ* is used only before a **definite** object. Gesenius states the rule: the marker "is not invariably necessary but is restricted to those cases in which the accusative of the object is more closely determined by being a proper name, or by having the article, or by a following determinate genitive (hence also by the suffixes)" (§117a). An indefinite object is left bare: *lōʾ ʾāḵal leḥem* "he ate no food".

Why only the definite? Gesenius suggests a history, and offers it as probable, not proven (§117b). The need for a sign of the object "could only have been felt after the case-endings had become wholly extinct". At first, he thinks, *ʾēṯ* marked only an object placed before the verb, where position could not show its role, or a proper name; later it spread through prose to definite objects generally. He does not explain why indefinite objects never joined them, and the sources consulted give no firmer account. Spanish has a close parallel: the *a* in *veo a Juan* marks a definite, personal object, and is absent in *veo una casa*.

## How it is built

1. Find the object of the verb: in "God heard the voice of the boy", the voice.
2. Ask whether it is definite. It is, if it is
   - a name: *ʾaḇrāhām*, *ḥawwâ*;
   - a noun with the article: *hāʾāreṣ*, *hāʾôr*;
   - a noun followed by a definite noun ("the voice of the boy"), or with a pronoun ending ("my son", "his wife");
   - a pronoun object: "you", "me" (as an ending on *ʾēṯ*, below);
   - *kōl* "all" (Gesenius §117c).
3. If it is definite, put *ʾēṯ* before it. If not, put nothing.
4. The shape: *ʾēṯ* standing alone, as in Genesis 1:1; *ʾeṯ-* with segol when joined by maqqef, which is the commonest form (lesson 1.8): *ʾeṯ-hāʾāreṣ*.
5. With a pronoun, the pronoun becomes an ending, and the marker usually takes the vowel *ō*: *ʾōṯî* "me", *ʾōṯəḵā* "you", *ʾōṯô* "him". The full set is in part 3.
6. If there are several objects, each usually gets its own marker: *ʾēṯ haššāmayim wəʾēṯ hāʾāreṣ*.

So "the LORD has given you the land" goes: verb *nāṯan*, subject *YHWH*, "to you" *lāḵem*, object *hāʾāreṣ* (definite, with the article), so *ʾeṯ-hāʾāreṣ*: *nāṯan YHWH lāḵem ʾeṯ-hāʾāreṣ* (Joshua 2:9).

## Where it applies

- **Definite nouns as objects:** with the article (*ʾeṯ-hāʾāreṣ*), names (*ʾeṯ-ḥawwâ*), nouns followed by a definite noun (*ʾeṯ-qôl hannaʿar*), nouns with pronoun endings ("his sheep", *ʾeṯ-ṣōʾnô*, Genesis 31:19).
- **Pronoun objects:** *ʾōṯî*, *ʾōṯəḵā*, *ʾōṯô*. Part 3 gives the set; part 4 shows the alternative, a pronoun ending on the verb itself (Gesenius §117b notes that prose often prefers *ʾēṯ* + ending).
- **"All":** *ʾeṯ-kol-* + noun, since *kōl* is definite in sense (§117c).
- **Each of several objects:** *ʾēṯ haššāmayim wəʾēṯ hāʾāreṣ*.
- **Fronted objects:** an object put before the verb for contrast, as in 1 Samuel 8:7, keeps its marker, which is then the clearest sign of who did what.
- **In prose.** Gesenius notes that *ʾēṯ* is "somewhat rare in poetry" and absent from several old poems (§117b). When you reach the Psalms, expect definite objects without it.
- **Not with indefinite objects:** *ʾereṣ wəšāmāyim* (Genesis 2:4), *leḥem*, *mayim*. Gesenius calls *ʾēṯ* before a truly indefinite noun in prose "very seldom", and most of his exceptions are nouns that are definite in sense (§117d).
- **Later:** in some passages *ʾēṯ* marks a noun that is not an object, for instance with passive verbs; these are part 7.

## Watch out

**Do not translate *ʾēṯ*.** It is not "the", and not "it". *ʾēṯ haššāmayim* is "the heavens"; the article *ha-* is "the", and *ʾēṯ* adds only "object". Leave it out of your English.

**Do not use *ʾēṯ* with an indefinite object.** By analogy with Genesis 1:1 you might write *\*lōʾ ʾāḵal ʾeṯ-leḥem* for "he ate no bread". The bread is indefinite, so no marker: *lōʾ ʾāḵal leḥem*.

**Do not leave it out before a definite object in prose.** *\*nāṯan YHWH lāḵem hāʾāreṣ* is the shape of poetry, not of classical prose. Write *ʾeṯ-hāʾāreṣ*.

**A noun without the article can still be definite.** *qôl hannaʿar* "the voice of the boy" has no article on *qôl*, but it is definite and takes *ʾēṯ*. So do names and nouns with "my", "his". Ask whether the noun is definite, not whether it has *ha-*.

**Do not mark the subject.** *ʾēṯ* goes on the object, never on the doer. In *ʾeṯ-qôl hannaʿar*, *hannaʿar* is not the subject; God is.

**Do not confuse *ʾēṯ* "with" and *ʾēṯ* OBJ.** They are spelled alike. *Ḥănôḵ ʾeṯ-hāʾĕlōhîm* after "walked" is "with God". Two tests: does the verb take an object at all? And with pronoun endings the two differ: OBJ is *ʾōṯô* "him", "with" is *ʾittô* "with him" (part 3). Eve's words in Genesis 4:1, *qānîṯî ʾîš ʾeṯ-YHWH*, are usually read as "with (the help of) the LORD"; the line is debated.

**Do not use *ʾēṯ* before a verbless predicate.** In *ʾattâ hāʾîš* there is no verb and no object. *\*ʾattâ ʾeṯ-hāʾîš* is wrong.

**Do not take *ʾeṯ-* for a different word.** With maqqef the vowel shortens to segol because the word loses its stress (lesson 1.8). *ʾēṯ* and *ʾeṯ-* are one word.

## Connections

Spanish "personal *a*" is the closest parallel for this reader: *vi a María* but *vi una casa*. Like Hebrew *ʾēṯ*, it marks objects that could be mistaken for subjects, and it has no translation. Arabic has no such word, because it kept the accusative ending *-a*; Gesenius ties the rise of Hebrew *ʾēṯ* to the loss of those endings (§117b).

## Exercises

1. Build "God created the light".
2. Build "The king found a sword", and explain why there is no *ʾēṯ*.
3. Build "Abraham remembered Sarah".
4. Build "Jacob heard the voice of Rachel".
5. Translate *lōʾ-šāṯâ mayim* and explain the absence of *ʾēṯ*.
6. In *kî lōʾ ʾōṯəḵā māʾāsû*, which word is the object, and how do you know although it comes before the verb?
7. A student writes *\*ʾeṯ-dāwiḏ hālaḵ* for "David went". What is wrong?
8. *Wayyiṯhallēḵ ḥănôḵ ʾeṯ-hāʾĕlōhîm*: why is *ʾeṯ-* not the object marker here?
9. Translate Genesis 1:1 word by word and name each part.

## Worked answers

1. *bārāʾ ʾĕlōhîm ʾeṯ-hāʾôr*. The object has the article, so it is definite and takes *ʾeṯ-*.
2. *māṣāʾ hammeleḵ ḥereḇ*. "A sword" is indefinite, and an indefinite object is left bare (Gesenius §117a).
3. *zāḵar ʾaḇrāhām ʾeṯ-śārâ*. A name is definite.
4. *šāmaʿ yaʿăqōḇ ʾeṯ-qôl rāḥēl*. "The voice of Rachel" is definite because it is followed by a name; *qôl* itself has no article.
5. "He did not drink water", "he drank no water". *Mayim* has no article and is not otherwise definite, so it takes no marker.
6. *ʾōṯəḵā* "you" is the object: it is the marker *ʾōṯ-* with the ending *-əḵā*. The marker shows the role, whatever the order.
7. *Hālaḵ* "go" takes no object, and David is the subject. The subject is never marked: *hālaḵ dāwiḏ*.
8. "Walk" takes no object, so *ʾēṯ* here is the preposition "with". With a pronoun the two would differ, *ʾōṯô* versus *ʾittô*.
9. *bə=rēʾšîṯ* "in (the) beginning", a prepositional phrase of time (lesson 2.5); *bārāʾ* "he created", qal qatal 3SG.M (2.6); *ʾĕlōhîm* "God", the subject after the verb; *ʾēṯ* the object marker (2.9); *ha=ššāmayim* "the heavens", with the article and doubling (2.2); *wə=* "and" (2.3); *ʾēṯ* again for the second object; *hā=ʾāreṣ* "the earth", with *hā-* before alef and the pausal vowel (2.2).
