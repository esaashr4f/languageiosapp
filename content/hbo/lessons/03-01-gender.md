## The idea

Every Hebrew noun is either **masculine** or **feminine**. There is no third gender, no "it". Things and ideas that Latin or German would make neuter are put into one of the two classes, more often the feminine (Gesenius §80a, §122q).

You met gender in lesson 2.4: *hûʾ* "he" and *hîʾ* "she", *ʾattâ* to a man and *ʾatt* to a woman. Lesson 2.6 added the qatal ending *-â* for "she". The same split runs through the nouns. A noun's gender matters because other words must agree with it: the verb, the adjective, the pronoun that refers back to it. You say *ʾîš ḥāḵām* "a wise man" but *ʾiššâ ḥăḵāmâ* "a wise woman".

The masculine is the plain form. It has no ending of its own (§80b). The feminine usually has one: *-â*, or a *-t* in one of several shapes. Gesenius explains that the feminine ending was originally *-at*, the same *-at* that marks "she" on the verb (§80b, §44a). In most nouns the *t* was lost at the end of the word: the vowel before it lengthened, and a silent *h* was written to show the long vowel (§80c). So *sûs* "horse" has a feminine *sûsâ* "mare". The old *t* still surfaces when another word is bound to the noun: *šip̄ḥâ* "maidservant" but *šip̄ḥaṯ śāray* "the maidservant of Sarai". That bound form is lesson 3.5.

But the ending is a habit, not a law. Many feminine nouns have no ending at all: *ʾēm* "mother", *ʿîr* "city", *ʾereṣ* "land", *ʾeḇen* "stone", *yāḏ* "hand". And a few masculine nouns end in *-â*. A noun's gender is a fact about the word, recorded in the dictionary (BDB writes "n.m." or "n.f."). The surest way to see it in a text is to look at what agrees with it.

## How it is built

### The feminine endings

Start from a masculine noun or adjective and add one of these (Gesenius §80c–d).

1. **Stressed *-â*,** written with a final *h*. This is the commonest. *naʿar* "boy" → *naʿărâ* "girl"; *par* "bull" → *pārâ* "cow"; *ṭôḇ* → *ṭôḇâ* "good"; *gāḏôl* → *gəḏôlâ* "great". The stress moves onto the ending, and a vowel two syllables before it shrinks: *gā-* becomes *gə-*. Lesson 3.3 explains that shrinking; for now notice it.
2. ***-t* after a vowel.** A word ending in *-î* takes *-ṯ*: Gesenius's example is *yəhûḏî* "a Jew (man)", *yəhûḏîṯ* "a Jewish woman". The word for "Hebrew" usually takes *-â* instead, with the *y* doubled: *ʿiḇrî*, *ʿiḇriyyâ* (§80c).
3. ***-eṯ* or *-aṯ* after a consonant,** with a helping vowel: segol in most words, pataḥ next to a guttural (§80d). *gəḇereṯ* "mistress", *kəṯōneṯ* "tunic", *môleḏeṯ* "kindred". This shape is commonest in the participle, the verb's adjective form (part 4): *bōraḥaṯ* "fleeing (woman)".
4. **Two more endings make abstract nouns:** *-ûṯ*, as in *malḵûṯ* "kingship", and *-îṯ*, as in *rēʾšîṯ* "beginning" from *rōʾš* "head" (§86k–l). Gesenius notes that *-ûṯ* becomes common only in the later books.

### Feminine with no ending

Gesenius sorts the feminine nouns without an ending into groups (§80b, §122b–o). They are tendencies, not rules, but they cover most of the words you will meet.

- **Female by meaning.** *ʾēm* "mother", *ʾāṯôn* "she-donkey", *ʿēz* "she-goat", *rāḥēl* "ewe" (the name Rachel). Often the male has a different word: *ʾāḇ* and *ʾēm*, *ḥămôr* "male donkey" and *ʾāṯôn*, *ʿeḇeḏ* "servant" and *šip̄ḥâ* "maidservant".
- **Places with limits.** Countries, cities and enclosed spaces: *ʾereṣ*, *ʿîr*, *bəʾēr* "well".
- **Parts of the body, especially those in pairs.** *yāḏ*, *kap̄* "palm", *reḡel* "foot", *ʾōzen* "ear", *ʿayin* "eye", *ʿeṣem* "bone".
- **Tools.** *ḥereḇ* "sword".
- **Some natural substances and forces.** *ʾeḇen* "stone", *ʾēš* "fire", *rûaḥ* "wind", *nep̄eš* "breath, life".

Gesenius offers reasons for these groups: a land as the mother of its people, tools and limbs as "subservient" (§122h, m). These are his interpretations, and no one can test them. Treat the groups as memory aids.

### Masculine with a feminine-looking ending

*laylâ* "night" ends in *-â* but is always masculine (§90f). So are *śāḏeh* "field" and *miqneh* "livestock", whose *-eh* comes from the last letter of the root (a weak root, part 4), not from an ending. Men's names can end in *-â* too: *yəhûḏâ* Judah.

### Some nouns take both genders

*šemeš* "sun", *dereḵ* "way" and *māqôm* "place" are treated now as masculine, now as feminine (§122l, o). Examples 6 and 7 show the sun both ways within Genesis.

### Agreement shows the gender

To say "the stone was big":

1. The noun is *ʾeḇen*. It has no ending, but BDB marks it "n.f.", and it belongs to the substances.
2. The adjective must agree, so it takes the feminine *-â*: *gāḏôl* → *gəḏōlâ*.
3. As a verbless clause with a definite subject (lesson 2.1): *hāʾeḇen gəḏōlâ*.

Reading works the other way. You see *haššemeš bāʾâ*: the verb ends in *-â*, so here the sun is feminine.

### Mixed groups

When men and women are referred to together, the masculine is used. Gesenius calls it the "prior gender", which "includes the feminine" (§122g). His example is example 11 below.

## Where it applies

- **Female people and animals** take a feminine noun, with an ending (*naʿărâ*, *pārâ*, *ʿiḇriyyâ*) or without (*ʾēm*, *ʿēz*).
- **Lands and cities** are feminine, including names of countries (§122h): *ʿîr*, *ʾereṣ*. Gesenius notes that a country's name can be masculine when it means the people (§122i).
- **Paired body parts, tools, and some substances** are feminine without an ending (lists above).
- **Abstract ideas** are usually feminine and usually have an ending: *ṣəḏāqâ* "righteousness", *milḥāmâ* "war", *ḥoḵmâ* "wisdom", *malḵûṯ* "kingship" (§122q).
- **"A good thing", "evil".** The feminine adjective standing alone acts as an abstract noun, where Latin or Greek would use the neuter: *ṭôḇâ* "goodness, good fortune", *rāʿâ* "harm, disaster" (§122q).
- **Groups** are often named by a feminine noun: *ʿēḏâ* "congregation", *mišpāḥâ* "clan" (compare §122s on feminine collectives).
- **Agreement.** A feminine noun takes a feminine adjective (example 4), a feminine pronoun (examples 4 and 2.4's *ʾăḥōṯî hîʾ*), and a feminine verb (example 7). Adjectives are lesson 3.11; the verb in full is part 4.
- **Mixed groups** take the masculine (example 11).
- **Later in the guide:** the plural endings *-îm* and *-ôṯ* (lesson 3.2), which do not follow gender as neatly as you would expect; the bound feminine in *-aṯ* (lessons 3.5–3.6); feminine suffixes such as *-āh* "her" (lesson 3.7).

## Watch out

**No ending does not mean masculine.** By analogy with *meleḵ*, you might read *ʿîr* as masculine and write *\*hāʿîr haggāḏôl*. It is feminine: *hāʿîr haggəḏōlâ*. Learn the gender with the word.

**An *-â* does not always mean feminine.** *laylâ* is masculine, so "this night" is *hallaylâ hazzeh*, never *\*hallaylâ hazzōʾṯ*. Names in *-â* (*yəhûḏâ*) can belong to men.

**Not every *-â* is an ending at all.** In *śāḏeh* and *miqneh* the final *-eh* belongs to the root; they are masculine. In *yāp̄eh* "beautiful" the *-eh* gives way to *-â* in the feminine, *yāp̄â*, rather than taking an extra ending. *\*yāp̄ehâ* is impossible.

**The feminine is not "the masculine plus an ending" in every case.** "Woman" is not *\*ʾîšâ* from *ʾîš*; it is *ʾiššâ*, with a doubled *š*. The narrator of Genesis 2:23 plays on the likeness of the two words, but they are separate in form (lesson 3.12).

**Spanish and French genders do not carry over.** *El sol*, *le soleil* are masculine; the Hebrew sun is either. *La mano*, *la main* happen to match *yāḏ*, but *la ciudad* and *ʿîr* match by chance too. Do not guess from a European language.

**Don't treat a mixed group as feminine.** "Male and female he created them" uses the masculine *ʾōṯām*, not *\*ʾōṯān*. The feminine plural is used only when every member is female.

**In the Torah, "girl" is often spelled without its *h*.** The consonants נער are read *naʿărâ*: WLC prints הַנַּעֲרָ in Genesis 24:14 and elsewhere. BDB counts about twenty such places in Genesis and Deuteronomy. In Genesis WLC treats it as a perpetual qere, like *hîʾ* (lesson 1.9); in Deuteronomy 22 it prints the bare consonants with a marginal qere. Either way it is not a masculine noun.

## Connections

Arabic has the same split and the same history. Its feminine *-at* (the *tāʾ marbūṭa*, ة) is pronounced *-a* at the end of a phrase but *-at* when a noun is bound to it: *madīna* "city" but *madīnat* + noun. Hebrew went one step further and lost the *t* everywhere except in the bound form. Arabic also has feminine nouns with no ending, many of them in the same classes: *ʾarḍ* "land", *yad* "hand", *šams* "sun".

## Exercises

1. Give the feminine of *naʿar*, *par* and *ʿiḇrî*, and say which ending each takes.
2. *ʾeḇen* has no ending. How would you show from a text that it is feminine?
3. Translate *wəhannaʿărâ yāp̄â ʿaḏ-məʾōḏ*, and name every word that shows the feminine.
4. Build "The city is great", with the predicate first.
5. A student writes *\*hallaylâ hazzōʾṯ* for "this night". What is wrong?
6. In Genesis 28:11 the sun "set" is *bāʾ*; in 15:17 it is *bāʾâ*. Is one of them a mistake?
7. Why is "them" in *zāḵār ûnəqēḇâ bārāʾ ʾōṯām* masculine?
8. Why does Hagar's title appear as *šip̄ḥaṯ* in *hāḡār šip̄ḥaṯ śāray*, when the word is *šip̄ḥâ*?

## Worked answers

1. *naʿărâ*, *pārâ*: the commonest ending, stressed *-â*. *ʿiḇriyyâ*: after *-î* the *y* doubles and *-â* follows (Gesenius §80c). The vowel of *naʿar* shifts because the stress moves onto the ending.
2. Look at what agrees with it. In Genesis 29:2 the adjective is feminine: *hāʾeḇen gəḏōlâ*. A feminine verb or pronoun would show the same. BDB also lists it as "n.f.".
3. "The girl was extremely beautiful." *naʿărâ* has the ending *-â*; the adjective *yāp̄â* is the feminine of *yāp̄eh*. No verb is needed: it is a verbless clause (lesson 2.1).
4. *gəḏōlâ hāʿîr*. *ʿîr* is feminine without an ending, so the adjective takes *-â*; the predicate has no article (lessons 2.1, 2.2).
5. *laylâ* is masculine despite its *-â* (Gesenius §90f), so "this" must be masculine: *hallaylâ hazzeh*, as in Exodus 12:8.
6. Neither. *šemeš* is one of the nouns used with both genders (Gesenius §122o). Each verb agrees with the gender the writer gave the word.
7. The pair is mixed, and the masculine covers mixed groups (Gesenius §122g).
8. The feminine ending was once *-at*. It lost its *t* at the end of a word, but kept it when another noun is bound to it (Gesenius §80b). This bound form is the construct state, lesson 3.5.
