## The idea

Hebrew nouns have three numbers. The **singular** is one. The **plural** is more than one. The **dual** is two, but only for things that come in pairs or are thought of as a pair: hands, eyes, a two-day stretch.

Each number has its ending, added to the singular:

- plural **-îm**, mostly on masculine nouns: *sûs* → *sûsîm* "horses";
- plural **-ôṯ**, mostly on feminine nouns: *mišpāḥâ* → *mišpāḥôṯ* "clans";
- dual **-ayim**, on either gender: *yāḏ* → *yāḏayim* "(both) hands".

The endings are old. Gesenius lists the relatives of *-îm*: Phoenician *-îm*, Aramaic *-în*, Arabic *-ūna*/*-īna* (§87d). The feminine *-ôṯ* stands for an older *-āṯ*, the form it still has in Arabic, Aramaic and Akkadian. Hebrew turned that *ā* into *ô* (§87l, §9q).

The word "mostly" matters. The endings are tied to gender only loosely. Some masculine nouns take *-ôṯ* (*ʾāḇ* → *ʾāḇôṯ* "fathers") and some feminine nouns take *-îm* (*šānâ* → *šānîm* "years"). The noun keeps the gender of its singular, whatever its ending. Gesenius says that a strict split between the two endings is found "only in adjectives and participles" (§87r). So the adjective tells you the gender, not the noun.

The dual is the remains of a wider system. Arabic still has duals in nouns, pronouns and verbs; Hebrew has kept the dual only in nouns, and almost only for pairs (§88a, g). Adjectives, verbs and pronouns have no dual. With a dual noun they use the plural.

## How it is built

### Step 1: choose the ending

1. **A masculine noun:** add stressed *-îm*. *sûs* → *sûsîm*. A word ending in *-eh* drops it: *śāḏeh* "field" → *śāḏôṯ* (this word takes *-ôṯ*), *ḥōzeh* "seer" → *ḥōzîm* (§87b). A word in *-î* usually contracts: *ʿiḇrî* → *ʿiḇrîm* "Hebrews" (§87a).
2. **A feminine noun in *-â*:** replace the *-â* with *-ôṯ*. *mišpāḥâ* → *mišpāḥôṯ*. **In *-eṯ*:** replace it too: *ʾiggereṯ* "letter" → *ʾiggərôṯ* (§87i). **A feminine without an ending:** add *-ôṯ*: *bəʾēr* → *bəʾērôṯ* "wells".
3. **A dual:** add *-ayim*. A feminine adds it to the old *-aṯ*, which becomes *-āṯ-*: *śāp̄â* "lip" → *śəp̄āṯayim* "both lips", *šānâ* → *šənāṯayim* "two years", *ʾammâ* "cubit" → *ʾammāṯayim* "two cubits" (§88a).

The *-ôṯ* is often written without its *w*: קֹלֹת for *qōlôṯ*. The transliteration then shows *ō*: *qōlōṯ*. It is the same ending. The same goes for *-îm* written ־ִם (§87a).

### Step 2: let the vowels shift

Both plural endings are stressed, and the dual's stress falls on its first syllable, *-ā́yim*. So the stress moves forward, off the noun's own vowels, and those vowels react. Gesenius explains it with *kānāp̄* "wing" → *kənāp̄ayim* "two wings" (§88b):

- the vowel just before the new stress stays long or lengthens: *-nā-*;
- the vowel two places before it, now far from the stress, is reduced to shewa: *kā-* → *kə-*.

The same happens in *dāḇār* → *dəḇārîm* "words" and *zāqēn* → *zəqēnîm* "elders". Lesson 3.3 sets out which vowels change and which never do. Three other things you will see now:

- Some nouns double their last consonant before an ending: *yām* "sea" → *yammîm*, *ʿēz* → *ʿizzîm*. The doubling was always there in the root and comes back once a vowel follows (lesson 3.3).
- Nouns of the *meleḵ* type rebuild their shape: *meleḵ* → *məlāḵîm*, but *reḡel* → *raḡlayim* "both feet" (lesson 3.4).
- A few common nouns have irregular plurals: *ʾîš* → *ʾănāšîm*, *ʾiššâ* → *nāšîm*, *yôm* → *yāmîm*, *ʿîr* → *ʿārîm*, *bayiṯ* → *bāttîm* (lesson 3.12).

### Step 3: make the rest agree

The adjective follows the noun's **gender**, not its ending, and uses the plural for a dual. To say "the great lights":

1. The noun *māʾôr* "light, lamp" is masculine (BDB "n.m."); its plural is *məʾōrôṯ*.
2. The adjective must be masculine plural: *gəḏōlîm*.
3. Both take the article (lesson 2.2): *hamməʾōrōṯ haggəḏōlîm*. The endings do not match, and that is correct.

### Plurals that mean one thing

Some nouns exist only in the plural, or use the plural for a single thing. Gesenius calls one group "plurals of extension", things spread over a surface: *mayim* "water", *šāmayim* "sky", *pānîm* "face" (§124b). Another is the abstract plural, as in *ḥayyîm* "life" (§124d). Their adjectives and verbs are plural.

*mayim* and *šāmayim* look like duals but are not. Gesenius takes them as plurals of old singulars *may*, *šamay*, which kept their stress on the *ay* (§88d).

The best-known case is *ʾĕlōhîm*. Meaning the God of Israel, it is singular in sense, and Gesenius calls it a "plural of majesty" (§124g). Its verb is singular: *bārāʾ ʾĕlōhîm* "God created" (lesson 2.6). Its adjective is "as a rule" singular too, but now and then plural (§132h; example 10). Meaning "gods", it is a true plural.

### Singulars that mean many

The opposite also exists. A **collective** noun is singular in form but names a group: *ṣōʾn* "flock, sheep", *bāqār* "cattle", *ʾāḏām* "humankind". Gesenius notes that *ʾāḏām* has no plural at all (§87t).

## Where it applies

- **Ordinary plurals** of countable things: *dəḇārîm*, *ʿārîm*, *qōlôṯ*.
- **Both endings on one noun.** Some nouns have two plurals with the same gender, such as *dôr* "generation", *dôrîm* and *dôrôṯ* (§87m).
- **Different endings, different senses.** With body parts the dual is the living pair, and the plural in *-ôṯ* something shaped like them: *yāḏayim* "hands", *yāḏôṯ* "arm-rests, handles"; *qarnayim* "horns" of an animal, *qərānôṯ* "horns" of the altar (§87o).
- **Male and female of one stem.** Where a noun has a feminine pair, the endings do follow sex: *bānîm* "sons", *bānôṯ* "daughters" (§87r).
- **The dual for pairs of the body:** *yāḏayim*, *ʿênayim*, *ʾoznayim* "ears", *raḡlayim*, *birkayim* "knees", *kənāp̄ayim* "wings", *šinnayim* "teeth".
- **The dual for paired objects:** *naʿălayim* "a pair of sandals", *moʾznayim* "scales" (§88e).
- **The dual for two units of time or measure:** *yômayim*, *šənāṯayim*, *ʾammāṯayim*. Also in numbers: *māʾṯayim* 200 (part 5).
- **The dual for more than two pairs.** A paired noun stays dual with a number above two: Gesenius cites *ʾarbaʿ raḡlayim* "four feet" (Leviticus 11:23) and *šēš kənāp̄ayim* "six wings" (Isaiah 6:2) (§88f).
- **Plurals of extension and abstraction:** *mayim*, *šāmayim*, *pānîm*, *ḥayyîm* (§124).
- **The plural of majesty:** *ʾĕlōhîm*, and also *ʾăḏōnîm* "lord" for a single master (§124i). You will meet *ʾăḏōnê hāʾāreṣ* "the lord of the land" for one man in Genesis 42:30.
- **Agreement.** Adjectives use the plural for a dual noun, in the noun's gender: Genesis 29:17 has *ʿênê lēʾâ rakkôṯ* "Leah's eyes were weak", with feminine plural *-ôṯ* (lesson 3.6).

## Watch out

**Do not read gender off the plural ending.** *\*hamməʾōrōṯ haggəḏōlōṯ* looks consistent, but *māʾôr* is masculine: *hamməʾōrōṯ haggəḏōlîm*. And *ʿārîm* is feminine: *ʿārîm gəḏōlōṯ*, not *\*ʿārîm gəḏōlîm*. Check the singular.

**There is no dual adjective or verb.** *\*yāḏayim gəḏōlayim* is impossible. Use the plural of the right gender.

**Not everything paired is dual.** "Arms" is *zərōʿîm* or *zərōʿôṯ*, never dual (§88e). And "two" with an ordinary noun is a number, not the dual: "two kings" is not *\*malkayim* (part 5).

***mayim* and *šāmayim* are not duals.** Do not translate *šāmayim* as "two heavens". They are plurals, so their adjectives and verbs are plural (§88d).

**A plural form is not always a plural meaning.** *ʾĕlōhîm bārāʾ*, with a singular verb, means "God created", not "gods". The verb shows which is meant (§124g). *pānîm* is one face.

**A singular form is not always singular.** *ṣōʾn* is a whole flock; it often takes a plural verb. Spanish *la gente* and French *le bétail* work the same way.

**Do not add *-îm* to a word with an *-eh*.** *\*śāḏehîm* is wrong; the *-eh* drops (§87b).

**The vowels do not stay put.** *\*dāḇārîm* keeps the first vowel where Hebrew reduces it: the form is *dəḇārîm*. Lesson 3.3 gives the rule.

## Connections

Arabic has the same three numbers and similar endings: masculine plural *-ūna*/*-īna* (*muʿallimūna* "teachers"), feminine plural *-āt* (*muʿallimāt*), and the dual *-āni*/*-ayni* (*yadāni* "two hands"). Hebrew *-ôṯ* is the *-āt* after the shift of *ā* to *ô*. Arabic also uses broken plurals (*kitāb*, *kutub*), which Hebrew does not; its plurals are all made with endings.

## Exercises

1. Give the plurals of *sûs*, *mišpāḥâ* and *bəʾēr*, and the dual of *yāḏ*.
2. *qôl* takes *-ôṯ*. What gender is *qōlôṯ*, and what adjective ending would go with it?
3. Translate *ʿārîm gəḏōlōṯ wəṭōḇōṯ*, and say why the adjectives end in *-ôṯ* although the noun ends in *-îm*.
4. Build "the great stars", using *kôḵāḇ* and *gāḏôl*.
5. Why is *kānāp̄* "wing" *kənāp̄ayim* in the dual, with a different first vowel?
6. A student translates *šāmayim* as "the two heavens". What is wrong?
7. Build "two years" and "two cubits".
8. In *bārāʾ ʾĕlōhîm*, the noun is plural and the verb singular. Explain.
9. Find and fix the error: *\*hayyāḏayim gəḏōlayim* "the big hands".

## Worked answers

1. *sûsîm*; *mišpāḥôṯ* (the *-â* is replaced); *bəʾērôṯ* (feminine without an ending, so *-ôṯ* is added); *yāḏayim* (Gesenius §§87–88).
2. Masculine, because the plural keeps the gender of the singular (§87p). An adjective would take *-îm*.
3. "Great and good cities." *ʿîr* is feminine, and adjectives follow the noun's gender, not its ending (§87r).
4. *hakkôḵāḇîm haggəḏōlîm*. *kôḵāḇ* is masculine with *-îm*; the adjective is masculine plural, and both take the article. Its *ô* is written with *w* and does not reduce (lesson 3.3).
5. The stress moves to the ending. The vowel just before it stays long, and the vowel two places before it reduces to shewa (Gesenius §88b).
6. *šāmayim* is not a dual but a plural, probably of an old singular *šamay* (§88d). "The heavens" or "the sky".
7. *šənāṯayim*, *ʾammāṯayim*. Feminine nouns add the dual to the old *-aṯ* (§88a).
8. *ʾĕlōhîm* is a plural of majesty: plural in form, singular in meaning when it refers to the God of Israel, so the verb is singular (§124g).
9. *hayyāḏayim haggəḏōlôṯ*. There is no dual adjective; *yāḏ* is feminine, so the adjective is feminine plural, and it takes the article because the noun has it (§88a).
