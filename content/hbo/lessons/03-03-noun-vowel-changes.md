## The idea

In lesson 3.2 *dāḇār* became *dəḇārîm*, but *sûs* stayed *sûsîm*. *zāqēn* "old" became *zəqēnîm*, *ʿam* "people" became *ʿammîm*. These are not separate irregularities. They come from one fact: **a Hebrew vowel's length depends on where the stress falls.**

Lesson 1.3 showed that the Tiberian pointing marks vowel quality, and that its length followed stress and syllable shape (Khan 2020, I.5). Gesenius describes the same thing historically (§27c). Many vowels in a noun were short to begin with. They became long only because they stood under the stress or in the open syllable just before it. He calls them **tone-long**: "tone" is his word for stress. When an ending pulls the stress forward, a tone-long vowel loses its support. It goes back to being short, or shrinks to a shewa.

Other vowels were long from the start, or are protected by the shape of their syllable. These never change. Gesenius calls the first kind **changeable** and the second **unchangeable** (§92b). The skill this lesson teaches is to tell them apart at a glance.

Arabic shows the short vowels that Hebrew lengthened. Gesenius's examples: Hebrew *māṭār* "rain" is Arabic *maṭar*; *ʿāqēḇ* "heel" is Arabic *ʿaqib* (§27c). The Hebrew *ā* and *ē* are the Arabic *a* and *i* stretched under the stress.

## How it is built

### Which vowels are changeable

A vowel is **changeable** when it is a qameṣ or ṣere (school "long" *ā*, *ē*) standing in an **open syllable**, written without a vowel letter. These are the tone-long vowels. *dā-ḇār*, *zā-qēn*, *kā-nāp̄*, *ḥā-ḵām*.

A vowel is **unchangeable** in four cases:

1. **It is written with a vowel letter:** *ô*, *û*, *î*, *ê*. *sûs* → *sûsîm*, *kôḵāḇ* → *kôḵāḇîm*. Gesenius's examples are *pāqîḏ* "overseer" → *pəqîḏîm* and *ʿôlām* → *ʿôlāmîm* (§92b).
2. **It is the vowel *ō* of the participle's first syllable:** *šōp̄ēṭ* "judge" → *šōp̄əṭîm*. This *ō* was long from the start.
3. **It stands in a closed syllable:** *miš-pāṭ* → *mišpāṭîm*. The *i* of *miš-* never changes.
4. **It stands before a doubled consonant:** *gib-bôr*, *ṣad-dîq*. Doubling closes the syllable.

### The rule when the stress moves one place

The plural endings *-îm* and *-ôṯ*, the dual *-ayim*, and most of the suffixes of lessons 3.7–3.8 move the stress forward by one syllable. Then, in a two-syllable noun (§92b):

- the vowel **just before the new stress** keeps or gains length. It is now the "pretonic" vowel, and a pretonic open syllable favours a long vowel (§26e);
- a changeable vowel **two places before the stress** reduces to shewa.

Work through *zāqēn* + *-îm*:

1. The stress was on *-qḗn*. Now it is on *-nî́m*.
2. *-qē-* is now just before the stress, so it stays: *-qē-*.
3. *zā-* is two places before it. Its qameṣ is changeable, so it becomes shewa: *zə-*.
4. Result: *zəqēnîm*.

Under a guttural the shewa takes the hatef form, because gutturals prefer a coloured vowel (lesson 1.5): *ḥāḵām* → *ḥăḵāmîm*, *ḥāṣēr* "courtyard" → *ḥăṣērôṯ*; with a suffix, *ʿānān* "cloud" → *ʿănānô* "his cloud".

### The participle type is different

In nouns of the participle shape *qōṭēl*, it is the **second** vowel that goes: *šōp̄ēṭ* → *šōp̄əṭîm*, *ʾōyēḇ* "enemy" → *ʾōyəḇîm* (§92c). The *ō* is unchangeable, so it holds; the *ē*, now right before the stress, is reduced instead. Gesenius records this as a fact of the type; the guide does not try to explain it further.

### Doubling that comes back

Some short nouns end in a consonant that the root has twice. At the end of a word a doubled consonant cannot be written, so *ʿam* shows one *m*. Once an ending follows, the doubling returns, and the vowel before it, now in a closed syllable, is short: *ʿam* → *ʿammîm*, *yām* → *yammîm*, *ḥēṣ* "arrow" → *ḥiṣṣîm*, *ḥōq* "statute" → *ḥuqqîm*, *ʾēm* → *ʾimmî* "my mother" (§27d). The vowel can change quality too: *ē* becomes *i*, *ō* becomes *u*. Gesenius explains that the long *ē* and *ō* were short *i* and *u* lengthened under the stress; without it they return (§27d).

### When the stress moves two places

The construct plural of lesson 3.6, and some suffixes, move the stress two places, or onto the next word. Then the vowel that had stayed long reduces as well, and the first vowel, which had become shewa, comes back short, usually as *i*. So *dəḇārîm* "words" but *diḇrê hāʿām* "the words of the people" (§92d). You will build these in lessons 3.6 and 3.7. Here, notice that they follow the same principle: a vowel without the stress near it shrinks.

### One-syllable words

A one-syllable word with a changeable vowel keeps it before an ending, because the vowel is now pretonic: *yāḏ* → *yāḏayim*, *dām* "blood" → *dāmîm*. But if the word loses its own stress by being joined to the next word, the vowel shortens: *yāḏ* "hand", *yaḏ-YHWH* "the hand of the LORD"; *bēn* "son", *ben-hammeleḵ* "the son of the king" (§27d). This is the construct state, lesson 3.5.

### Building a plural from scratch

To make "rivers" from *nāhār*:

1. Choose the ending. BDB gives *nəhārôṯ* (and *nəhārîm*), so the masculine noun takes *-ôṯ*.
2. The stress moves to *-rṓṯ*.
3. *-hā-*, just before it, stays.
4. *nā-*, two places before, is changeable: it becomes shewa. Result: *nəhārôṯ*.

## Where it applies

- **Every plural and dual ending:** *dəḇārîm*, *zəqēnîm*, *kənāp̄ayim* (lesson 3.2).
- **The feminine ending *-â*,** which is also stressed: *gāḏôl* → *gəḏôlâ*, *ḥāḵām* → *ḥăḵāmâ* (lesson 3.1). The same steps apply.
- **Pronoun suffixes** (lessons 3.7–3.9): *dāḇār* → *dəḇārî* "my word", but *ʾōyēḇ* → *ʾōyiḇḵā* "your enemy", where the second vowel reappears short (§27d).
- **The construct state** (lessons 3.5–3.6): *māṭār* → *məṭar* "rain of", *dəḇārîm* → *diḇrê*.
- **Adjectives** follow exactly the same rules as nouns: *yāšār* → *yəšārîm*, *ḥāḏāš* → *ḥăḏāšîm*.
- **Verbs** obey the same principle. Lesson 2.6 showed *šāmar* → *šāmərû*. The verb lessons of part 4 rely on it.
- **Pause** works in reverse: under the major break the stress is strong and a reduced vowel can return (lesson 1.8).

## Watch out

**Do not reduce an unchangeable vowel.** By analogy with *dəḇārîm* you might write *\*səsîm* for "horses" or *\*gəbbôrîm*. *sûs* has a vowel letter and *gibbôr* a doubled consonant: *sûsîm*, *gibbôrîm*.

**Do not reduce the wrong vowel.** With *dāḇār* it is the first that goes. With a participle like *šōp̄ēṭ* it is the second: *šōp̄əṭîm*, not *\*šəp̄ēṭîm*.

**Do not forget the doubling.** *\*ʿamîm* and *\*ḥōqîm* are wrong; the root's doubled letter returns: *ʿammîm*, *ḥuqqîm*. A single short syllable with pataḥ, ṣere or ḥolem at the end of a noun is a sign that this may happen. BDB lists such nouns under a root with a repeated letter (*ʿ-m-m*, *ḥ-q-q*).

**Do not put a bare shewa under a guttural.** *\*ḥəḵāmîm* is impossible; write *ḥăḵāmîm* (lesson 1.5).

**Plene and defective spelling do not change the rule.** *gibbōrîm* written without *w* (example 5) is still *gibbôr* with an unchangeable vowel. Decide by the vowel's history, not by its spelling in one verse. When unsure, look up the noun's plural in BDB, which lists it.

**A qameṣ is not always tone-long.** In *ḥoḵmâ* "wisdom" the qameṣ is qameṣ ḥaṭuf, a short *o* in a closed syllable (lesson 1.6). It does not reduce.

**Spanish and French stress is not a guide.** In Spanish *pared* → *paredes* the stress stays on the same syllable and nothing shrinks. In Hebrew the stress always moves to the ending, and the noun pays for it.

## Connections

Arabic keeps the short vowels that Hebrew lengthened under the stress: *maṭar*/*māṭār*, *ʿaqib*/*ʿāqēḇ* (Gesenius §27c), and *dabar* is Gesenius's reconstructed ground form of *dāḇār* (§92b). An Arabic speaker sees that Hebrew *ā* in an open syllable is often an Arabic *a*, and that is exactly the *ā* that reduces.

## Exercises

1. Give the plural of *zāqēn* and explain each step.
2. Why is the plural of *sûs* not *\*səsîm*?
3. Make the plurals of *ḥāḏāš* and *yāšār*.
4. Make the plural of *ʾōyēḇ*, and say why it differs in pattern from *zāqēn*.
5. Make the plurals of *ʿam* and *ḥēṣ*. What returns, and why?
6. Is the first vowel of *mišpāṭ* changeable? Give the plural.
7. A student writes *\*ḥəḵāmîm*. Correct it.
8. Translate *šəmaʿ ʾel-haḥuqqîm wəʾel-hammišpāṭîm*, and give the singular of each plural noun.

## Worked answers

1. *zəqēnîm*. The stress moves to *-îm*. *-qē-* is now pretonic and holds; *zā-*, two places away and changeable, reduces to shewa (Gesenius §92b).
2. Its *û* is written with a vowel letter and is unchangeable. The plural is *sûsîm*.
3. *ḥăḏāšîm*: the first vowel reduces, and under the guttural ḥ the shewa becomes ḥaṭep̄ pataḥ. *yəšārîm*: first vowel reduces.
4. *ʾōyəḇîm*. The *ō* of the participle type is unchangeable, so the second vowel, *ē*, is the one reduced (§92c). In *zāqēn* the first vowel is changeable and goes.
5. *ʿammîm*, *ḥiṣṣîm*. Both roots end in a doubled consonant (BDB lists them under *ʿ-m-m* and *ḥ-ṣ-ṣ*); the doubling returns once a vowel follows, and the vowel before it is short (§27d). *ē* becomes *i*.
6. No. *miš-* is a closed syllable with a short vowel. *mišpāṭîm*: *-pā-* is now pretonic and stays.
7. *ḥăḵāmîm*. A guttural takes a hatef vowel instead of vocal shewa (lesson 1.5).
8. "Listen to the statutes and the judgements." *ḥuqqîm* is from *ḥōq*, with its doubled *q* restored; *mišpāṭîm* is from *mišpāṭ*, unchanged except for the ending.
