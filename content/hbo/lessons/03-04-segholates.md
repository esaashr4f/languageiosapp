## The idea

Many of the commonest nouns look alike: *meleḵ* "king", *dereḵ* "way", *ʾereṣ* "land", *ḥereḇ* "sword", *nep̄eš* "life", *sēp̄er* "book", *qōḏeš* "holiness", *naʿar* "boy". Two syllables, the stress on the first, and a segol or pataḥ in the second. Lesson 1.8 called them **segholates**, after the segol.

They look like two-syllable words, but they are not built like *dāḇār*. Their second vowel is not part of the word at all. Gesenius explains that each segholate goes back to a base with **one vowel**: *malk*, *sipr*, *qudš* (§93c, §84a). Hebrew does not, as a rule, end a word in two consonants. So a **helping vowel**, usually segol, was slipped in between them to make the word pronounceable (§84a). The first vowel then took the stress and changed shape: *malk* → *meleḵ*, *sipr* → *sēp̄er*, *qudš* → *qōḏeš*.

That one fact explains every form of these nouns. Whenever an ending begins with a vowel, the cluster is no longer at the end of the word. The helping vowel is not needed, it disappears, and the old base shows through: *malk-î* "my king", *qoḏš-î* "my holiness". The plural is the one surprise. It is built as if the noun had two full vowels: *məlāḵîm*. Lesson 3.3 has prepared you for that.

Gesenius adds a caution. Many segholates may go back to two-syllable bases with a short vowel in each, as Arabic *malik* "king" suggests. But the helping vowel of *meleḵ* is not a trace of that old vowel; it is new (§84a). What matters for reading is the one-vowel base.

## How it is built

### The three classes

Each segholate belongs to one of three classes, by the vowel of its base (§93c):

- **a-class**, base *qaṭl*: *meleḵ* (*malk*), *kerem* "vineyard" (*karm*), *nep̄eš* (*napš*). The base *a* appears as segol in the plain form.
- **i-class**, base *qiṭl*: *sēp̄er* (*sipr*), *qeḇer* "grave" (*qibr*), *beḡeḏ* "garment" (*bigd*). The plain form has ṣere or segol.
- **u-class**, base *quṭl*: *qōḏeš* (*qudš*), *ʾōhel* "tent", *bōqer* "morning". The plain form has ḥolem; the base vowel appears as *o* (qameṣ ḥaṭuf).

### The four forms you need

1. **Plain singular:** stress on the first syllable, helping vowel in the second: *méleḵ*, *sḗp̄er*, *qṓḏeš*. The bound form of lesson 3.5 is the same: *sēp̄er habbərîṯ* "the book of the covenant" (§93c).
2. **Singular with a suffix:** the base, with its own short vowel, and no helping vowel: *malkî* "my king", *karməḵā* "your vineyard", *qiḇrô* "his grave", *biḡḏô* "his garment", *qoḏšî* "my holiness" (§93c). The suffixes are lesson 3.7.
3. **Plural:** shewa under the first consonant, qameṣ before the ending: *məlāḵîm*, *səp̄ārîm*, *qoḏāšîm*. Gesenius's explanation is that the plurals of nouns that really had two *a* vowels became the model for all segholates (§84a). In this lesson you treat it as the rule: *Cə-Cā-C-îm*. Feminine segholates take *-ôṯ*: *ḥereḇ* → *ḥărāḇôṯ* (§93a).
4. **Bound plural, "the kings of":** the base again, with *-ê*: *malḵê*, *qoḏšê*, *biḡḏê* (§93d). The *k* of *malḵê* is soft, without dagesh, because a vowel once stood before it (§93d). Lesson 3.6 builds these.

The dual is usually added to the base, like a suffix: *reḡel* → *raḡlayim*, *bereḵ* → *birkayim*, *qeren* → *qarnayim* (§88b).

### With a guttural

A guttural as second or third consonant takes **pataḥ** as its helping vowel instead of segol (§84a): *naʿar*, *zeraʿ* "seed", *šaʿar* "gate". Some keep segol: *leḥem*, *ʾōhel* (§84a). In the plural and with suffixes the guttural takes a hatef vowel where a shewa would stand: *naʿar* → *nəʿārîm* "young men" (§93c).

### With a weak middle letter

When the middle consonant is *w* or *y*, the base is *mawt*, *zayt*, *bayt*. In the plain form the *w* or *y* stays a consonant, and the helping vowel appears as ḥireq: *māweṯ* "death", *zayiṯ* "olive", *bayiṯ* "house". Everywhere else the *aw* and *ay* contract to *ô* and *ê* (§93e): *môṯ ʾaḇrāhām* "the death of Abraham", *bêṯ ʾĕlōhîm* "the house of God", *zêṯîm* "olives", *bêṯî* "my house". *ʿayin* "eye" works the same way: *ʿênayim* "eyes".

### Building the forms of a new segholate

Take *kerem* "vineyard". BDB lists *karmî* "my vineyard" and the plural *kərāmîm*.

1. The suffixed form shows the base: *karm-*, so it is a-class.
2. "Your vineyard" (to a man): *karm-* + *-əḵā* = *karməḵā*.
3. "Vineyards": shewa, qameṣ, ending: *kərāmîm*.
4. "The vineyards of": base + *-ê* = *karmê*.

You need the suffixed form to know the class. The plain form alone can mislead (see Watch out).

## Where it applies

- **The plain form,** wherever the noun stands alone: subject, object, after a preposition, with the article (*hammeleḵ*, *haḥereḇ*).
- **The bound singular,** "king of", "book of": same as the plain form (example 1).
- **Every suffix** of lesson 3.7, and **the dual**: the base (*malkî*, *raḡlayim*).
- **The plural** with *-îm* or *-ôṯ*: *Cə-Cā-C-* (*məlāḵîm*, *nəʿārîm*, *ḥărāḇôṯ*).
- **The bound plural** and the "heavy" suffixes *-ḵem*, *-hem* on the plural (lessons 3.6, 3.8): the base again (*malḵê*, *malḵêhem* "their kings").
- **Feminine segholates.** *malkâ* "queen" is the base *malk* + *-â*; this is why it has *a* where *meleḵ* has segol. Nouns in *-eṯ* (*gəḇereṯ*, *kəṯōneṯ*) "follow in every respect the analogy of the segholate forms" (§80d): *gəḇirtî* "my mistress", built on *gəḇirt-* (lesson 3.1).
- **Many feminine nouns without an ending** are segholates: *ʾereṣ*, *ḥereḇ*, *nep̄eš*, *reḡel*, *ʾeḇen*. They inflect exactly like *meleḵ*, but take *-ôṯ* in the plural (§93a).
- **Verbs.** The same base shape returns in some verb forms of part 4; the guide will point it out.

## Watch out

**The plain form does not show the class.** *kerem* and *beḡeḏ* have the same vowels, but "your vineyard" is *karməḵā* and "his garment" is *biḡḏô*. Writing *\*birməḵā* or *\*baḡḏô* applies the wrong class. Learn the suffixed form with the word; BDB gives it.

**Do not keep the helping vowel before an ending.** *\*meleḵî* for "my king" is wrong; the segol was only there to break up the cluster: *malkî*.

**Do not build the plural on the base.** *\*malkîm* looks logical from *malkî*, but the plural is *məlāḵîm*. The base appears with suffixes and in the bound plural, not in the plain plural.

**Do not stress the last syllable.** Read *méleḵ*, not *\*meléḵ*. If you hear the second vowel as a real vowel, you will expect it to behave like the *ā* of *dāḇār*. It will not.

**Do not mistake *bayiṯ*, *māweṯ* for words with three vowels.** The ḥireq of *bayiṯ* is the helping vowel; the *y* is the middle root letter. Outside the plain form, read *bêṯ*, *bêṯî*, never *\*bayṯî*. The plural of *bayiṯ* is irregular, *bāttîm* (lesson 3.12).

**The segol in *ʾereṣ* is not always there.** After the article and in pause the word is *ʾāreṣ* (lesson 2.2). That is the base vowel *a* lengthened, as Gesenius notes for pause (§29k). The suffixed form shows the same *a*: *ʾarṣî* "my land".

**Spanish and French offer no parallel.** Spanish *mar*, *mares* adds an ending and keeps the stress pattern; nothing in the stem disappears. In a segholate a whole vowel comes and goes. Think rather of Arabic, where *malik* and *mulūk* reshape the inside of the word.

## Connections

Arabic keeps the two kinds of plural that Hebrew segholates hint at. *malik* "king" has the broken plural *mulūk*, made by reshaping the inside of the word; Hebrew *məlāḵîm* uses an ending but reshapes the inside too. The Arabic words *bayt* "house", *mawt* "death" and *zayt* "oil" match Gesenius's bases *bayt*, *mawt*, *zayt* for *bayiṯ*, *māweṯ*, *zayiṯ* (§93e).

## Exercises

1. Give the base of *meleḵ*, *sēp̄er* and *qōḏeš*, and say why the plain forms have a second vowel.
2. Build "my king" and "kings".
3. "His garment" is *biḡḏô*. Build "garments" and "the garments of".
4. Why does *naʿar* have pataḥ where *meleḵ* has segol?
5. Translate *ʾaḥărê môṯ ʾaḇrāhām* and explain *môṯ*.
6. A student writes *\*meleḵî* for "my king" and *\*malkîm* for "kings". Correct both.
7. Translate *ʾên zeh kî ʾim-bêṯ ʾĕlōhîm*, and give the plain form of *bêṯ*.
8. *kerem* and *beḡeḏ* look alike. How do you find out which class each belongs to?

## Worked answers

1. *malk*, *sipr*, *qudš* (Gesenius §93c). Hebrew does not end a word in two consonants, so a helping vowel was inserted, and the stressed first vowel took the shapes *e*, *ē*, *ō* (§84a).
2. *malkî*: the suffix begins with a vowel, so the base shows through and the helping vowel goes. *məlāḵîm*: shewa, qameṣ, ending (§93c–d).
3. *bəḡāḏîm*, the regular segholate plural. *biḡḏê*, the base with *-ê*; the *ḡ* is soft because a vowel once stood before it.
4. The guttural ʿ prefers *a*, so the helping vowel is pataḥ, and the first vowel stays *a* too (§84a).
5. "After the death of Abraham." *māweṯ* has a weak middle *w*. Outside the plain form, *aw* contracts to *ô* (§93e).
6. *malkî* and *məlāḵîm*. The helping vowel never appears before an ending; the plain plural is not built on the base (§93c–d).
7. "This is none other than the house of God." The plain form is *bayiṯ*; *ay* contracts to *ê* (§93e).
8. Look at a suffixed form, in the text or in BDB: *karmî* shows an a-class base, *biḡḏô* an i-class base.
