## The idea

English links two nouns with "of" or with *'s*: "the word of the LORD", "the king's house". Spanish and French use *de*. Hebrew has no word for "of". It puts the two nouns side by side, the owned thing first and the owner second, and pronounces them as one unit: *dəḇar YHWH* "the word of the LORD", *bêṯ hammeleḵ* "the house of the king".

The first noun is said to be in the **construct state**, glossed CSTR: a bound form that leans on the noun after it. The second is in its ordinary form, the **absolute state**. Grammars call the pair a **construct chain**.

The construct is not marked by an ending. It is marked by what happens to the first noun's vowels. Gesenius explains the mechanism (§89a). The two nouns are said so closely together that the stress of the first is "forced on to" the second. The first noun loses its own stress. Its tone-long vowels, which needed that stress, shorten or reduce, exactly as in lesson 3.3. So *dāḇār* "word" becomes *dəḇar* "word of", and *yāḏ* "hand" becomes *yaḏ* "hand of". Gesenius concludes that the construct is in origin "phonetic and rhythmical", not a grammatical ending (§89a). He compares English compounds such as "landlord", in which two words share one stress.

Often the two words are joined by maqqef: *bêṯ-YHWH*. That is a matter of the accents, not a rule; the chain is the same without it (§89b).

Old Semitic marked "of" with a case ending on the second noun, as Arabic does: *baytu l-maliki*. Hebrew lost its case endings (§89a). The word order and the bound form of the first noun carry the meaning alone.

## How it is built

### The steps

To say "the house of the king":

1. **The thing possessed comes first.** *bayiṯ* "house".
2. **Put it in the construct state.** *bayiṯ* is a segholate with a middle *y*, so its construct is *bêṯ* (lesson 3.4).
3. **The possessor comes second, in its normal form.** *meleḵ*.
4. **Decide definiteness on the second noun only.** "The king": *hammeleḵ*.
5. Result: *bêṯ hammeleḵ*. Because the second noun is definite, the whole chain is: "*the* house of the king". Nothing goes on the first noun.

### How the first noun changes

Apply lesson 3.3 with the stress gone from the first noun.

- **Two changeable vowels:** the first reduces, the second shortens. *dāḇār* → *dəḇar*, *zāqēn* → *zəqan* "elder of", *ḥāḵām* → *ḥăḵam* (§93, paradigm II).
- **A changeable vowel before an unchangeable one:** only the first reduces. *māqôm* → *məqôm* "place of".
- **Nouns in *-eh*:** the ending becomes *-ēh*. *śāḏeh* → *śəḏēh* "field of", *maḥăneh* → *maḥănēh* "camp of".
- **One-syllable words with a long vowel in a closed syllable:** the vowel shortens. *yāḏ* → *yaḏ*, *bēn* → *ben*, *kōl* → *kol* (§27d).
- **Segholates:** no change. *ʾereṣ*, *sēp̄er*, *meleḵ* are their own construct (§93c). Those with a weak middle contract: *bayiṯ* → *bêṯ*, *māweṯ* → *môṯ*.
- **Unchangeable vowels:** no change. *sûs*, *qôl*, *ʾîš*.
- **Feminine nouns in *-â*** take *-aṯ*: *šip̄ḥâ* → *šip̄ḥaṯ* (lesson 3.1). Lesson 3.6 covers these, with the construct plural.

A few common nouns have irregular constructs: *ʾāḇ* → *ʾăḇî* "father of", *ʾāḥ* → *ʾăḥî*, *peh* → *pî* "mouth of" (lesson 3.12).

### Definiteness

The chain takes its definiteness from its last noun (§127a). That noun is definite if it has the article, is a name, or has a possessive suffix:

- *bêṯ hammeleḵ* "the house of the king": article;
- *dəḇar YHWH* "the word of the LORD": name;
- *zəqan bêṯô* "the elder of his house": suffix (lesson 3.7).

If the last noun is indefinite, so is the chain: *ʾîš milḥāmâ* "a man of war", but *ʾîš hammilḥāmâ* "the man of war" (Gesenius's pair, §127a). A definite chain used as object takes *ʾēṯ* (lesson 2.9): *ʾeṯ-bêṯ hammeleḵ*.

## Where it applies

- **Possession:** *bêṯ hammeleḵ*, *zəqan bêṯô*.
- **A name that says which one:** *ʾereṣ kənaʿan*, *śəḏēh ʾĕḏôm*. Gesenius calls this an explanatory genitive.
- **Material:** *nezem zāhāḇ* "a gold ring".
- **A quality:** *gibbôr ḥayil* "a man of valour", *ʾîš milḥāmâ* "a warrior". Hebrew has few adjectives, so this is a very common way of describing (Gesenius §128p).
- **Age:** *ben-šānâ* "a year old"; *ben-ʾāḏām* "a son of man", that is, a human being.
- **Belonging to a group:** *bənê yiśrāʾēl* "the Israelites" (plural construct, lesson 3.6).
- **The whole or a part:** *kol-hāʿām* "all the people", *kol-hāʾāreṣ* "the whole earth".
- **The object of an action named by the first noun:** *yirʾaṯ YHWH* "the fear of the LORD", the fear one has towards him (lesson 3.6).
- **"Most":** *qōḏeš qoḏāšîm* "most holy" (lesson 3.4; lesson 5.5).
- **With a suffix instead of a second noun:** every possessive suffix of lesson 3.7 is attached to the noun much as a second noun would be. Gesenius treats the suffix as a definite genitive (§127a).
- **Later in the guide:** chains of three or more nouns and the construct plural (lesson 3.6); a construct before a whole clause (part 6).

## Watch out

**Never put the article on the first noun.** By analogy with "*the* house of *the* king" or *la casa del rey*, you might write *\*habbêṯ hammeleḵ*. Hebrew puts the article only on the last noun; the chain is then definite as a whole (§127a).

**Do not write "of" with *min* or *lə-*.** Spanish *de* and French *de* tempt you to write *\*bayiṯ min-hammeleḵ*. *min* means "from". Use the chain. *lə-* is used for "of" only in the special case below.

**"A son of Jesse" is not *ben-yišay*.** A name makes the chain definite, so *ben-yišay* means "the son of Jesse". For "a son of" or "one of the sons of", keep the first noun absolute and add *lə-*: *bēn ləyišay* (§129c).

**Nothing comes between the two nouns.** An adjective describing the first noun cannot follow it directly: *\*dəḇar haṭṭôḇ YHWH* is impossible. The adjective goes after the whole chain (lessons 3.6 and 3.11).

**Two nouns cannot share one possessor.** For "the sons and daughters of David" you cannot write *\*bənê ûḇənôṯ dāwiḏ*. Hebrew says "the sons of David and his daughters" (§128a), repeating the owner as a suffix.

**Do not use the absolute form in first position.** *\*dāḇār YHWH* is two separate nouns, not a chain. Where the construct differs, use it: *dəḇar YHWH*.

**English order is reversed.** "God's word" puts the owner first. Hebrew puts it second: *dəḇar ʾĕlōhîm*. Gesenius's comparison with "God's-word" is about the shared stress, not the order (§89a).

**A noun that looks the same in both states is still construct.** *ʾereṣ*, *ʾîš*, *sēp̄er* do not change, but in *ʾereṣ kənaʿan* the first noun is construct all the same: gloss it CSTR, and remember that it cannot take the article.

## Connections

The construct chain is the Arabic *iḍāfa*. *bêṯ hammeleḵ* and *bayt al-malik* "the king's house" work alike: owned thing first, no article on it, the whole chain definite because the last noun is. Arabic still marks the second noun with a case ending (*al-maliki*), which Hebrew lost. Arabic *ʾahl* or *ibn* + noun, as in *ibn al-sabīl* "traveller", is the same device as Hebrew *ben-šānâ*.

## Exercises

1. Build "the word of the king" from *dāḇār* and *meleḵ*, and explain each step.
2. Give the construct of *zāqēn*, *māqôm*, *śāḏeh* and *yāḏ*.
3. Translate *ʾaḇrām yāšaḇ bəʾereṣ-kənāʿan*. Which noun is in the construct state, and how can you tell?
4. A student writes *\*habbêṯ hammeleḵ* for "the house of the king". Correct it.
5. Build "a man of God" and "the man of God", using *ʾĕlōhîm* with and without the article.
6. How would you say "a son of Saul"? Why not *ben-šāʾûl*?
7. Translate *wayyiqqaḥ hāʾîš nezem zāhāḇ*. Is the ring definite?
8. Build "the elder of the city".

## Worked answers

1. *dəḇar hammeleḵ*. The thing owned, *dāḇār*, comes first in the construct; its stress passes to the next word, so its first vowel reduces and its second shortens (Gesenius §89a). The owner comes second; it is definite, so it takes the article, and the chain is definite as a whole (§127a).
2. *zəqan*, *məqôm*, *śəḏēh*, *yaḏ* (lessons 3.3 and 3.5; §§27d, 93).
3. "Abram settled in the land of Canaan." *ʾereṣ* is construct. Its form does not change, because segholates are their own construct, but it stands first and is bound to the name after it.
4. *bêṯ hammeleḵ*. The article goes only on the last noun.
5. *ʾîš ʾĕlōhîm* and *ʾîš hāʾĕlōhîm*. 1 Kings 13 shows both: a prophet is introduced as *ʾîš ʾĕlōhîm* "a man of God" (13:1), and from then on he is *ʾîš hāʾĕlōhîm* "the man of God" (13:4). The article on the last noun decides (§127a).
6. *bēn ləšāʾûl*. A name is definite, so *ben-šāʾûl* would mean "the son of Saul". For an indefinite first noun, Hebrew keeps it absolute and uses *lə-* (§129c).
7. "The man took a gold ring." No: the second noun, *zāhāḇ*, is indefinite, so the chain is indefinite.
8. *zəqan hāʿîr*. Construct of *zāqēn*, then the definite noun; *ʿîr* takes *hā-* before ʿayin (lesson 2.2).
