## The idea

You have used adjectives since lesson 2.1: *ṭôḇ haddāḇār* "the word is good". This lesson puts together what you know and adds the rest.

A Hebrew adjective is, in form, a noun. It takes the same endings as a noun (lessons 3.1–3.2), its vowels shift by the same rules (lesson 3.3), it can take the article, and it can stand alone as a noun: *ṣaddîq* "a righteous person". What makes it an adjective is that it agrees with another noun.

It can do two jobs, and the article tells you which.

- **Describing (attributive):** "the good word". The adjective follows the noun and agrees with it in **gender, number and definiteness**. If the noun is definite, the adjective takes the article: *haddāḇār haṭṭôḇ*.
- **Stating (predicative):** "the word is good". The adjective agrees in gender and number but **never takes the article**, and there is no verb "is" (lesson 2.1). It usually comes first: *ṭôḇ haddāḇār*.

Gesenius gives the rule for describing in one sentence: the adjective "stands after the substantive, and agrees with it in gender and number" (§132a), and when the noun is definite "the attribute ... necessarily takes the article" (§126u). The definiteness agreement is the feature that English and Spanish lack. It is the key to telling a phrase from a sentence.

Where English uses an adjective, Hebrew often uses a construct chain (Gesenius §128p): *ʾîš milḥāmâ* "a warrior", *har qoḏšî* "my holy mountain", literally "the mountain of my holiness" (lesson 3.5).

## How it is built

### The four forms

An adjective has four forms: masculine and feminine, singular and plural. There is no dual.

- *ṭôḇ*, *ṭôḇâ*, *ṭôḇîm*, *ṭôḇôṯ* "good": unchangeable *ô*.
- *gāḏôl*, *gəḏôlâ*, *gəḏôlîm*, *gəḏōlôṯ* "great": the first vowel reduces (lesson 3.3).
- *ḥāḵām*, *ḥăḵāmâ*, *ḥăḵāmîm* "wise": hatef under the guttural.
- *qāṭōn*, *qəṭannâ*, *qəṭannîm* "small": the *n* doubles before an ending.
- *raḇ*, *rabbâ*, *rabbîm*, *rabbôṯ* "much, many"; *ḥay*, *ḥayyâ*, *ḥayyîm* "alive": doubling returns (lesson 3.3).
- *raʿ*, *rāʿâ*, *rāʿîm*, *rāʿôṯ* "bad": the guttural cannot double, so the vowel lengthens instead.
- *yāp̄eh*, *yāp̄â*, *yāp̄ôṯ* "beautiful": the *-eh* drops. (The masculine plural, *yāp̄îm* by pattern, is not attested.)
- *ʿiḇrî*, *ʿiḇriyyâ*; *miṣrî*, *miṣrîṯ*: gentilic adjectives (lesson 3.1).

Adjectives also have construct forms, used when the adjective is bound to a noun that limits it: *yāp̄eh* → *yəp̄ēh*, *yāp̄â* → *yəp̄aṯ*: *yəp̄aṯ-tōʾar* "beautiful of form" (Genesis 29:17).

### Describing

To say "the great lights":

1. The noun: *məʾōrōṯ*, masculine plural, with the article: *hamməʾōrōṯ*.
2. The adjective agrees: masculine plural, *gəḏōlîm* (not *-ôṯ*: lesson 3.2).
3. The noun is definite, so the adjective takes the article: *haggəḏōlîm*.
4. It follows the noun: *hamməʾōrōṯ haggəḏōlîm*.

The noun counts as definite also when it has a suffix or ends a definite chain (§126u). So: *yāḏəḵā haḥăzāqâ* "your strong hand", *maʿăśēh YHWH haggāḏōl* "the great work of the LORD".

With a construct chain, the adjective goes after the whole chain, whichever noun it describes (§132a; lesson 3.6).

With several adjectives, each one takes the article if the noun is definite (§126v): *hāʾēl haggāḏōl haggibbōr wəhannôrāʾ* "the great, mighty and awesome God". A demonstrative goes last: *hāʾîš haṭṭôḇ hazzeh*.

### Stating

To say "the land is good":

1. Subject: *hāʾāreṣ*, feminine.
2. The adjective agrees in gender and number: *ṭôḇâ*. No article.
3. Order: predicate first is commonest, *ṭôḇâ hāʾāreṣ*; subject first is also correct.

### Adjectives as nouns

An adjective with no noun stands for a person or thing with the quality. With the article it is definite: *haggəḏōlâ* "the elder (daughter)", *haṣṣaddîq* "the righteous one". Without it, indefinite or general: *ṣaddîq ʿim-rāšāʿ* "the righteous with the wicked" (Genesis 18:23). The feminine stands for an abstract: *rāʿâ* "harm", *ṭôḇâ* "good fortune" (lesson 3.1; §122q).

### Intensifying

*məʾōḏ* "very" follows: *ṭôḇ məʾōḏ*, and is doubled for more: *məʾōḏ məʾōḏ* (lesson 2.2). Comparison, "greater than", uses *min* (lesson 5.5).

## Where it applies

- **Describing, indefinite:** *nəḡāʿîm gəḏōlîm*, *ʾiššâ ḥăḵāmâ* (lesson 3.1).
- **Describing, definite** by article, name, suffix or chain: *hammāʾôr haggāḏōl*, *yāḏəḵā haḥăzāqâ*, *maʿăśēh YHWH haggāḏōl*.
- **Stating:** *ṭôḇ məʾōḏ*, *ṭôḇâ hāʾāreṣ*, *wəʾanšê səḏōm rāʿîm* (lesson 3.6).
- **Stating with *hāyâ*:** when a past or future state needs a verb, or the subject comes first in a narrative: *wərāḥēl hāyəṯâ yəp̄aṯ-tōʾar*.
- **As a noun:** *haggəḏōlâ*, *ṣaddîq ʿim-rāšāʿ*, *heḥāḵām* (lesson 2.2).
- **The feminine as an abstract:** *rāʿâ*, *ṭôḇâ*, and plural *gəḏōlôṯ* "great things" (§122q).
- **In the construct, with a limiting noun:** *yəp̄aṯ-tōʾar*, *yəp̄ôṯ marʾeh*, *kəḇaḏ-peh* "heavy of mouth" (Exodus 4:10).
- **Participles** behave as adjectives in all these positions (part 4): *ʾēš ʾōḵəlâ* "a consuming fire" (Deuteronomy 4:24).
- **Numbers and *kol*** have rules of their own (lessons 5.3 and 5.6). *raḇ*, *rabbîm* "many" sometimes stands before its noun, like a number (§132b).

## Watch out

**Do not put the adjective first to describe.** By analogy with Spanish *buen hombre* or French *bon homme*, you might write *\*ṭôḇ ʾîš* for "a good man". In Hebrew that is a sentence, "a man is good" at best. The describing adjective follows: *ʾîš ṭôḇ*.

**Match the article.** *\*hāʾîš ṭôḇ* is a sentence, "the man is good". "The good man" is *hāʾîš haṭṭôḇ*. Spanish *el hombre bueno* has one article; Hebrew needs two.

**A suffix counts as definite.** *\*bənî qāṭōn* for "my young son" is a sentence, "my son is young". The phrase is *bənî haqqāṭōn*.

**Agree with the gender, not the ending.** *\*ʿārîm gəḏōlîm* is wrong; *ʿîr* is feminine: *ʿārîm gəḏōlôṯ* (lesson 3.2).

**No dual adjectives.** *\*ʿênayim yāp̄ayim* is impossible; use the feminine plural: *ʿênayim yāp̄ôṯ* (lesson 3.2).

**Do not split a chain with an adjective.** *\*maʿăśēh haggāḏōl YHWH* is impossible. Put it at the end (lesson 3.6).

**Do not read every *-â* form as an adjective.** In *zaʿăqaṯ səḏōm ... kî-rābbâ* "the outcry of Sodom ... how great it is" (Genesis 18:20), *rābbâ* is a stative qatal verb, "it has become great", not the adjective *rabbâ* (lesson 2.6). The stress shows it: the accent falls on the first syllable, as in a qatal form, and the qameṣ is the lengthening of pause (lesson 1.8); the adjective *rabbâ* is stressed on its ending. In meaning the two are close.

**English comparatives have no Hebrew ending.** There is no *\*gəḏōlēr*. "The greater light" is *hammāʾôr haggāḏōl* (example 2); "greater than" uses *min* (lesson 5.5).

## Connections

Arabic has the same rule of definiteness agreement: *al-bayt al-kabīr* "the big house", *al-bayt kabīr* "the house is big". It also places the adjective after the noun and after the whole *iḍāfa*: *bayt al-malik al-kabīr*, ambiguous between "the king's big house" and "the house of the great king", exactly as in Hebrew. Spanish and French mark definiteness once and agreement in gender and number; Hebrew and Arabic mark all three on both words.

## Exercises

1. Give all four forms of *gāḏôl* and of *qāṭōn*.
2. Translate *hāʾîš haṭṭôḇ* and *ṭôḇ hāʾîš*, and explain the difference.
3. Build "a wise woman" and "the wise woman".
4. Build "your great name" (to a man), using *šimḵā* "your name".
5. Build "The cities are great", and say why the adjective has *-ôṯ*.
6. Translate *wəʾeṯ-yāḏəḵā haḥăzāqâ*. Why does the adjective have the article when the noun does not?
7. Build "the great and good land".
8. Translate *šēm haggəḏōlâ lēʾâ*. What does *haggəḏōlâ* stand for?
9. Find and fix the error: *\*hammeleḵ ḥāḵām haggāḏôl* "the wise great king".
10. Translate *yəp̄aṯ-tōʾar* and explain its construction.

## Worked answers

1. *gāḏôl*, *gəḏôlâ*, *gəḏôlîm*, *gəḏōlôṯ*; *qāṭōn*, *qəṭannâ*, *qəṭannîm*, (*qəṭannôṯ*). The first vowel reduces before the endings; in *qāṭōn* the *n* doubles (lesson 3.3).
2. *hāʾîš haṭṭôḇ* "the good man": both definite, so a phrase. *ṭôḇ hāʾîš* "the man is good": the adjective has no article, so it is the predicate (Gesenius §126u; lesson 2.1).
3. *ʾiššâ ḥăḵāmâ*, *hāʾiššâ haḥăḵāmâ*.
4. *šimḵā haggāḏôl*. The suffix makes the noun definite, so the adjective takes the article (§126u).
5. *gəḏōlôṯ heʿārîm*, or *heʿārîm gəḏōlôṯ*. *ʿîr* is feminine, so the adjective is feminine plural, whatever the noun's ending (lesson 3.2). It is a predicate, so no article.
6. "And your strong hand." The suffix makes *yāḏəḵā* definite, and a describing adjective agrees in definiteness (§126u).
7. *hāʾāreṣ haggəḏôlâ wəhaṭṭôḇâ*. Each adjective takes the article and the feminine ending (§126v).
8. "The name of the elder was Leah." *haggəḏōlâ* is an adjective used as a noun: "the great (daughter)", that is, the elder.
9. *hammeleḵ heḥāḵām haggāḏôl*. Both adjectives need the article, because the noun is definite (§126v). *ḥāḵām* takes *he-* (lesson 2.2).
10. "Beautiful of form." The adjective *yāp̄â* is in the construct, *yəp̄aṯ*, bound to the noun that says in what way she was beautiful.
