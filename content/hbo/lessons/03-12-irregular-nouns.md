## The idea

A handful of the commonest nouns do not follow the patterns of lessons 3.2 to 3.8. They are the family words, *ʾāḇ* "father", *ʾāḥ* "brother", *ʾāḥôṯ* "sister", *bēn* "son", *baṯ* "daughter", *ʾîš* "man", *ʾiššâ* "woman", and a few everyday nouns, *bayiṯ* "house", *yôm* "day", *ʿîr* "city", *peh* "mouth". You have met many of their forms already in passing; this lesson puts them in order.

Gesenius insists that the label "irregular" is not quite fair. Their oddities, he writes, "are almost always subordinate to the usual phonetic laws, and the usual designation of the nouns as irregular is, therefore, not justified" once their ground forms are known (§96). In other words, most of them are short old nouns whose full base shows only when an ending is added. Three causes account for nearly everything:

1. **A hidden final vowel or consonant.** *ʾāḇ*, *ʾāḥ* end in a vowel *-î* in their bound forms: *ʾăḇî*, *ʾăḥî* "father of, brother of". *baṯ* is from *bint*, so *bittî* "my daughter" doubles the *t*.
2. **A plural from another stem.** *ʾîš* has the plural *ʾănāšîm*; *ʾiššâ* has *nāšîm*; *yôm* has *yāmîm*. The singular and plural were probably once different words, and Gesenius reports several competing explanations (§96).
3. **A guttural that cannot double.** *ʾaḥîm* "brothers" should have a doubled *ḥ*, and keeps its short *a* as if it did (§96, §22c).

Where the sources disagree on the history, this lesson says so. The forms themselves are certain and frequent.

## How it is built

### Father and brother

*ʾāḇ* and *ʾāḥ* add every suffix to a form ending in *-î* (§96; §90k): *ʾāḇî* "my father", *ʾāḇîḵā* "your father", *ʾāḇîw* (also *ʾāḇîhû*) "his father", *ʾāḇîhā*, *ʾāḇînû*, *ʾăḇîḵem*, *ʾăḇîhem*. The construct is *ʾăḇî*, *ʾăḥî*: *ʾăḇî haggilʿāḏ* "the father of Gilead". The 3SG.M suffix after a vowel is *-w* or *-hû*, not *-ô*.

Plurals: *ʾāḇôṯ* "fathers" (masculine, with *-ôṯ*; lesson 3.2), construct *ʾăḇôṯ*, with suffixes *ʾăḇōṯay*, *ʾăḇōṯênû*. *ʾaḥîm* "brothers", construct *ʾăḥê*, *ʾaḥay* "my brothers", *ʾeḥāyw* "his brothers" (the *a* becomes *e* before *ḥā*, §96).

*ʾāḥôṯ* "sister": *ʾăḥōṯî*, *ʾăḥōṯô*. Gesenius derives it from *ʾaḥawat* with the *w* lost (§96).

### Man and woman

*ʾîš*: construct *ʾîš*, *ʾîšî* "my husband", *ʾîšāh* "her husband". Plural *ʾănāšîm*, construct *ʾanšê*. Whether *ʾîš* and *ʾănāšîm* share a root is disputed: Gesenius sets out the view that both come from *ʾ-n-š*, then gives his preference for a separate stem for the singular (§96).

*ʾiššâ*: Gesenius takes it as from *ʾinšâ*, with the *n* assimilated (§96). Construct *ʾēšeṯ* "wife of": *ʾēšeṯ ʾaḇrām*. With suffixes, the base *ʾišt-*: *ʾištî* "my wife", *ʾištô* "his wife". Plural *nāšîm*, construct *nəšê*, *nāšay* "my wives". So the "irregular" *ʾēšeṯ* is only the feminine *-t* on *ʾišš-*, with the doubling dropped at the end of the word.

Note that Genesis 2:23 explains the name *ʾiššâ* by *ʾîš*: "she shall be called woman, for from man she was taken". The narrator's wordplay is not a statement of etymology, and the grammarians derive the two words differently (§96).

### Son and daughter

*bēn*: construct *ben-* (usually with maqqef), *bənî* "my son", *binḵā* "your son", *bənô* "his son". Plural *bānîm*, construct *bənê*, *bānay*, *bānāyw*.

*baṯ*: from *bint*, the feminine of *bēn* (§96). So: *bittî* "my daughter", *bittô* "his daughter" (with *t* doubled). Plural *bānôṯ*, construct *bənôṯ*, *bənōṯay*.

### House, day, city

*bayiṯ*: construct *bêṯ*, *bêṯî*, *bêṯô* (lesson 3.4). Plural *bāttîm*, construct *bāttê*, *bāttênû*. The doubled *t* is unexplained; Gesenius lists four proposals and concludes, "A definite solution is at present impossible" (§96).

*yôm*: construct *yôm*. Dual *yômayim*. Plural *yāmîm*, construct *yəmê*, *yāmay* "my days". Gesenius takes the plural to come "probably from a different singular", *yām* (§96).

*ʿîr*: plural *ʿārîm*, construct *ʿārê*, again probably from a related singular *ʿār* (§96). The plural is feminine like the singular: *ʿārîm gəḏōlôṯ* (lesson 3.2).

### Other everyday nouns

- *peh* "mouth": construct *pî*, *pîḵā* "your mouth", *pîw* "his mouth". Its history is disputed (§96).
- *rōʾš* "head": plural *rāʾšîm*, construct *rāʾšê*. The *ō* is an obscured *ā* (§96).
- *mayim* "water": construct *mê* or *mêmê*; *mêmêḵā* "your water".
- *ʾāmâ* "slave woman": plural *ʾămāhôṯ*, with a consonantal *h* (§96).
- *kəlî* "vessel, object": plural *kēlîm*, construct *kəlê*: *kəlê-ḵesep̄* "objects of silver".
- *śeh* "sheep or goat (one animal)": construct *śēh*.
- *ḥām* "father-in-law" and *ḥāmôṯ* "mother-in-law" follow *ʾāḇ* and *ʾāḥôṯ*: *ḥāmîhā*, *ḥămôṯāh* (§96).

### Building "the wives of his sons"

1. "Sons": *bānîm*; "his sons": *bānāyw* (plural suffix, lesson 3.8).
2. "Wives of": *nāšîm* in the construct, *nəšê*.
3. *nəšê-ḇānāyw* (Genesis 7:7). The *b* is soft after the vowel of *nəšê*.

## Where it applies

- **Family relations in narrative**, constantly: *ʾāḇîw*, *ʾāḥîw*, *ʾištô*, *bənô*, *bittô*.
- **Idioms with *bēn* and *baṯ*:** age (*ben-šānâ*, lesson 3.5), membership (*bənê yiśrāʾēl*), quality (*ben-ḥayil* "a capable man"), and personified places (*baṯ-ṣiyyôn* "daughter Zion", in poetry).
- **Idioms with *ʾāḇ*:** *bêṯ ʾāḇ* "father's house, clan" (example 11); *ʾăḇî* + a place name for the founder of a town (*ʾăḇî haggilʿāḏ*, Joshua 17:1).
- **Idioms with *ʾîš*:** *ʾîš* "each one" with a following *rēʿēhû* or *ʾāḥîw* "his fellow, his brother" (lesson 3.7); *ʾîš* + noun for a man characterised by it (*ʾîš milḥāmâ*, lesson 3.5).
- **Idioms with *peh*:** *ʿal-pî* "according to", "at the command of"; *pî ḥereḇ* "the edge of the sword", literally "the mouth of the sword".
- **Idioms with *yôm*:** *hayyôm* "today" (lesson 2.2), *bayyôm hahûʾ* (lesson 3.10), *yāmîm* "some time, a year" in some contexts.
- **Later in the guide:** *ʾeḥāḏ* "one", which also has virtual doubling of *ḥ* (lesson 5.3); the locative *bayṯâ* "homewards" (lesson 5.2).

## Watch out

**Do not give *ʾāḇ* the ordinary suffixes.** By analogy with *qôlô* you might write *\*ʾāḇô* for "his father" or *\*ʾāḇəḵā* for "your father". The forms are *ʾāḇîw*, *ʾāḇîḵā*.

**Do not make a plural from the singular of *ʾîš*, *ʾiššâ*, *yôm*.** *\*ʾîšîm*, *\*ʾiššôṯ*, *\*yômîm* are not the prose plurals. Use *ʾănāšîm*, *nāšîm*, *yāmîm*. (Gesenius notes *ʾîšîm* three times in poetry and *ʾiššōṯ* once, in Ezekiel 23:44, §96.)

**Do not use *ʾiššâ* as the construct.** "Abram's wife" is *ʾēšeṯ ʾaḇrām*, not *\*ʾiššaṯ ʾaḇrām*.

**Do not forget the doubled *t* of *baṯ*.** "My daughter" is *bittî*, not *\*baṯî*.

**Do not read *bāttîm* with a short *o*.** The older school pronunciation *bottîm* is wrong; Gesenius shows from the Babylonian pointing that the vowel is *ā* (§96).

**Feminine plurals of masculine-looking words keep their gender.** *ʾāḇôṯ* is masculine; *ʿārîm* and *nāšîm* are feminine. Adjectives follow the gender of the singular (lesson 3.2).

**Spanish and French also have irregular family words** (*padre*, *père*), but their irregularity lies elsewhere. In Hebrew watch the bound and suffixed forms, where the hidden *-î* of *ʾāḇî-* and the base *bitt-* appear.

## Connections

Arabic has the same small class: *ʾab*, *ʾaḫ* "father, brother", which add a long vowel before suffixes and in the bound form (*ʾabū*, *ʾabī*, *ʾabā*: *ʾabūhu* "his father", *ʾaḫī* "my brother"), the same vowel that shows as *-î* in Hebrew *ʾāḇîw*. Arabic *bint* "daughter" is Gesenius's ground form for Hebrew *baṯ*, and Arabic *fam*/*fū* "mouth" is among the forms he cites for *peh* (§96). Arabic plural *ʾummahāt* "mothers", with its added *h*, parallels Hebrew *ʾămāhôṯ*.

6. Build "the houses of the sons of Israel".
7. A student writes *\*ʾāḇô* for "his father" and *\*baṯî* for "my daughter". Correct both.
8. Translate *wayyāḇōʾ nōaḥ ûḇānāyw wəʾištô ûnəšê-ḇānāyw ʾittô ʾel-hattēḇâ*, and identify each family noun.
9. Why are *bāttîm* and *ʾaḥîm* written as they are? Which explanation is certain?

## Worked answers

1. *ʾāḇî*, *ʾāḇîḵā*, *ʾāḇîw* (or *ʾāḇîhû*). The suffixes attach to *ʾāḇî-* (Gesenius §96).
2. *ʾēšeṯ yaʿăqōḇ*, *ʾištô*. The construct is *ʾēšeṯ*; the suffixed base is *ʾišt-*.
3. *bittî* (from *bint*, so *t* doubled) and *bənōṯāyw* (feminine plural *-ôṯ* + *-āyw*; lesson 3.8).
4. "Where is your brother Abel?" *ʾāḥî-* already ends in a vowel, so the suffix is added directly (§91c).
5. *ʾănāšîm*, *nāšîm*, *yāmîm*. Gesenius thinks the plural of *ʾîš* and of *yôm* may come from different singulars or stems, and reports competing views (§96).
6. *bāttê bənê yiśrāʾēl*. Two construct plurals and a name (Exodus 12:27 has *ʿal-bāttê ḇənê-yiśrāʾēl*).
7. *ʾāḇîw*, *bittî*.
8. "Noah went into the ark, with his sons, his wife and his sons' wives." *bānāyw* (plural of *bēn* with "his"), *ʾištô* (*ʾiššâ* with "his"), *nəšê* (construct of *nāšîm*), *bānāyw* again.
9. *ʾaḥîm* keeps its short *a* because *ḥ* is treated as doubled, though a guttural cannot be (virtual doubling, §22c; lesson 2.2). That is certain. The doubled *t* of *bāttîm* has no agreed explanation; Gesenius concludes that no solution is yet possible (§96).
