## The idea

Part 3 showed how nouns are built: a root and a pattern of vowels, prefixes and endings. Verbs are built the same way, and part 4 is about them. First the large picture, which organises everything else.

A Hebrew verb root does not have one set of forms. It can be poured into up to seven **stems**. A stem is a fixed pattern for the whole verb, with its own vowels, sometimes a prefix, sometimes a doubled letter, that changes what the root means in a regular way. The root *k-b-d* means "heavy". In the plain stem it means "be heavy". With the middle letter doubled it means "make weighty, honour". With a prefix *h-* it means "make heavy". The root stays; the stem says how the idea is applied.

The Jewish grammarians called the stems *binyanim*, from *b-n-h* "build"; Gesenius renders the word "formations" (§39c). English grammars also say "conjugations", which is confusing, because a conjugation in Latin or Spanish is a class of verbs. In Hebrew one verb can appear in several stems. This guide says "stem" and uses the Hebrew names.

Why does Hebrew work like this? Because a root is not a word but a set of consonants carrying an idea, made into words by patterns (lesson 0.2), as with *meleḵ* "king", *malkâ* "queen", *malḵûṯ* "kingship". Verbs do it more regularly. Gesenius notes that other languages have traces of the same thing, as in English *fall* and *fell*, *lie* and *lay*, but that in Hebrew the derived forms are "incomparably more regular and systematic" (§39c–d).

## How it is built

### The names

The stems are named after the old model verb *pāʿal* "do". Each name is that verb put into the stem's pattern (Gesenius §39e). So the name tells you the shape:

- **qal** "light": the plain stem, with nothing added. *pāqaḏ* "he attended to".
- **niphal** (*nip̄ʿal*): a prefix *n-*. *nip̄qaḏ* "it was missed".
- **piel** (*piʿʿēl*): the middle root letter doubled, with *i* and *ē*. *piqqēḏ* "he mustered".
- **pual** (*puʿʿal*): doubled like the piel, with *u* and *a*. *puqqaḏ* "it was counted".
- **hiphil** (*hip̄ʿîl*): a prefix *h-* and *î* after the second root letter. *hip̄qîḏ* "he appointed".
- **hophal** (*hop̄ʿal*): a prefix *h-* with *o*. *hop̄qaḏ* "it was deposited".
- **hithpael** (*hiṯpaʿʿēl*): a prefix *hiṯ-* and the middle letter doubled. *hiṯpaqqēḏ* "he mustered himself".

The six stems after the qal are the **derived stems**. Gesenius notes that the old grammarians called the qal "light" because it carries no additions, and the others "heavy", weighted with doubling or prefixes (§39e).

### Three families and their passives

The derived stems fall into three groups by shape (Gesenius §39g):

- **The doubled stems:** piel, its passive pual, and the hithpael, which is built on the piel with a prefix.
- **The h-stems:** hiphil and its passive hophal.
- **The n-stem:** niphal, which is itself often passive.

A **passive** form makes the person or thing acted on into the subject: "it was counted" instead of "he counted it". The pual and hophal are passives of the piel and hiphil, and are told apart from them, Gesenius says, by "more obscure vowels" (§39e): *u* and *o* in place of *i* and *a*. The piel *piqqēḏ* becomes the pual *puqqaḏ*; the hiphil *hip̄qîḏ* becomes the hophal *hop̄qaḏ*. The qal had its own old passive too, but it survives only in scattered forms (lesson 7.2).

### What the stems tend to mean

Each stem has typical meanings. They are tendencies, not rules, and what a stem does with a given root must be learned from the dictionary.

- **Qal:** the basic meaning. Action (*pāqaḏ* "attend to") or state (*kāḇēḏ* "be heavy").
- **Niphal:** usually passive or middle: something happens to the subject, or the subject does it to itself. *nip̄qaḏ* "be missed".
- **Piel:** often makes a state into an action, or an action into a thorough or repeated one. *kibbēḏ* "honour", from "be heavy".
- **Hiphil:** usually causative: "make someone do or be". *hiḵbîḏ* "make heavy".
- **Hithpael:** usually reflexive ("do to oneself") or reciprocal ("do to one another").

How to describe the piel in particular is disputed. The traditional label is "intensive" (Gesenius §52f). Many modern grammars prefer other terms. Lesson 7.1 sets out the debate. In part 4 the guide states what each stem does with each verb and treats the labels as rough guides.

### Very few roots use all seven

Gesenius stresses that "very few verbs exhibit them all" (§39e). Most roots appear in two or three stems. Some live mainly in a derived stem: the common verb "speak" is a piel, *dibbēr*; its qal survives only as a participle, *dōḇēr*, and a handful of other forms (oshb). The model verb *p-q-d* used in this lesson is one of the rare roots with all seven: Gesenius notes that it was chosen as a model for exactly this reason (§39e, note 3), and every form in the examples is in the Bible.

## Where it applies

- **Every verb belongs to one or more stems.** When you meet a verb, the first questions are: what is the root, and which stem is it in? Lesson 0.2 taught you to find the root; the stem is the next layer.
- **The dictionary lists a root once, with its stems under it.** BDB gives *p-q-d* once, with a section for each stem in which it occurs. The meaning you need is the one under the right stem.
- **Each stem has the full verb system:** qatal, yiqtol, wayyiqtol, imperative, infinitives and participles. Lessons 4.2 to 4.10 teach these in the qal; lessons 4.20 to 4.24 show the same system in the derived stems.
- **Nouns share the roots.** *piqqāḏôn* "deposit" from *p-q-d*, *kāḇôḏ* "glory" from *k-b-d*.
- **Roots used only in derived stems.** Some verbs exist only, or mainly, in a derived stem: *dibbēr* "speak" (piel), *biqqēš* "seek" (piel, lesson 3.8). They are cited by that stem, with the stem named.
- **Two stems that differ only in vowels.** A qal and a piel can share their consonants exactly. The consonants כבד can be *kāḇēḏ* "it is heavy" or *kibbēḏ* "he honoured". The pointing and the doubling decide.

## Watch out

**Do not treat a stem as a tense.** The niphal is not a past and the hiphil is not a future. Each stem has all the tenses and moods. *Hip̄qîḏ* is a hiphil qatal; the hiphil yiqtol is *yap̄qîḏ*.

**Do not read the stem's label as its meaning in every verb.** "Hiphil = causative" works for *hiḵbîḏ* "make heavy", but *hip̄qîḏ* means "appoint, put in charge", and *hišlîḵ* "throw" has no plain "cause" in it at all. A student who writes *\*hip̄qîḏ* "he caused to visit" has applied a label instead of the dictionary. The right reading is "he appointed".

**Do not assume a root has a qal.** *\*dāḇar* "he spoke" does not occur; the verb is *dibbēr* (piel). Look up which stems a root actually has.

**Do not confuse the niphal with a noun prefix *m-*.** Both add a consonant before the root. *Nilḥam* "he fought" is a niphal verb of *l-ḥ-m*; *milḥāmâ* "war", from the same root, is a noun. The niphal prefix is *n-*, and in the qatal it is followed by *i*.

**Do not assume the passive of a piel is the niphal.** The niphal often serves as the passive of the qal, but the passive of the piel is the pual and the passive of the hiphil is the hophal. *Puqqaḏ* answers to *piqqēḏ*, not *nip̄qaḏ*.

## Connections

Arabic has the same system in a richer form: the plain verb *kataba* "write", Form II *kattaba* "make write" with the middle letter doubled, like the piel; Form IV *ʾaktaba*, a causative with a prefix, like the hiphil; Form VII *inkataba*, with *n-*, a passive or middle like the niphal; Form V *takattaba*, a *t-* prefix on the doubled form, like the hithpael. Gesenius notes that Arabic "has preserved great wealth" here and Hebrew "holds the middle place" between it and Aramaic (§39d). If you know the Arabic forms, the Hebrew stems will feel familiar.

## Exercises

1. Give the stem of each form and say how you know: *nip̄qaḏ*, *hip̄qîḏ*, *puqqaḏ*, *pāqaḏ*.
2. The qal *šāḇar* means "break". Using the tendencies in this lesson, what might the piel *šibbar* add? Check your answer against the vocabulary.
3. Why are *puqqaḏ* and *hop̄qaḏ* called passives? What are they the passives of?
4. *Kāḇəḏâ* and *wayyaḵbēḏ*: give the root of each, the stem, and the difference in meaning.
5. Take apart *wəlōʾ-nip̄qaḏ lāhem məʾûmâ* and translate it. Then say how the qal sentence *wəlōʾ-p̄āqaḏnû məʾûmâ* tells the same event.
6. A student reads *hišlîḵ* as "he caused to throw". What has gone wrong?
7. Why is "speak" listed in the dictionary as *dibbēr* and not as a qal?
8. The consonants פקד stand alone in an unpointed text. Name two stems they could be, and what decides between them in the pointed text.

## Worked answers

1. *nip̄qaḏ*: niphal, from the prefix *n-* with *i*. *hip̄qîḏ*: hiphil, from the prefix *h-* and the *î* after the second root letter. *puqqaḏ*: pual, from the doubled middle letter and the vowels *u*, *a*. *pāqaḏ*: qal, plain, with nothing added.
2. The piel often makes an action thorough or repeated. *šibbar* is "smash, shatter", breaking into pieces (BDB). The tendency fits here, but you still need the dictionary to know it.
3. They turn the person or thing acted on into the subject: "it was counted", "it was deposited". *puqqaḏ* is the passive of the piel; *hop̄qaḏ* is the passive of the hiphil. Gesenius describes them as marked by "more obscure vowels", *u* and *o* (§39e).
4. Both are *k-b-d*. *Kāḇəḏâ* is qal: "it (she) is heavy", a state. *Wayyaḵbēḏ* is hiphil: "he made heavy", causing the state.
5. *wə=* "and", *lōʾ* "not", *nip̄qaḏ* niphal qatal 3SG.M "was missed", *lāhem* "to them", *məʾûmâ* "anything": "and nothing of theirs went missing". In the qal sentence the shepherds are the subject and do the missing: "we did not miss anything". In the niphal sentence the thing missed is the subject.
6. The label "causative" has been applied without the dictionary. *hišlîḵ* is a hiphil, but the verb means "throw, cast" (BDB). Translate it "he threw".
7. Because its qal has no qatal or yiqtol in use, only a participle and a few other forms: the verb lives in the piel. A verb like this is cited by its commonest stem, with the stem named.
8. For example qal *pāqaḏ* and piel *piqqēḏ* (or pual *puqqaḏ*). The vowels decide, and the dagesh in the *q*: the piel and pual double the middle letter; the qal does not.
