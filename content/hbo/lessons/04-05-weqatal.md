## The idea

A story is a chain of wayyiqtols. Instructions, laws, plans and prophecies are chains too, and Hebrew links them with the mirror image: *wə-* + **qatal**. "Go, and you shall say to my servant David ..." "You shall make the ark ... and you shall cover it with pitch." This is the **weqatal**.

Gesenius describes the two chains as a pair. A past narrative begins with a qatal and continues with wayyiqtols; "conversely, the representation of a series of future events begins with the imperfect, and is continued in the perfect" (§49a). The first verb sets the time or the mood: an imperative, a yiqtol, a participle, a time phrase. Every following step is a weqatal, which takes its time from that first verb (§112a, d).

So the weqatal is not a past, though its stem is a qatal. *Wəʾāmartā* is "and you shall say", not "and you said". This is the second half of the system the older grammars called "waw conversive". As with the wayyiqtol, this guide names the form by its shape and leaves the theories to lesson 4.6.

In the gloss, *wə-* is WQTL, and the stem and ending follow without QTL: *wə-šāmar-tā* `WQTL-guard-2SG.M`.

## How it is built

### The form

The weqatal is the ordinary conjunction *wə-* (with all its forms, *û-*, *wā-*, *wa-*, *wî-*: lesson 2.3) placed directly in front of a qatal. Gesenius stresses that "this wāw is in form an ordinary wāw copulative" (§49h). Unlike the wayyiqtol, there is no doubling and no special stem.

What changes is the **stress**. In the first and second person singular, the plain qatal is stressed on the stem: *hāláḵtî* "I went", *šāmártā* "you guarded" (lesson 4.2). After the *wə-* of the weqatal the stress moves to the ending: *wəhālaḵtî́* "and I will go", *wəšāmartā́* "and you shall keep" (Gesenius §49h). The shift is not carried out everywhere: never in the 1PL, not in pause, and not consistently in some weak verbs (Gesenius §49k–m). Since the accents are removed from the examples in this guide, you will usually recognise a weqatal by its place in the chain, not by its stress. The acute accents here are the guide's.

### How to tell weqatal from *and* + qatal

A *wə* + qatal at the head of its clause can be:

- **a weqatal**: it continues an imperative, a yiqtol, a participle or another future statement, and means "and (then) he will / you shall / I will";
- **a plain "and" + qatal**: "and he did". In classical prose this is uncommon at the start of a clause, because the past is normally continued with the wayyiqtol (Gesenius §106e; §112pp).

So ask: what came before? If the passage is a command, a plan or a prediction, it is a weqatal. In this guide's glosses the decision is made for you, and the note says why.

### Building a chain

"Go and gather the elders of Israel, and say to them ..." (Exodus 3:16):

1. The first verb is a command: the imperative *lēḵ* "go!" (lesson 4.7).
2. The next step continues the command: weqatal, 2SG.M. *ʾāsap̄* "gather" → *ʾāsap̄tā*; with *wə-*: *wəʾāsap̄tā*.
3. The third step does the same: *wəʾāmartā* "and you shall say".
4. *lēḵ wəʾāsap̄tā ʾeṯ-ziqnê yiśrāʾēl wəʾāmartā ʾălēhem*.

## Where it applies

- **After an imperative:** the next steps of a command. *lēḵ wəʾāmartā*; *lēḵ wəʾāsap̄tā ... wəʾāmartā* (Gesenius §112r).
- **After a yiqtol of future or instruction:** *qinnîm taʿăśeh ... wəḵāp̄artā* (Gesenius §112p).
- **After a jussive or cohortative, a wish:** God's *yəhî məʾōrōṯ ... wəhāyû* "let there be lights ... and let them be" (Genesis 1:14–15; Gesenius §112q). Lesson 4.8.
- **After a participle of what is about to happen:** "I am about to send rain ... and I will wipe out" (Genesis 7:4; Gesenius §112t). Lesson 4.9.
- **After a verbless statement or a reason:** *wəʾāhaḇtā* after the Shema (Gesenius §112x).
- **Opening a future scene with *wəhāyâ* "and it will be":** *wəhāyâ kî-yirʾû* (Gesenius §112y). It mirrors *wayhî* in narrative (lesson 4.18).
- **Habits, past or present:** after a yiqtol of habit, the weqatal continues the habit: *yiqqaḥ ... wənāṭâ* "he used to take ... and pitch"; *yaʿăzoḇ ... wəḏāḇaq* "leaves ... and clings" (Gesenius §112e, m).
- **In conditions and laws:** after *kî* "if, when" or *ʾim* "if", the consequences are weqatals (Gesenius §112p, ff). Lesson 6.7.

## Watch out

**Do not translate a weqatal as a past.** *Wəʾāmartā ʾel-ʿaḇdî* is "and you shall say to my servant", not "and you said". The stem is qatal; the time comes from the chain.

**Do not put anything between *wə-* and the verb.** As with the wayyiqtol, any word that comes first breaks the chain, and the verb becomes a yiqtol: *wəʾōṯāḵ yəḥayyû*, not *\*wəʾōṯāḵ wəḥiyyû*. A negative breaks it too: "and you shall not eat" is *wəlōʾ ṯōʾḵal*, with yiqtol, never *\*wəlōʾ ʾāḵaltā* in the sense of a command.

**Do not make the stem a yiqtol after *wə-*.** By analogy with *wayyiqtol*, an English speaker might expect "and you will keep" to be *\*wəṯišmōr*. That is possible Hebrew, but it is not the chain form and often means "so that you may keep" (lesson 6.4). The chain form is *wəšāmartā*.

**Do not double after *wə-*.** *\*wəššāmartā* mixes up the two chains. The doubling belongs to the wayyiqtol's *wa-* only.

**Do not use weqatal to continue a past story.** "He went and he said" is *wayyēleḵ wayyōʾmer*, not *\*hālaḵ wəʾāmar*. Gesenius notes that a plain *wə* + qatal continuing a past narrative "agrees rather with Aramaic syntax" and belongs mainly to later books (§106e).

**Do not expect the stress to show in this guide.** The shift *šāmártā* → *wəšāmartā́* is real but irregular, and the accents are stripped from `orig`. Read the context.

**Do not read every *wəhāyâ* as "and it was".** In narrative of the past, Hebrew says *wayhî*. *Wəhāyâ* nearly always looks forward: "and it will be", or, in a habit, "and it would be" (Exodus 33:7).

## Connections

Arabic has no weqatal: it marks the future with *sa-* or *sawfa* and the prefix form, and continues it with *wa* + prefix form. But Arabic does use the suffix form for the future in two places that may help you feel the logic: in oaths and wishes (*raḥimahu llāh* "may God have mercy on him") and after *ʾin* "if" (*ʾin jāʾa* "if he comes"). In both, a qatal-type form looks forward. Modern Hebrew (`heb`) has lost the weqatal and uses the yiqtol for the future.

## Exercises

1. Build "Go and say to Pharaoh" with *lēḵ* and the weqatal of *ʾāmar*.
2. Continue *tišmōr ʾeṯ-bərîṯî* "you will keep my covenant" with "and you will remember my words".
3. Translate *wəzāḵartî ʾeṯ-bərîṯî* and explain why it is not "and I remembered".
4. Gloss *wəhārəḡû* piece by piece.
5. In Genesis 12:12, why is the last verb *yəḥayyû* a yiqtol and not a weqatal?
6. A student writes *\*wayyišmōr* for "and he will keep". What has he confused, and what is the right form?
7. Translate *ʿal-kēn yaʿăzoḇ-ʾîš ʾeṯ-ʾāḇîw wəʾeṯ-ʾimmô wəḏāḇaq bəʾištô*. Is the weqatal future here?
8. How would you say "and you shall not kill" in a chain of laws? Explain.
9. Where does the stress fall in *wəhālaḵtî*, and how does that differ from *hālaḵtî*?

## Worked answers

1. *lēḵ wəʾāmartā ʾel-parʿōh*. The imperative sets the command; the weqatal 2SG.M *wəʾāmartā* continues it (Gesenius §112r).
2. *tišmōr ʾeṯ-bərîṯî wəzāḵartā ʾeṯ-dəḇāray*. A yiqtol of future or instruction is continued by weqatal (Gesenius §112p); "my words" is the plural with suffix *dəḇāray* (lesson 3.8).
3. "And I will remember my covenant." It continues a description of the future (Gesenius §112p), so it takes the future time of its chain; the past "and I remembered" would be *wāʾezkōr*, a wayyiqtol.
4. *wə-* `WQTL`, *hārəḡ* `kill`, *-û* `3PL`: "and they will kill". The vowel *a* of the stem has reduced before the vowel ending, as in *šāmərû* (lesson 2.6).
5. Because the object *ʾōṯāḵ* stands between *wə-* and the verb, for contrast: "but *you* they will keep alive". A chain form must follow its *wə-* directly; otherwise the future is expressed by yiqtol (Gesenius §112c).
6. He has put the wayyiqtol prefix on a future. *Wayyišmōr* is "and he kept" (lesson 4.4). The future chain form is the weqatal *wəšāmar* "and he will keep".
7. "That is why a man leaves his father and his mother and clings to his wife." It is not a future but a general present: it continues the yiqtol of what always happens (Gesenius §112m).
8. *wəlōʾ ṯirṣāḥ*. The negative *lōʾ* breaks the chain, so the verb is a yiqtol, as in the commandments (lesson 4.3).
9. In *wəhālaḵtî́* "and I will go" the stress is on the ending *-tî*; in *hāláḵtî* "I went" it is on the syllable before (Gesenius §49h).
