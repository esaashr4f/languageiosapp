## The idea

An **adverb** is a word that tells you how, when, where or how far something happens: "there", "now", "thus", "together". You have already met a dozen of them as single words: *šām* "there", *ʿattâ* "now", *kōh* and *kēn* "thus", *ʿôḏ* "still", *ʾāz* "then", *məʾōḏ* "very", *pōh* "here". This lesson gathers them, adds the commonest missing ones, and shows where they stand in the clause.

The first thing to know is that Hebrew has very few adverbs that were born as adverbs. Gesenius says only *lōʾ* "not" and a handful of words such as *šām* "there" are "of obscure origin" (§100a). Almost every other adverb is a word from another class put to adverbial use:

- **a noun used bare**, with no preposition: *məʾōḏ* "very" is a noun meaning "might, strength"; *yaḥad* "together" is a noun meaning "union"; *tāmîḏ* "continually" is a noun meaning "continuity" (§100c; bdb);
- **a noun with a preposition**: *ləḇaddô* "alone", literally "to his separation" (§100b);
- **a short demonstrative word**: *kēn*, *kāḵâ* "thus", *ʾaḵ* "only", *ʾāḵēn* "surely", *ʾāz* "then" (§100i);
- **a noun with an ending *-ām***: *yômām* "by day" from *yôm*, *ḥinnām* "for nothing" from *ḥēn* "favour", *rêqām* "empty-handed" from *rêq* "empty", *ʾāmnām* "truly" (§100g).

Why does a bare noun work as an adverb? In the older Semitic languages a noun had case endings, and the accusative was also the case of time, place and manner: Gesenius calls it "the adverbial case of the Semites" (§100c, §118m). Arabic still uses it so. Hebrew lost the endings, but the habit stayed: a noun standing loose in the clause, with no preposition, can say when, where or how. Lesson 5.2 builds on this.

The *-ām* ending is disputed. Gesenius treats it as a word-forming ending, but reports that others see an old accusative in it, like the Arabic *-an* of adverbs (§100h). Nobody knows for certain.

A **focus particle** is a word that picks out one part of the clause and says something about it: "also this", "only this", "even this". Hebrew has three common ones: *gam* "also, even", *raq* "only", *ʾaḵ* "only; surely". A fourth, *ʾap̄* "also, even", is rarer in prose.

## How it is built

The rules of placement come from what each word does.

1. **A focus particle stands directly before the word it focuses.** *Gam-ʾeḥāyw* is "his brothers too", with *gam* before "his brothers". English can say "his brothers went too", with "too" at the end; Hebrew keeps the particle at the front of its word.
2. **A restricting particle stands at the head of the clause when the restriction covers the whole statement.** *Raq* and *ʾaḵ* then mean "only, but, however": "only (this): the blood you shall not eat". BDB lists this as the restrictive sense of *ʾaḵ*, "in contrast to what precedes", and cites Genesis 9:4 for it.
3. **A sentence adverb comes first.** *ʿAttâ* "now", *wəʿattâ* "and now", *ʾāḵēn* "surely" and *ʾāz* "then" open their clause. *Wəʿattâ* often means "so, therefore" rather than clock time: it turns from what has been said to what must now be done.
4. **Adverbs of place and manner follow the verb**, usually after the subject or object it belongs with: *wayyēšeḇ šām* "and he settled there".
5. **A degree word follows its adjective**: *ṭôḇ məʾōḏ* "very good", never before it.

### Building a sentence

"Only the blood you shall not eat":

1. The restriction covers the whole command, so *raq* comes first.
2. The object is fronted for contrast: *haddām* "the blood".
3. A permanent prohibition is *lōʾ* + yiqtol (lesson 4.8): *lōʾ ṯōʾḵēlû*.
4. *Raq haddām lōʾ ṯōʾḵēlû* (Deuteronomy 12:16).

## Where it applies

- **Place:** *šām* "there", *miššām* "from there", *pōh* and *kōh* "here", *hălōm* "hither" (example 1, example 4). Motion "to there" is *šāmmâ*, with the directional ending (lesson 5.2).
- **Time:** *ʿattâ* "now", *ʾāz* "then, at that time", *ʿôḏ* "still, again", *ṭerem* "not yet", *tāmîḏ* "continually", *yômām* "by day", *pitʾōm* "suddenly" (example 2). *ʿAḏ-hēnnâ* "until now" is *hēnnâ* "here" used of time (Gesenius §100f).
- **After *ʾāz* the yiqtol can refer to the past:** *ʾāz yāšîr-mōšeh* "then Moses sang" (Exodus 15:1), *ʾāz yəḏabbēr yəhôšuaʿ* "then Joshua spoke" (Joshua 10:12). Lesson 4.3 introduced this; Gesenius explains it as the yiqtol presenting the action as it unfolds (§107c).
- **Manner:** *kēn*, *kōh*, *kāḵâ* "thus, so"; *yaḥdāw*, *yaḥad* "together"; *ləḇaddô* "alone" (examples 9 and 12).
- **The prophetic formula:** *kōh ʾāmar YHWH* "thus says the LORD" (lesson 2.6). *Kōh* points forward to the words that follow; *kēn* usually points back, as in *wayhî-ḵēn* "and it was so".
- **Degree:** *məʾōḏ* after an adjective or verb; doubled *bimʾōḏ məʾōḏ* "exceedingly" (Genesis 17:2).
- **Discourse "now":** *wəʿattâ* introducing a request or conclusion (example 3).
- **Truth and surprise:** *ʾāḵēn* "surely, so after all" (example 8); *ʾaḵ* in its other sense "surely"; *ʾāmnām* or *ʾumnām* "truly", in a question "really?" (Genesis 18:13).
- **Restriction:** *raq* and *ʾaḵ* "only, but" (examples 6 and 7); with a noun, "only Noah": *ʾaḵ-nōaḥ* (Genesis 7:23).
- **Addition:** *gam* "also, even", before the word it focuses (example 5); *gam ... gam* "both ... and" (lesson 2.7). *ʾAp̄* "also, even" is commoner in poetry; in prose it is mostly *ʾap̄ kî* "even though, how much more" (lesson 5.8).
- **Cost and result:** *ḥinnām* "for nothing" (example 10), *rêqām* "empty-handed" (example 11).

## Watch out

**Do not put *gam* at the end, as English puts "too".** *\*wayyēləḵû ʾeḥāyw gam* is wrong. *Gam* stands before the word it focuses: *wayyēləḵû gam-ʾeḥāyw*.

**Do not put *məʾōḏ* before an adjective by analogy with "very good", French *très bon* or Spanish *muy bueno*.** *\*məʾōḏ ṭôḇ* is wrong. The noun "might" follows what it measures: *ṭôḇ məʾōḏ* (Genesis 1:31).

**Do not translate *wəʿattâ* as "and now" every time.** At the start of a speech's main point it is "so", "therefore": *wəʿattâ yiḡdal-nāʾ* "so let ... be great". Read it as a hinge, not a clock.

**Do not confuse *raq* "only" with *raḵ* "tender" (Genesis 18:7), or *ʾaḵ* with *ʾāḥ* "brother".** *Raq* ends in *qof*, *raḵ* in final *kaf*; *ʾaḵ* has *kaf*, *ʾāḥ* has *ḥêṯ*.

**Do not take *ʾaḵ* at the head of a clause as "only" in the sense of "merely".** It often means "but, however", correcting what came before (example 7), and sometimes "surely". Read the context.

**Do not add *bə-* to an adverbial noun that does not need one.** *\*bəyômām* is wrong for "by day": *yômām* already carries the adverbial sense. Gesenius notes *bəp̄itʾōm* once, in Chronicles, a late text (§100h).

**Do not read *kōh* as "thus" when it stands after a verb of standing or going.** In *hiṯyaṣṣēḇ kōh* it is "here" (example 4).

**Do not read *ʾāz* + yiqtol as a future by reflex.** *ʾĀz yāšîr* is "then he sang" (Gesenius §107c). Only the context tells you whether a yiqtol after *ʾāz* is past or future.

## Connections

Arabic has the same habit of using a noun in the accusative as an adverb: *jiddan* "very", from *jidd* "seriousness", *ʾabadan* "never, ever", *ṣabāḥan* "in the morning". Hebrew lost the ending but kept the use, so *məʾōḏ* "very" is a noun "might" in the same role as *jiddan*. Spanish forms adverbs from a noun in a similar way: *-mente* in *claramente* is the Latin noun *mens* "mind", "with a clear mind".

2. Say "his brothers too fell down before him", putting *gam* in the right place.
3. Why is *\*məʾōḏ ṭôḇ* wrong, and what is the right order?
4. Translate *raq haddām lōʾ ṯōʾḵēlû* and explain why *raq* is at the head of the clause.
5. *Ḥinnām*, *rêqām*, *yômām*: give the noun each is built from and the meaning.
6. Translate *wayyiwwāṯēr yaʿăqōḇ ləḇaddô*. What changes in Elijah's "I alone am left"?
7. In *hiṯyaṣṣēḇ kōh*, is *kōh* "thus" or "here"? Give the reason.
8. Translate *ʾāḵēn nôḏaʿ haddāḇār* and say what *ʾāḵēn* adds.
9. A student translates *ʾāz yəḏabbēr yəhôšuaʿ* as "then Joshua will speak". Correct him.
10. Why can a bare noun like *məʾōḏ* or *tāmîḏ* serve as an adverb in Hebrew?

## Worked answers

1. "And he settled there"; "and he went away from there". *Min* "from" joins *šām* and its *n* assimilates to the *š*, which is doubled: *miššām* (lesson 2.5).
2. *Wayyippəlû gam-ʾeḥāyw ləp̄ānāyw*. *Gam* stands directly before "his brothers", the word it focuses, joined by maqqef. The verse itself has the brothers going first: *wayyēləḵû gam-ʾeḥāyw wayyippəlû ləp̄ānāyw*.
3. *Məʾōḏ* is a noun "might" used adverbially, and it follows what it measures: *ṭôḇ məʾōḏ* "very good". The English, French and Spanish order does not apply.
4. "Only the blood you must not eat." The restriction qualifies the whole command, which limits a permission given just before, so *raq* opens the clause; *haddām* is fronted for contrast.
5. *Ḥēn* "favour": *ḥinnām* "for nothing". *Rêq* "empty": *rêqām* "empty-handed, in vain". *Yôm* "day": *yômām* "by day". All add *-ām* (Gesenius §100g); whether *-ām* is an old accusative is disputed (§100h).
6. "Jacob was left alone." Elijah says *wāʾiwwāṯēr ʾănî ləḇaddî* "and I alone was left" (1 Kings 19:10): the verb is the 1SG wayyiqtol with *wā-*, and the suffix on *ləḇaḏ* changes to the first person, *ləḇaddî*.
7. "Here." After a verb of standing, *kōh* is an adverb of place (bdb lists both senses); "thus" would make no sense with "stand".
8. "So the thing is known after all." *ʾĀḵēn* marks a statement as true, often one the speaker has just realised (bdb: "surely, truly; but in fact").
9. "Then Joshua spoke." After *ʾāz* the yiqtol can describe a past action (Gesenius §107c; lesson 4.3). The context of Joshua 10 is a past battle.
10. Older Semitic marked time, place and manner with the accusative case ending; Hebrew lost the endings but kept the use, so a noun standing without a preposition can work as an adverb (Gesenius §100c, §118m).
