## The idea

Lesson 5.1 showed that many Hebrew adverbs are nouns standing loose in the clause, because the old Semitic accusative case was also the case of time, place and manner. This lesson takes that idea to its two most productive uses.

The first is an ending. Hebrew nouns lost their case endings, with one exception: an unstressed *-â*, written with final *hê*, that means "towards". *Miṣrayim* is "Egypt"; *miṣraymâ* is "to Egypt". *Hāʾōhel* is "the tent"; *hāʾōhĕlâ* is "into the tent". Grammars call it the **directional *hê*** or *hê locale*; the guide glosses it DIR. You have met it several times already: *šəḵemâ* "to Shechem", *dōṯāynâ* "to Dothan", *habbōrâ* "into the pit".

Why does one ending survive when the others vanished? Gesenius holds that "it can hardly be doubted" that this *-â* is the old accusative ending *-a*, the same as the Arabic accusative *-a*, kept because the accusative was the case of the goal of motion (§90a, c; §118b, d). Its written *hê* is a vowel letter, which is why it has no mappiq (lesson 1.7). And it is almost always unstressed (§90i), so it does not shift the stress or change the vowels of the word: *ʾarṣâ* keeps the stress where *ʾereṣ* has it, and the *segol* vowels of the segholate become the base form *ʾarṣ-* (§90i).

The second use needs no ending at all. A bare noun can say **when**: *ʿereḇ* "in the evening", *bōqer* "in the morning", *hayyôm* "today", *happaʿam* "this time". It can say **how long**: *kol-yəmê ḥayyêḵā* "all the days of your life". And after a verb of motion, a bare noun can still say **where to**: *nēṣēʾ haśśāḏeh* "let us go out into the field" (Gesenius §118d–k). English needs "in", "on", "for" or "to" for all of these. Hebrew often does not.

## How it is built

The directional ending is attached in four steps.

1. **Take the noun in the form it would have before an ending.** For most nouns that is the absolute: *ḥārān* → *ḥārānâ*, *miṣrayim* → *miṣraymâ* (the helping vowel *i* drops). Segholates use their base: *ʾereṣ* → *ʾarṣâ*, *har* "mountain" → *hārâ* in *hāhārâ*. Feminines in *-â* bring back their old *-t*: *tirṣâ* → *tirṣāṯâ* "to Tirzah" (Gesenius §90i).
2. **Add *-â*, unstressed.** The word keeps its stress: *miṣráymâ*, *ʾárṣâ*.
3. **Add the article if the place is definite.** *Hāʾōhĕlâ* "into the tent", *habbayṯâ* "into the house", *hāhārâ* "to the hill country".
4. **Or put the noun in construct before its genitive.** *Bêṯâ yôsēp̄* "to Joseph's house"; *ʾarṣâ miṣrayim* "to the land of Egypt" (Exodus 4:20; Gesenius §90c).

For bare time and place words, the steps are shorter:

1. Decide what the noun answers: when, how long, or where to.
2. Leave the preposition out. *Bōqer*, *ʿereḇ*, *hayyôm*, *māḥār*, *kol-hayyôm* "all day".
3. Put it where the clause allows. Time words often come first, to set the scene: *ʿereḇ wîḏaʿtem* "in the evening you will know".

### Building a sentence

"Abram went down to Egypt to sojourn there":

1. "Went down": wayyiqtol of *yāraḏ*: *wayyēreḏ* (lesson 4.14).
2. The subject follows: *ʾaḇrām*.
3. "To Egypt": the goal of motion, so *miṣrayim* + *-â*: *miṣraymâ*.
4. "To sojourn": *lə* + infinitive of *gûr*: *lāḡûr* (lesson 4.15).
5. "There": *šām*. With a verb of rest the plain form is used, not *šāmmâ*.
6. *Wayyēreḏ ʾaḇrām miṣraymâ lāḡûr šām* (Genesis 12:10).

## Where it applies

- **Goal of motion with a place name:** *ḥārānâ*, *miṣraymâ*, *šəḵemâ* (examples 1–2; lesson 4.12).
- **Goal with a common noun and the article:** *hāʾōhĕlâ*, *hāhārâ*, *habbayṯâ* "into the house" (10× in WLC), *haḥûṣâ* "outside" (examples 3, 6; Genesis 15:5).
- **Goal with a construct noun:** *bêṯâ yôsēp̄* (example 4), *ʾarṣâ miṣrayim* "to the land of Egypt" (Exodus 4:20; Gesenius §90c).
- **Points of the compass:** *ṣāp̄ōnâ wāneḡbâ wāqēḏmâ wāyāmmâ* "northward, southward, eastward and westward" (Genesis 13:14). *Yāmmâ* "seaward" is "westward" (Gesenius §90c): the sea lies to the west. *Qeḏem* means both "front" and "east" (bdb). *Neḡeḇ* is the south country of Judah, and so "south".
- **"Down" and "up":** *ʾarṣâ* "to the ground" (example 5); *ləmaʿlâ* "upwards", with *lə-* added (Gesenius §90e); *haššāmaymâ* "towards the sky", the ending on a plural (Genesis 15:5).
- **Adverbs with the ending:** *šāmmâ* "thither" (lesson 4.20), *ʾānâ* "whither?", *hēnnâ* "hither".
- **With a preposition as well:** *lə-*, *ʿaḏ-* and even *min* can stand before a word with *-â*, though the ending is then redundant: *ʿaḏ-ʾăp̄ēqâ* "as far as Aphek" (§90e).
- **Time, rarely:** *miyyāmîm yāmîmâ* "from year to year" (1 Samuel 1:3; lesson 4.6; Gesenius §90h).
- **Bare goal after a verb of motion:** *nēṣēʾ haśśāḏeh* (example 7; §118f).
- **Bare place "where":** *bêṯ ʾāḇîḵ* "in your father's house" (Genesis 38:11), *peṯaḥ-hāʾōhel* "at the entrance of the tent" (Genesis 18:1). Gesenius notes these are almost always construct nouns, and suggests the preposition was dropped to avoid *bə-bêṯ*, *bə-peṯaḥ* (§118g).
- **Bare time "when":** *ʿereḇ*, *bōqer*, *laylâ* "at night", *ṣohŏrayim* "at noon", *hayyôm*, *māḥār*, *happaʿam* (examples 8–10, 12; §118i).
- **Bare time "how long":** *kol-yəmê ḥayyêḵā* (example 11), *kol-hayyôm* "all day" (Genesis 6:5; lesson 5.1).

## Watch out

**Do not use the directional ending for rest.** *\*wayyēšeḇ šāmmâ* for "he stayed there" is wrong. *Šāmmâ* is "to there"; rest is *šām*. The same holds for nouns: "he was in Egypt" is *bəmiṣrayim*, not *\*miṣraymâ*. Gesenius notes a few weakened uses (§90d), but in prose the rule holds.

**Do not stress the ending.** It is *miṣráymâ*, *ʾárṣâ*, *ḥārā́nâ*, with the stress where the word had it. A stressed *-â* is a different ending: the feminine (*malkâ* "queen") or the cohortative (lesson 4.8). The written forms can look alike: read the context and, in pointed text, the accents.

**Do not confuse the directional *-â* with the feminine *-â*.** *Ḥārānâ* is "to Haran", not a feminine noun. The feminine ending would turn into *-aṯ* in construct; the directional ending does not, and stands even on a construct (*bêṯâ yôsēp̄*).

**Do not add *-â* to a person.** *\*śārâ* + *-â* is impossible; "to Sarah" is *ʾel-śārâ* (example 3). The ending is for places and directions.

**Do not expect *bayṯâ* in prose.** Gesenius cites *bayṯâ* "homewards", but WLC has it only twice, in a psalm and in Aramaic. Prose says *habbayṯâ* "into the house" or *bêṯâ* + a name.

**Do not supply "in" or "on" in Hebrew by analogy with English.** *\*bəhayyôm* for "today" is wrong: *hayyôm* alone is "today", while *bayyôm hahûʾ* "on that day" is a different expression with its own *bə-*. Likewise *ʿereḇ* alone is "in the evening". If you do use a preposition, prose uses *bāʿereḇ*, with the article (16× in WLC); *bəʿereḇ* without it occurs once, in Proverbs.

**Do not read *hayyôm* always as "today".** It can also mean "that day", the day in question, or "by day" (Gesenius §118i): *kəḥōm hayyôm* is "in the heat of the day" (Genesis 18:1).

**Do not read every bare noun after a verb of motion as its object.** In *wayyēṣəʾû haśśāḏeh* "they went out into the field", the field is the goal, not something acted on. *Yāṣāʾ* "go out" takes no object.

## Connections

Arabic keeps what Hebrew lost: its accusative *-a* still marks time, place and manner on any noun, as in *ṣabāḥan* "in the morning" and *laylan* "at night", and *yawman* "one day". Hebrew *-â* is that same *-a*, frozen on the goal of motion. Spanish *hacia* and French *vers* do the job with a preposition, where Hebrew puts it at the end of the noun.

## Worked answers

1. *Ḥārānâ*: the ending is added to the absolute. *Miṣraymâ*: the helping vowel of *-ayim* drops. *ʾArṣâ*: the segholate returns to its base *ʾarṣ-*, as before suffixes (Gesenius §90i; lesson 3.4).
2. "Abraham hurried into the tent, to Sarah." A place takes the directional ending; a person as the goal takes *ʾel* (Gesenius §118e).
3. *Šāmmâ* means "to there", for motion; staying needs plain *šām*: *wayyēšeḇ šām* "and he stayed there" (Genesis 26:17; lesson 5.1).
4. "Into Joseph's house." The ending sits on the construct *bêṯ*, between it and its genitive, which is possible because the *-â* is an old case ending, not a separate word (Gesenius §90c).
5. "In the evening you will know"; "tomorrow I will stand". Both open with a bare time noun and no preposition, an old accusative of time (Gesenius §118i).
6. The goal. *Yāṣāʾ* "go out" takes no object, and the first half of the verse, *nēṣēʾ haśśāḏeh*, is cited by Gesenius as an accusative of place "whither" (§118f).
7. *Wayyaʿal ʾaḇrām hāhārâ*: the wayyiqtol, the subject, then the goal with the article and the directional ending, as in *hāhārâ himmālēṭ* (example 6). Constructed; the words are attested separately.
8. *Hayyôm* alone means "today"; the bare noun is already adverbial (Gesenius §118i). *Bayyôm hahûʾ* "on that day" has *bə-* because it is a different expression, with a demonstrative.
9. "Today" (*hayyôm ʾattem yōṣəʾîm*, Exodus 13:4); "of the day", the day in question (*kəḥōm hayyôm* "in the heat of the day", Genesis 18:1).
10. The *hê* is a vowel letter for *â*, not a consonant *h*, so it has no mappiq (lesson 1.7). The ending was once a vowel *-a*, and the *hê* only spells it.
