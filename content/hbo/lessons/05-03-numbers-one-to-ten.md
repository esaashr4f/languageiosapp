## The idea

You already know *ʾeḥāḏ* "one" (lesson 2.1) and have seen *šənêhem* "the two of them" (lesson 4.18). This lesson gives the numbers up to ten and the rules for joining them to a noun.

The numbers fall into two groups, and the reason lies in what they are.

**One and two agree with their noun, like adjectives.** *ʾEḥāḏ* is masculine, *ʾaḥaṯ* feminine; *šənayim* is masculine, *šəttayim* feminine. *ʾEḥāḏ* behaves as an adjective and follows its noun: *ḥălôm ʾeḥāḏ* "one dream". "Two" is a noun in the dual (lesson 3.2), a "pair": *šənê hammalʾāḵîm* "the pair of the messengers", "the two messengers" (Gesenius §97a).

**Three to ten are nouns too, and their gender runs the other way.** With a masculine noun you use the form that looks feminine, with *-â*: *šəlōšâ ḇānîm* "three sons". With a feminine noun you use the short form with no ending: *šālôš bānôṯ* "three daughters". Gesenius calls this a "strange phenomenon" (§97a). It is not a Hebrew quirk: Arabic and the other Semitic languages do the same (§97c).

Why? The numbers were once abstract nouns, like English "a trio", "a dozen", and a "trio of sons" was a noun followed by its genitive. Gesenius reports two explanations of the reversed gender and does not settle between them (§97a):

- **The older view:** the original number nouns were all feminine, with *-â*, like many abstract nouns. When numbers came to be used like adjectives, a new shorter form was made for feminine nouns, and the old *-â* form stayed with the masculines.
- **Reckendorf's view,** which Gesenius calls "much more intelligible": the original numbers had no gender ending. The feminine-looking forms were first created in the teens (13–19) for use with masculine nouns, and the distinction spread from there to 3–10.

Either way, the practical rule is the same, and it is fixed. Treat the *-â* form as "the masculine-noun form" and the short form as "the feminine-noun form", and forget what the endings look like.

## How it is built

To count a noun from three to ten:

1. **Find the noun's gender in the singular.** *Šānâ* "year" is feminine, even though its plural is *šānîm*; *ʾāḇ* "father" is masculine even though its plural is *ʾāḇôṯ*. Gender belongs to the noun, not to the plural ending (lesson 3.2).
2. **Choose the number form.** Masculine noun: the *-â* form (*šəlōšâ*, *ʾarbāʿâ*, *ḥămiššâ*, *šiššâ*, *šiḇʿâ*, *šəmōnâ*, *tišʿâ*, *ʿăśārâ*). Feminine noun: the short form (*šālōš*, *ʾarbaʿ*, *ḥāmēš*, *šēš*, *šeḇaʿ*, *šəmōneh*, *tēšaʿ*, *ʿeśer*).
3. **Put the noun in the plural** (Gesenius §134e). *ʾArbāʿâ rāʾšîm* "four heads", never *\*ʾarbāʿâ rōʾš*.
4. **Choose the order.** Most often the number comes first, either in the absolute (*šəlōšâ ḇānîm*) or in construct (*šēšeṯ yāmîm* "six days"). In lists it can follow the noun (Gesenius §134a–c).
5. **For "the three ...", put the number in construct before the definite noun:** *šənê hammalʾāḵîm* "the two messengers", *šəlōšeṯ hayyāmîm* "the three days".

The construct forms follow lesson 3.5: the *-â* of the masculine-noun forms becomes *-aṯ* or *-eṯ*: *šəlōšâ* → *šəlōšeṯ*, *šiššâ* → *šēšeṯ*, *šiḇʿâ* → *šiḇʿaṯ*. "Two" loses its dual ending: *šənayim* → *šənê*, *šəttayim* → *šətê*, and takes suffixes on that base: *šənêhem* "the two of them".

### Building a phrase

"Seven sons and three daughters":

1. *Bēn* "son" is masculine: seven with a masculine noun is *šiḇʿâ*. Plural *bānîm*: *šiḇʿâ ḇānîm* (soft *ḇ* after the vowel).
2. *Baṯ* "daughter" is feminine: three is *šālōš*. Plural *bānôṯ*: *šālôš bānôṯ*.
3. Joined with *wə-*: *šiḇʿâ ḇānîm wəšālôš bānôṯ* (Job 1:2).

## Where it applies

- **One, after its noun, agreeing:** *ḥălôm ʾeḥāḏ* "one dream" (Genesis 41:26), *yôm ʾeḥāḏ* "one day" (Genesis 1:5); feminine *ʾaḥaṯ* (example 9).
- **One as "a certain":** *ʾîš ʾeḥāḏ* "a certain man" (lesson 2.1).
- **"One of", "the one ... the other":** *ʾaḥaṯ miṣṣalʿōṯāyw* (example 9), *ʾaḥaḏ hehārîm* "one of the mountains" with construct *ʾaḥaḏ* (Genesis 22:2; Gesenius §97a); *hāʾaḥaṯ ... haššēnîṯ* (example 1).
- **Two, agreeing, usually construct before the noun:** *šətê nāšîm*, *šənê hammalʾāḵîm* (example 1; Genesis 19:1).
- **Two as a dual noun instead of a number:** *šənāṯayim* "two years" (example 6), *yômayim* "two days" (Exodus 21:21; lesson 3.2). Only nouns of time and measure, and pairs like hands and eyes, do this.
- **Three to ten, reversed gender, plural noun:** examples 2–5, 7, 10–12.
- **Construct before a definite noun, "the three/seven ...":** *šiḇʿaṯ hayyāmîm* "the seven days" (Genesis 7:10).
- **With a suffix, "the two of us/them":** *šənêhem* (example 8; 1 Samuel 20:11), *šənênû* "the two of us" (Genesis 31:37).
- **After the noun, in lists and measurements:** Gesenius notes this order especially in lists (§134c).
- **As adverbs, "once, twice, seven times":** *šeḇaʿ* alone can be "seven times" (Gesenius §100f); *šeḇaʿ pəʿāmîm* "seven times" with *paʿam* (Genesis 33:3).
- **Approximation, "about":** *kə-* before a number: *kaʿăśereṯ hayyāmîm* "about ten days later" (1 Samuel 25:38; lesson 2.5).

## Watch out

**Do not make three to ten agree like adjectives.** *\*šālōš bānîm* for "three sons" follows the logic of Spanish *tres hijos*/*tres hijas* (no change) or of Hebrew adjectives (agreement). Both fail. The masculine noun takes the *-â* form: *šəlōšâ ḇānîm*.

**Do not choose the form by the plural ending.** *\*ḥămiššâ šānîm* is wrong, because *šānâ* is feminine: *ḥāmēš šānîm* (example 6). Likewise *ʾāḇ* "father" is masculine despite *ʾāḇôṯ*.

**Do not use a singular noun with three to ten.** *\*šiḇʿâ bēn* is wrong: *šiḇʿâ ḇānîm*. The singular is used only after eleven and above with certain nouns (lesson 5.4).

**Do not use *šənayim* for a pair of things that Hebrew counts with the dual.** "Two years" is *šənāṯayim*, not normally *šətê šānîm*. Check the noun: *šənê* and *šətê* are used with most nouns, the dual with a few nouns of time and measure.

**Do not put *ʾeḥāḏ* before its noun.** *\*ʾeḥāḏ ḥălôm* is wrong; it is an adjective: *ḥălôm ʾeḥāḏ*. The construct *ʾaḥaḏ* comes before a noun only in the sense "one of": *ʾaḥaḏ hehārîm*.

**Do not read *šeḇaʿ* "seven" into every *š-b-ʿ*.** The same letters spell the verb *nišbaʿ* "swear" (lesson 4.20), and the narrator explains the name Beersheba, *bəʾēr šāḇaʿ*, by the oath sworn there: *kî šām nišbəʿû šənêhem* "for there the two of them swore" (Genesis 21:31). Read the pointing and the context: the number has *segol*, *šeḇaʿ*, and stands before a noun.

**Do not read the construct *-eṯ* as a feminine noun ending.** *Šēšeṯ yāmîm* is "six days", with the masculine-noun form in construct; it is not "a six (f.)".

## Connections

Arabic has exactly the same reversed agreement: *thalāthatu rijālin* "three men" with the feminine-looking *-a(tu)*, *thalāthu nisāʾin* "three women" without it, and like Hebrew it takes a plural noun in the genitive after three to ten. Hebrew *šəlōšâ*, *šālōš* and Arabic *thalātha*, *thalāth* are the same word. Spanish and French show no such reversal; their numbers above two have no gender at all.

5. Translate *wəlô šəmōnâ ḇānîm*. Where is the verb?
6. Correct *\*ʾarbāʿâ ʾammôṯ* for "four cubits".
7. Why is *ʾeḥāḏ* placed after its noun but *šənê* before it?
8. Translate *wayyiqqaḥ ʾaḥaṯ miṣṣalʿōṯāyw* and explain the gender of *ʾaḥaṯ*.
9. Say "ten camels" and "ten cows" (*gāmāl* m., *pārâ* f.).
10. Give the two explanations Gesenius reports for the reversed gender of three to ten.

## Worked answers

1. *Šəlōšâ ḇānîm*, *šālōš bānôṯ* (*šālôš* in Job 1:2). *Bēn* is masculine, so it takes the form with *-â*; *baṯ* is feminine, so it takes the short form (Gesenius §97a).
2. *Šeḇaʿ šānîm*, the short form, because *šānâ* is feminine; the plural ending *-îm* does not change its gender (example 12).
3. "Six days you shall work." *Šēšeṯ* is the construct of *šiššâ*, "a six of days" (Gesenius §134a).
4. *Šənê hammalʾāḵîm* (Genesis 19:1): "two" in construct before the definite noun. *Šənêhem*: the construct base with the suffix *-hem* (Genesis 22:6).
5. "And he had eight sons." There is no verb: possession is *lə-* + suffix in a verbless clause (lesson 3.9).
6. *ʾArbaʿ ʾammôṯ* (Deuteronomy 3:11). *ʾAmmâ* is feminine, so four takes the short form.
7. *ʾEḥāḏ* is an adjective and follows its noun like other adjectives (lesson 3.11). *Šənayim* is a dual noun, "a pair", and as a noun it stands in construct before what it counts (Gesenius §97a).
8. "And he took one of his ribs." *Ṣēlāʿ* is feminine, so "one" is *ʾaḥaṯ*; *min* gives "one of".
9. *ʿĂśārâ ḡəmallîm* (Genesis 24:10); *ʿeśer pārôṯ* (constructed by the rule: *pārâ* is feminine, so the short form).
10. (a) The number nouns were originally feminine abstracts; a shorter form was later made for feminine nouns, leaving the *-â* forms with masculines. (b) Reckendorf: the original forms had no gender; feminine-looking forms arose first in the teens for masculine nouns and spread to 3–10 (Gesenius §97a). The question is not settled.
