## 1. Variety taught

- **Base:** modern standard literary Georgian (სალიტერატურო ქართული), the written and educated spoken norm centred on Tbilisi, as described by hewitt1995 (§1.4, §1.8 "Scope of the Present Work") and taught by aronson1990. It grew out of the Kartlian and Kakhetian dialects of the east (hewitt1995 §1.4; the dialect names are glottolog's).
- **Spoken register:** everyday Tbilisi speech is taught alongside, labelled `colloquial` in the note of each example. Typical differences to flag: the copula enclitic -ა for არის (*ეს სახლია* "this is a house"), dropped long case forms, the quotative particles, shortened question forms, and loanwords from Russian (kat-07-09). Do not invent colloquial forms: take them from a source or mark them `(unverified)`.
- **Older and other varieties:** Old Georgian (5th to 11th centuries) and Middle Georgian (Rustaveli) appear only in kat-07-11 and the readings kat-08-05 to kat-08-07, labelled as such. Pre-reform spellings in 19th-century texts (e.g. ტფილისი for თბილისი, -ზედ for -ზე, -დამ for -დან in kawikisource's Chavchavadze) are kept in the readings and explained there. Dialect forms (Gurian, Imeretian, Khevsur, Ingilo, Fereydan) are named in kat-07-10 only.
- **Why this variety:** it is the language of Georgian schools, media, signage and almost all writing; every grammar and dictionary listed describes it; and it is what a learner meets in Georgia.

## 2. Phoneme inventory

Sources: hewitt1995 §§2.1–2.4 (pp. 19–30, publisher's preview, opened); aronson1990 Lesson 1 §§1.1–1.9 (opened); IPA values as given on wiktionary's transliteration page and wikipedia (Romanization of Georgian). Where they differ, both are given.

### Vowels

Five vowels, no length contrast, no reduction in unstressed syllables (hewitt1995 §2.1.2, §2.4.1; aronson1990 §1.7; wals 2A "average (5-6)").

| Letter | Translit | IPA | Description |
|---|---|---|---|
| ა | a | [ɑ] (wiktionary, wikipedia); [a] "low, open, slightly fronted" (aronson1990 §1.7) | open a, as in Spanish *casa* |
| ე | e | [ɛ] | open e, as in *get* |
| ი | i | [i] (wiktionary); "between close and half close" (aronson1990) | as in Spanish *si* |
| ო | o | [ɔ] | open o, as in German *Glocken* |
| უ | u | [u] | as in Spanish *tu* |

Every vowel is a syllable; there are no diphthongs in the native analysis (a sequence like ია is two syllables) and no syllabic consonants: "a Georgian word has as many syllables as it has vowels" (aronson1990 §1.8).

### Consonants

28 consonant phonemes (hewitt1995 §2.1.1). Stops and affricates come in threes: **voiced**, **voiceless aspirated** and **voiceless ejective** (glottalised); the uvular has only the ejective (wals 7A "ejectives only", 4A voicing in plosives and fricatives).

| Place | Voiced | Aspirated | Ejective | Fricatives | Sonorants |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Labial | ბ b [b] | ფ p [pʰ] | პ ṗ [pʼ] | ვ v [v], see below | მ m [m] |
| Dental | დ d [d] | თ t [tʰ] | ტ ṭ [tʼ] | | ნ n [n], ლ l [l], რ r [ɾ] |
| Alveolar affricate | ძ ʒ [dz] | ც c [tsʰ] | წ c̣ [tsʼ] | ზ z [z], ს s [s] | |
| Postalveolar affricate | ჯ ǯ [dʒ] | ჩ č [tʃʰ] | ჭ č̣ [tʃʼ] | ჟ ž [ʒ], შ š [ʃ] | |
| Velar | გ g [ɡ] | ქ k [kʰ] | კ ḳ [kʼ] | | |
| Uvular | | | ყ q̇ [qʼ] | ღ ɣ [ʁ] (wikipedia) or [ɣ] (wiktionary); ხ x [χ] (wikipedia) or [x] (wiktionary) | |
| Glottal | | | | ჰ h [h] | |

Points to teach, each with its source:

- **Ejectives** are made with a closed glottis: "simultaneously pronouncing a glottal stop and the corresponding stop" (aronson1990 §1.1). To English ears they often sound voiced (aronson1990 §1.1). The contrast is lexical: ქარი *kari* "wind" vs კარი *ḳari* "door" (both checked in wiktionary; hewitt1995 §3.1.1 also cites კარი).
- **Aspirates** are like English initial p, t, k (aronson1990 §1.1).
- **ყ q̇** varies from a uvular ejective stop through an affricate [qχʼ] to an ejective fricative [χʼ] (aronson1990 §1.3); hewitt1995 §2.2.1: "regularly accompanied by strong friction to produce the articulation [qx']".
- **ხ x and ღ ɣ** are further back than German *ach* (postvelar/uvular) (aronson1990 §1.2; hewitt1995 §2.1.1 lists them as "dorso-velar/uvular"). The two transcriptions [x ɣ] and [χ ʁ] describe the same sounds; teach them as uvular with a note.
- **ვ v** is [v] initially and between vowels, [w] after consonants and at syllable end, [β] in some positions, and [f] or [ɸ] before voiceless consonants: ვწერ *vc̣er* "I write" [fcer] (aronson1990 §1.5; hewitt1995 §2.2.1, with the examples *v-tamaš-ob* [ftamašob] "I play", *bavšv-s*). It is generally lost before or after labials and before o, u (aronson1990 §1.11.1; hewitt1995 §2.2.1: past participles in -e-ul- from -ev-).
- **რ r** is a single flap, as in Spanish *pero* (aronson1990 §1.6). **ლ l** is clear before i, e and velarised elsewhere (aronson1990 §1.6).
- **ჰ h** is written before some stops in grammatical forms (third-person object marker) but is "generally not pronounced except in very careful speech" (aronson1990 Lesson 1 note 4).
- **Final voiced stops**: aronson1990 §1.1 says they are "generally pronounced as the corresponding aspirates"; hewitt1995 §2.2.1 says the tendency is "slight" and "hardly a pronounced or widespread phenomenon". See section 6.
- **Clusters**: Georgian allows very long initial clusters; Vogt (cited in hewitt1995 §2.1.1.1) counts 740 initial complexes, up to six consonants. **Harmonic clusters** (a labial, dental or affricate stop followed by a velar or uvular of the same type: ბგ, დგ, თქ, ტყ, ფხ, წყ, ჭყ...) are released once and behave as single units (hewitt1995 §2.1.1.1, after Gamq'relidze; aronson1990 §1.1, §1.9). Non-harmonic clusters have separate releases: თბილისი [tʰbilisi] (aronson1990 §1.9). Example clusters to use: მწვრთნელი *mc̣vrtneli* "trainer" (wiktionary, checked); გვფრცქვნი *gvprckvni* "you peel us" (hewitt1995 §2.1.1.1, where it is written gv-prckvn-i; the Mkhedruli spelling is ours from his transliteration, in which unmarked p is ფ **(unverified)**).
- **Stress** is weak and does not change vowel quality; its placement is disputed (section 6). Teach: in words of up to four syllables, stress the first syllable or the antepenult; long words may have both (aronson1990 §1.8). Hewitt (§2.4.1) quotes Dirr (first syllable in two- and three-syllable words, antepenult in longer) and Tschenkéli (first syllable, never the last), and notes surnames in -ძე stress the penult (ševardnáʒe).
- **Intonation**: yes-no questions have a rise at the end, often with lengthening of the last vowel (aronson1990 §1.8); question-word questions put a high fall on the verb's stressed syllable (hewitt1995 §2.4.2). This is the main way a yes-no question is marked (kat-02-09).

## 3. Script and transliteration

### Script

- **Mkhedruli** (მხედრული), the everyday script: 33 letters in modern use, one sound each, no upper and lower case (aronson1990 §1.10). Letters differ by height: some sit between the base lines, some rise above, some descend below, some do both (aronson1990 §1.10). **Mtavruli** (მთავრული) is a set of capital-height forms used for headings and all-caps text, not for sentence-initial capitals; Unicode encodes it separately (U+1C90–U+1CBF; wiktionary Module:Geor-translit). The two older scripts, **Asomtavruli** and **Nuskhuri**, are church scripts (hewitt1995 §1.2); they are described once, in kat-01-08, and never used in examples.
- Alphabet order follows Greek for the first 22 letters (aronson1990 §1.10.2): ა ბ გ დ ე ვ ზ თ ი კ ლ მ ნ ო პ ჟ რ ს ტ უ ფ ქ ღ ყ შ ჩ ც ძ წ ჭ ხ ჯ ჰ. Five obsolete letters (ჱ ჲ ჳ ჴ ჵ) and the additional letters ჶ ჷ ჸ for other languages are noted once, in kat-01-08.
- Georgian has no capital letters, so proper names are not marked (aronson1990 Introduction). Glosses give names with an English capital.
- Unicode: Mkhedruli is U+10D0–U+10F0. Files must be NFC. Never use Latin look-alikes for Georgian letters.

### Transliteration used in this guide

**Scheme: the Wiktionary standard transliteration**, which is the usual Caucasological system (Iberian-Caucasian Linguistics yearbook; Holisky in *The World's Writing Systems*; Fähnrich) as implemented in wiktionary's Module:Geor-translit (opened 2026-11-12). Ejectives take a dot (below for ḳ ṭ c̣ č̣, above for ṗ q̇); aspirates are plain.

Why this scheme:

1. It marks every ejective, so the three-way contrast that defines Georgian pronunciation is visible in every example (the brief's requirement).
2. It is strictly one letter for one letter, with no digraphs, so it round-trips: Mkhedruli can be rebuilt from it mechanically. The national system of 2002 uses digraphs (ts, ch, kh, gh, zh, sh, dz), so *ts* in that system is ambiguous between ც and თ+ს, and *kh* between ხ and ქ+ჰ.
3. It is exactly what `tools/wikt.py` prints next to every form, so a verifier can compare our transliteration with the source character for character.
4. It is close to aronson1990's system (dots for ejectives; Aronson leaves q undotted because there is no plain q to contrast with, Lesson 1 note 3), so readers moving on to Aronson will feel at home. hewitt1995 marks ejectives with an apostrophe instead (k'ac-i, c'el-i, q'): tell the reader this once, in kat-01-08.

## 4. Grammatical and glossing conventions

### Terms

- **Screeve** (Georgian მწკრივი *mc̣ḳrivi* "row"): one tense-aspect-mood paradigm of six forms. There are eleven, in three **series** (hewitt1995 §4.7; the eleven names below are exactly the rows of wiktionary's conjugation tables):
  - Series I, present subseries: present, imperfect, present subjunctive.
  - Series I, future subseries: future, conditional, future subjunctive.
  - Series II (aorist series): aorist, optative.
  - Series III (perfect series): perfect, pluperfect, perfect subjunctive.
  Define "screeve" in kat-04-08 and use it from then on; before that, say "tense".
- **Verb classes**, four (hewitt1995 §4.7.1–4.7.4 calls them Transitive, Intransitive, Medial, Indirect, plus Statives §4.7.5; aronson1990 numbers them I. to IV. conjugation). This guide uses plain names with the number in brackets at first mention: **transitive (class 1)**, **unaccusative or "happening" intransitive (class 2)**, **medial (class 3)**, **indirect (class 4)**. Class membership is defined by how the verb forms its future and aorist and which case its subject takes, not by meaning alone (hewitt1995 §4.0, chapter not opened: **(unverified)** wording).
- **Preverb** (PV), **version** (NV, SV, OV, LV), **thematic suffix** (TS), **masdar** (verbal noun): keep these names; define each at first use. aronson1990 avoids "version" (Introduction: it uses "indirect object" and "reflexive indirect object" instead); hewitt1995 §4.4 uses it. This guide uses "version", because Wiktionary headwords ("with neutral versioner") and most course materials do.
- **Case names and order**: nominative, ergative, dative, genitive, instrumental, adverbial, vocative. This is the order of wiktionary's tables. hewitt1995 §3.1.1 orders them nominative, vocative, dative, ergative, genitive, instrumental, adverbial: reorder when checking. Use "ergative" (not "narrative", the older English name).

### Case-marking by series (the core rule)

Taken from the subject pronouns printed in wiktionary's conjugation tables (e.g. დაწერს: series I "მე, შენ, ის" = nominative; series II "მე, შენ, მან" = ergative; series III "მე, შენ, მას" = dative, headed "inversion") and from wals 98A's glossed examples:

| | Series I | Series II | Series III |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transitive (class 1) subject | NOM | ERG | DAT (inversion) |
| Transitive direct object | DAT | NOM | NOM |
| Medial (class 3) subject | NOM | ERG | DAT (inversion) |
| Unaccusative (class 2) subject | NOM | NOM | NOM |
| Indirect (class 4) experiencer | DAT | DAT | DAT |
| Indirect (class 4) stimulus | NOM | NOM | NOM |

The medial row is what makes the system "active" rather than plainly ergative (wals 98A: "active-inactive", with *nino-m daamtknara* "Nino yawned", ERG with an intransitive). Class 1 and 3 in series III and class 4 everywhere invert: the logical subject is marked with the object-set prefixes and the dative case. In series III the indirect object of a three-person verb moves into a -თვის phrase **(unverified; check hewitt1995 §4.7.1 or aronson1990 Lesson 10)**.

### Gloss conventions

Segment with hyphens; clitics with `=`. One gloss per piece; fused meanings joined by full stops. Abbreviations and the lesson that unlocks each are in `glossary.json`.

- **Nouns**: stem-case: `ḳac-i` `man-NOM`, `ḳac-ma` `man-ERG`, `ḳac-s` `man-DAT`, `ḳac-eb-i` `man-PL-NOM`. The plural -eb- is its own piece. The long forms -sa, -isa, -ita, -ada (before *da* "and", and in postposed genitives, hewitt1995 §3.1.1) are glossed the same as the short forms. Postpositions are suffixed and joined with a hyphen and glossed in lower case: `saxl-ši` `house-in` (the dative -s is lost before -ši and -ze; gloss the fused form as `house.DAT-in` only in the lesson that explains it, kat-03-02, then plain `house-in`). Postpositions on the genitive keep the -is: `saxl-is-tvis` `house-GEN-for`.
- **Truncation and syncope** are not marked: `megobr-is` `friend-GEN`, `ded-is` `mother-GEN`.
- **Adjectives** before a noun take a reduced ending (hewitt1995 §3.2.1, preview); gloss the ending that is there and leave a bare stem unglossed: `ḳarg-i ḳac-i` `good-NOM man-NOM`, `ḳarg ḳac-s` `good man-DAT`.
- **Verbs**: segment in template order: preverb - person prefix - version vowel - root - (passive -d- / causative) - thematic suffix - screeve marker - person suffix.
  - Present (unmarked, no tense gloss). Person and set are written together, `1SBJ`, `3SG.SBJ`, `1SG.OBJ` (the linter reads `1SBJ` as `1` + `SBJ`): `v-c̣er` `1SBJ-write`; `c̣er-s` `write-3SG.SBJ`; `v-c̣er-t` `1SBJ-write-PL`; `c̣er-en` `write-3PL.SBJ`; `v-xaṭ-av` `1SBJ-draw-TS`.
  - Object prefixes: `m-xaṭ-av-s` `1SG.OBJ-draw-TS-3SG.SBJ` "he draws me"; `g-xaṭ-av` `2OBJ-draw-TS`; `gv-xaṭ-av-s` `1PL.OBJ-draw-TS-3SG.SBJ`. Third-person direct objects are zero and not segmented. Third-person indirect objects s-/h-/zero: `IO` (`3IO` when written).
  - **Inversion verbs and series III are glossed by marker set, not by role**: `m-i-q̇var-s` `1SG.OBJ-OV-love-3SG.SBJ` "I love him/her" (lit. "he/she is loved to me"). The literal line shows the inversion. This keeps one gloss per prefix across the whole guide and is how hewitt1995 and aronson1990 both analyse the prefixes (as object markers). Explain this once, in kat-04-17.
  - Future = preverb + present; gloss the preverb `PV` and do not add FUT: `da-v-c̣er` `PV-1SBJ-write` "I will write". The translation carries the tense. Same for the conditional and future subjunctive (PV + imperfect, PV + present subjunctive).
  - Imperfect and subjunctive endings as one piece with any fused person: `v-c̣er-di` `1SBJ-write-IMPF`, `c̣er-da` `write-IMPF.3SG`, `c̣er-dnen` `write-IMPF.3PL`; `v-c̣er-de` `1SBJ-write-SBJV`.
  - Aorist: `da-v-c̣er-e` `PV-1SBJ-write-AOR`, `da-c̣er-a` `PV-write-AOR.3SG`, `da-c̣er-es` `PV-write-AOR.3PL`.
  - Optative: `da-v-c̣er-o` `PV-1SBJ-write-OPT`, `da-c̣er-o-s` `PV-write-OPT-3SG.SBJ`, `da-c̣er-on` `PV-write-OPT.3PL`.
  - Perfect: `da-m-i-c̣er-i-a` `PV-1SG.OBJ-OV-write-PRF-3SG.SBJ`; for -av- verbs `da-m-i-xaṭ-av-s` `PV-1SG.OBJ-OV-draw-TS-3SG.SBJ` with PRF in the literal note **(segmentation of series III endings is an analytical choice; hewitt1995 §4.7.1 not opened: (unverified))**. Pluperfect `da-m-e-c̣er-a`: gloss the e- as `PLUP` and the -a as `AOR.3SG`? Decide in kat-04-22 after opening a source; until then gloss the whole ending as one piece `PLUP.3SG`.
  - Copula: `ar-is` is not segmented: `aris` `be.3SG`; `var` `be.1SG`; enclitic `=a` `=be.3SG`.
  - Class 2 present: `i-c̣er-eb-a` `PASS-write-TS-3SG`, `v-i-c̣er-eb-i` `1SBJ-PASS-write-TS-1/2`: the -i of 1st/2nd person is glossed `SBJ`, giving `1SBJ-PASS-write-TS-SBJ`. Treat this as provisional until kat-04-10 is drafted against a source.
  - Masdar and participles: `c̣er-a` `write-MSD`; `da-c̣er-il-i` `PV-write-PTCP-NOM`; `da-sa-c̣er-i` `PV-FUT.PTCP-write-NOM` (circumfix: gloss the prefix, leave -i as NOM); `da-u-c̣er-el-i` `PV-PRIV-write-PRIV-NOM`.
- Negators, particles and conjunctions are glossed by a lower-case English word: *ar* `not`, *ver* `cannot`, *nu* `don't`, *xom* `isn't.it`, *da* `and`, *ḳi* `but/yes` by context.
- Never gloss "I" as a bare capital; it is `1SG`.

### Citation forms

- Nouns and adjectives: nominative singular (*ḳaci*), with genitive where it syncopates or truncates: *megobari*, gen. *megobris*; *deda*, gen. *dedis*.
- Verbs: wiktionary lemmatises a verb under its **third person singular present** (or future, for verbs without a present), with the masdar as "verbal noun": *c̣ers* (masdar *c̣era*), future *dac̣ers*. This guide cites verbs the same way: 3SG present / 3SG future / masdar, plus aorist and perfect where they are not predictable: **წერს, დაწერს, წერა; aor. დაწერა; pf. დაუწერია**. Vocabulary blocks put the extra forms in the notes field.

## 6. Points of scholarly disagreement or uncertainty

Say so in the lesson when one of these comes up.

1. **Stress.** Weak, and its position is disputed: first syllable, antepenult, or both (aronson1990 §1.8 "linguists have not been able to agree on exactly where it falls"; hewitt1995 §2.4.1 quoting Dirr and Tschenkéli). Do not mark stress in examples.
2. **Final devoicing** of voiced stops: "generally" (aronson1990 §1.1) versus "slight" (hewitt1995 §2.2.1). Teach it as a tendency.
3. **Alignment.** Georgian has been called ergative, split-ergative, active (wals 98A "active-inactive") and, for verb agreement, accusative (wals 100A). The facts are not in dispute; the label is (tuite2017 and harris1981 at second hand). Teach the facts by series and class, and say the labels differ.
4. **Verb classes as unergative/unaccusative.** Harris's analysis (harris1981, second hand) of class 3 as unergative and class 2 as unaccusative is widely used but class membership has many lexical exceptions. Say "most verbs that describe an activity" rather than stating a semantic rule.
5. **Version.** Whether version is one category (hewitt1995 §4.4) or a set of separate functions of the pre-radical vowel (aronson1990 Introduction and Lesson 13 §6) is an analytical choice. This guide uses "version" and says Aronson does not.
6. **Is the perfect evidential?** Series III perfect has both a plain "have done" reading and an inferential or hearsay reading (*turme*); how central the evidential sense is varies by description **(unverified: no source opened on this; check hewitt1995 §4.7 or aronson1990 Lesson 10)**.
7. **Segmenting series III and the -i/-a person endings.** Where to cut fused endings is analysis, not fact. The gloss conventions in section 4 are this guide's choice.
8. **Old plural.** -ნ-/-თ- plurals are archaic but alive in formal style (ადამიანთა "of people" in the title of udhr, ადამიანთა უფლებების საყოველთაო დეკლარაცია; hewitt1995 §3.1: "still found in higher, archaising styles as well as in some set-expressions").
9. **Origins.** Do not give etymologies of Georgian morphemes (e.g. the origin of the thematic suffixes, the -ma ergative, or the Kartvelian reconstructions) unless a source opened in the session states them. Klimov's and Fähnrich's etymological dictionaries are cited by wiktionary; cite through wiktionary's etymology section.

## 8. Notes for drafters

- British spelling; second person; short sentences; one idea per paragraph. No "simply", no "just" as filler.
- Every lesson states *why*. For Georgian the reason is often systemic (which series, which class, which marker set) rather than historical; historical claims need a source (section 6, point 9).
- Build the chain of thought for every verb form: "Which class is the verb? Which series is the screeve in? So the subject is in case X and the object in case Y. Which persons are involved? So the prefix is ... and the suffix is ..."
- Until kat-04-12 (aorist), examples must stay in series I (present, imperfect, future, conditional) and use only nominative and dative arguments. Until kat-04-17, avoid indirect verbs except as clearly flagged previews (`preview:`), with one exception: *uq̇vars* "loves" and *aḳvs* "has" are so frequent that kat-00-02 may show them as unglossed teasers.
- Constructed examples use only words already introduced in a `:::vocab` block and only grammar already introduced.
- For every verb used, record its class and its 3SG present, future, aorist and perfect in the vocab notes, checked in wiktionary or marked `(unverified)`.
- Georgian has no gender: never translate *is* as "he" or "she" without context; say "he/she/it" in literal lines.
- Connections worth making for this reader:
  - **Spanish**: the dative experiencer of *me gusta* is the same idea as *m-o-sc̣ons* "I like it" (lit. "it pleases me"); Spanish clitic doubling (*le di a Juan*) resembles Georgian marking the object both on the noun and on the verb.
  - **French**: none structural; useful for the open vowels and for *tu/vous* politeness, which matches *šen/tkven*. French borrowings in Georgian are few; Russian is the main European source.
  - **Japanese and Korean**: verb-final order, postpositions, no articles, dropped pronouns, and dative-subject "I have" constructions (Korean *-ege ... itta*); Korean's three-way stop contrast (plain, aspirated, tense) is the nearest familiar thing to Georgian's, though Korean tense stops are not ejectives.
  - **Arabic**: Georgian has Arabic loanwords, mostly received through Persian (hewitt1995 §3.1.1, quoting Vogt, mentions stems "of foreign origin, especially Arabo-Persian"). Check any claimed loan in wiktionary's etymology section before stating it. Arabic's emphatic consonants are not ejectives: do not equate them.
  - **Basque** (if in the app): another ergative language of Europe with case-driven alignment, but without Georgian's split by tense.
