## The idea

Every language has to say who does what to whom, and when. Latin and Spanish put most of that information in the endings of nouns and verbs. English puts it in word order and in small words like "will" and "to".

Georgian puts most of it inside the verb. A Georgian verb is a frame with slots in front of the root and behind it. The slots name the subject and the objects, say in which direction the action goes, say whom it is done for, and fix its tense. One verb is often a complete sentence.

That is the organising idea of Georgian: **the verb is the sentence**. The nouns around it are often optional, and when they are present, the verb decides which case they take.

This lesson is a tour, not a lesson to learn from. Every form below is a preview, taught properly in part 2 or part 4. Read the notes, watch the pieces, and do not try to memorise anything.

## The people are in the verb

Here is one verb in two forms.

:::example
orig: ვწერ
translit: vc̣er
morphemes: v-c̣er
gloss: 1SBJ-write
translation: I write (it). / I am writing (it).
note: Preview. The prefix v- says "I" is the subject. No word for "I" is needed.
source: wiktionary
confidence: verified
preview: 1, SBJ
:::

:::example
orig: წერს
translit: c̣ers
morphemes: c̣er-s
gloss: write-3SG.SBJ
translation: He, she or it writes (it).
note: Preview. The suffix -s says the subject is one third person. Georgian has no gender, so the verb does not say "he" or "she".
source: wiktionary
confidence: verified
preview: 3, SG, SBJ
:::

Spanish and Latin do the same with their verb endings. Georgian goes further: the verb marks its object as well.

:::example
orig: მხატავს
translit: mxaṭavs
morphemes: m-xaṭ-av-s
gloss: 1SG.OBJ-draw-TS-3SG.SBJ
translation: He or she draws me.
note: Preview. m- says "me" is the object; -s says "he or she" is the subject. The piece -av is a stem-builder, taught in part 4.
source: bolkvadze2023 §9.1.3
confidence: verified
preview: 1, SG, OBJ, TS, 3, SBJ
:::

:::example
orig: გხატავ
translit: gxaṭav
morphemes: g-xaṭ-av
gloss: 2OBJ-draw-TS
translation: I draw you.
note: Preview. g- says "you" is the object. The "I" is understood: when "you" is the object, the verb leaves the "I" unmarked.
source: bolkvadze2023 §9.1.2
confidence: verified
preview: 2, OBJ, TS
:::

A verb that agrees with more than one person at once is called **polypersonal**. Korean and Japanese verbs do not mark person at all; Spanish verbs mark the subject; a Georgian verb agrees with the subject and with an object. The prefixes are short and the rules for combining them are strict. Part 4 builds them up one at a time.

## Direction and completion: the preverb

In front of the person prefix there can be a **preverb**, a short prefix that says which way the action goes: up, down, in, out, away from the speaker, towards the speaker. With many verbs it no longer says direction. It says that the action is complete, and that is how Georgian makes its future.

:::example
orig: დაწერს
translit: dac̣ers
morphemes: da-c̣er-s
gloss: PV-write-3SG.SBJ
translation: He or she will write it.
note: Preview. The same form as c̣ers, with the preverb da- in front. The preverb turns "writes" into "will write".
source: wiktionary
confidence: verified
preview: PV, 3, SG, SBJ
:::

There is no separate future ending. For most verbs the future is the present plus a preverb. Which preverb a verb takes has to be learnt with the verb, the way a Japanese learner learns which counter goes with which noun.

## For whom: version

Between the person prefix and the root there is room for one vowel. It says whom the action is for. Georgian grammarians call this **version**.

:::example
orig: ვაშენებ, ვიშენებ, ვუშენებ
translit: vašeneb, višeneb, vušeneb
morphemes: v-a-šen-eb v-i-šen-eb v-u-šen-eb
gloss: 1SBJ-NV-build-TS 1SBJ-SV-build-TS 1SBJ-OV-build-TS
translation: I build it. I build it for myself. I build it for him or her.
note: Preview. Only the vowel after v- changes: a- for no one in particular, i- for myself, u- for someone else.
source: bolkvadze2023 §9.4
confidence: verified
preview: 1, SBJ, NV, SV, OV, TS
:::

Not every scholar uses the term: Aronson's grammar describes the same vowels as markers of an indirect object instead. The facts are the same. This guide uses "version" and teaches it in part 4.

## The tense decides the cases

Now the surprise. Georgian nouns have case endings, as Latin ones do. In Latin the subject is always in the same case. In Georgian the case of the subject, and of the object, depends on the tense of the verb.

Watch the endings on *bavšv-* "child" and *c̣eril-* "letter" in three sentences.

:::example
orig: ბავშვი წერილს წერს.
translit: bavšvi c̣erils c̣ers.
morphemes: bavšv-i c̣eril-s c̣er-s
gloss: child-NOM letter-DAT write-3SG.SBJ
literal: Child letter writes.
translation: The child is writing a letter.
note: Preview. Present tense. The child has the ending -i (nominative), the letter -s (dative).
source: constructed
confidence: constructed
preview: NOM, DAT, 3, SG, SBJ
:::

:::example
orig: ბავშვმა წერილი დაწერა.
translit: bavšvma c̣erili dac̣era.
morphemes: bavšv-ma c̣eril-i da-c̣er-a
gloss: child-ERG letter-NOM PV-write-AOR.3SG
literal: Child letter wrote.
translation: The child wrote a letter.
note: Preview. Simple past. Now the child has -ma (ergative) and the letter has -i, the ending the child had before.
source: constructed
confidence: constructed
preview: ERG, NOM, PV, AOR, 3, SG
:::

:::example
orig: ბავშვს წერილი დაუწერია.
translit: bavšvs c̣erili dauc̣eria.
morphemes: bavšv-s c̣eril-i da-u-c̣er-i-a
gloss: child-DAT letter-NOM PV-OV-write-PRF-3SG.SBJ
literal: To the child the letter has been written.
translation: The child has written a letter (it seems).
note: Preview. Perfect. The child is now in the dative, the case the letter had in the present. This tense often means "apparently, it turns out".
source: constructed
confidence: constructed
preview: DAT, NOM, PV, OV, PRF, 3, SG, SBJ
:::

The child is the writer in all three. Its case goes from nominative to ergative to dative.

The reason lies in how Georgian groups its tenses. Georgian grammar sorts the verb's tense and mood forms into three groups, called **series**. Each single set of forms, such as "the present", is called a **screeve**, a word borrowed from Georgian *mc̣ḳrivi* "row"; part 4 defines it properly. The present, the imperfect and the future belong to series I. The simple past belongs to series II. The perfect belongs to series III. Each series has its own way of marking the subject and the object:

- Series I: subject in the nominative, object in the dative.
- Series II: subject in the ergative, object in the nominative.
- Series III: subject in the dative, object in the nominative.

There is a second condition. This pattern belongs to verbs of doing, like "write". Verbs of happening, like "die" or "go bankrupt", keep their subject in the nominative in all three series. So the case of a Georgian subject depends on two things at once: the series of the verb and the class of the verb.

Linguists argue about what to call this. Georgian has been called ergative, split-ergative and active. The World Atlas of Language Structures (feature 98A) classes its case marking as "active-inactive", and Hewitt describes the question as a matter of "lively debate". An ergative pattern is one where the doer of a transitive verb gets a special case, as the child does with -ma. The facts are not in dispute. This guide teaches the facts, series by series and class by class, and leaves the labels aside.

## What this means for you

Three habits follow.

Build every sentence from the verb outwards. First decide on the verb, its series and its class; then the nouns' cases follow.

Learn each verb with its key forms, not just its present. The future, the past and the perfect are often not predictable from the present, and they decide the cases.

Expect to meet the nouns' endings in more jobs than in Latin. The dative, for example, marks the object in the present, the "to whom" of giving, and the person who loves, wants or has. Part 3 maps them.

The rest of the language, nouns, adjectives and word order, is regular and comes quickly. The verb takes the time, and it is the part of Georgian that makes it worth learning.
