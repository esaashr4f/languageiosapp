## The route

After this part come nine more. Read them in order. Each lesson uses only what earlier lessons have taught, so jumping ahead will show you forms that nobody has explained yet.

Part 1 teaches the sounds and the alphabet. Part 2 gets you to short, complete sentences in the present. Part 3 covers the nouns: their seven cases, the postpositions built on them, pronouns and adjectives. Part 4 is the longest and the most important: the verb, from its template to the three series, the four verb classes, the ergative and the dative subject. Parts 5 and 6 build longer sentences. Part 7 covers the special topics, including colloquial speech, dialects and a short look at Old Georgian. Part 8 reads real texts, from proverbs to Rustaveli. Part 9 is reference: tables, vocabulary and the book list.

Each lesson has the same shape. First the idea and the reason behind it. Then how the form is built, step by step. Then examples, simplest first. Then the traps. A table comes only after the pattern has been explained, as a place to look things up. Each lesson ends with exercises and worked answers. Do the exercises before reading the answers. Building a Georgian verb yourself is the only real test of whether you have understood it.

## How to read an example

Every example has the same layers. Here is one. It is a preview: its forms are taught in part 2.

:::example
orig: ქალი სახლს ხატავს.
translit: kali saxls xaṭavs.
morphemes: kal-i saxl-s xaṭ-av-s
gloss: woman-NOM house-DAT draw-TS-3SG.SBJ
literal: Woman house draws.
translation: The woman is drawing a house.
note: Preview, to show the layers of an example.
source: constructed
confidence: constructed
preview: NOM, DAT, TS, 3, SG, SBJ
:::

The first line is the Georgian as you would see it in print, in the Georgian alphabet.

The second line is the **transliteration**: the same text, letter for letter, in Latin letters. Every Georgian letter has exactly one Latin equivalent, so you can always rebuild the Georgian from it. In the app you can hide this line once you read the script.

The third line cuts each word at the joins between its pieces. A hyphen separates them. An equals sign marks a small word that leans on its neighbour.

The fourth line is the **gloss**. It labels each piece, word under word and piece under piece. Meanings are in lower case: *woman*, *draw*. Grammatical labels are in capitals: NOM, DAT. A full stop joins labels carried by one piece: the -s at the end of *xaṭavs* says both "third person singular" and "subject", so it is glossed 3SG.SBJ. Tap a label in the app to see what it stands for. Each label is explained in plain words in the lesson that introduces it, and all of them are listed in part 9. The labels follow the Leipzig Glossing Rules, the standard used by linguists.

Two habits of Georgian glosses deserve a word now. Person markers on the verb are glossed by the set they belong to, SBJ (subject set) or OBJ (object set), because in some tenses the "subject" markers end up agreeing with the thing done and the "object" markers with the doer. Part 4 explains why. And Georgian has no gender, so a literal line says "he/she/it" where English would have to choose.

A literal translation follows where it helps to show the Georgian order. Then comes natural English, and a note saying what the example is there to show.

## The transliteration in brief

The transliteration is the standard one used by Caucasus specialists and by Wiktionary. It needs one rule now. Georgian has three kinds of p, t, k and similar sounds. A **dot** on a letter marks the hardest kind, the "ejective", made with a closed throat: *ḳ*, *ṭ*, *ṗ*, *c̣*, *č̣*, *q̇*. A plain letter without a dot, *k*, *t*, *p*, is the breathy kind, as in English "kit". So *ḳari* and *kari* are different words. Part 1 explains these sounds and lesson 1-8 gives the full table.

Georgian has no capital letters, so names are written in lower case in the transliteration too: *tbilisi*, *nino*. English translations use normal capitals.

Georgian stress is weak and its rules are disputed, so this guide does not mark it.

## Real sentences and made-up ones

Each example and each table has a small coloured dot in the app. Tap it to see its label and its source.

- **attested**: a real sentence from a real text, cited by work and passage. Translations are always this guide's own.
- **constructed**: a sentence built for this guide from rules and words that have each been checked. Early lessons use mostly constructed examples, because real sentences use too much grammar at once.
- **verified**: a form or table checked against a named source.
- **uncertain**: the sources disagree. The lesson says so in words as well.
- **unverified**: written but not yet checked. You should not see this label in a finished guide.

## Where the facts come from

The grammar follows three reference works. Tinatin Bolkvadze and Dodona Kiziria's *Georgian: A Comprehensive Grammar* (2023) is the main check for forms and their uses. George Hewitt's *Georgian: A Structural Reference Grammar* (1995) is the standard description in English. Howard Aronson's *Georgian: A Reading Grammar* (1990), a course book, guides the order in which the verb is taught.

Individual words and their full sets of forms were checked against the Georgian entries of Wiktionary, which give complete tables for most nouns and many verbs. Where Wiktionary has no table, the form was checked in one of the grammars. Comparisons with other languages use the World Atlas of Language Structures.

Real sentences come from public texts: the Georgian version of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, Georgian Wikipedia, and nineteenth-century literature published on Georgian Wikisource.

When the sources disagree, the lesson says so and names them. The commonest disagreements concern where the stress falls, what to call the case system, and how to cut some verb endings into pieces.

## How much to memorise

Less than a classroom course would ask, with one exception. Nouns, adjectives and word order can be understood and rebuilt from a few rules. The verb cannot, not completely. For each verb you will learn a small set of key forms, and from part 4 every vocabulary list gives them. Learn those forms with the verb, as you would learn the gender of a French noun. Everything else in this guide is there to be understood, then looked up when you need it.
