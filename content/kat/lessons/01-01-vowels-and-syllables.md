## The idea

Georgian has five vowels: *a*, *e*, *i*, *o*, *u*. They are close to the five vowels of Spanish or Japanese, and they behave like them: each is a pure, steady sound, with no glide at the end.

Georgian does not do three things English and Japanese do to vowels. It has no long vowels: no pair of words differs only in length, as Japanese *obasan* and *obāsan* do. It does not weaken unstressed vowels, as English does to the last vowel of "sofa". And it does not run two vowels together into one syllable. In English "die" or Spanish *aire*, two vowel sounds share a syllable. Such a pair is called a **diphthong**. Georgian has none. Wherever two vowels stand side by side, each makes its own syllable.

That gives the most useful rule of this lesson: **a Georgian word has as many syllables as it has vowels.** Aronson states it in exactly these terms. Consonants never make a syllable on their own, however many of them pile up. A **syllable** is a beat of the word, built round one vowel.

## How it is built

To say a Georgian word, take three steps.

1. Find the vowels. Each one is a beat. *deda* "mother" has two, *ia* "violet" has two, *xe* "tree" has one.
2. Give each vowel its value. *a* is open, as in Spanish *casa* or French *patte*. *e* is the vowel of English "get". *i* is between the vowels of "bit" and "beat". *o* is a rounded vowel at the height of *e*, as in German *Glocken*. *u* is the vowel of Spanish *tú*, with the lips well rounded. Never let *e* or *o* glide, as English does in "day" and "go".
3. Say every syllable at much the same strength. Do not punch one syllable and swallow the rest.

Sounds are written here in the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA), in square brackets. This guide writes the vowels [a ɛ i ɔ u], following Aronson. Wiktionary prints the same sounds with finer symbols, [ä e̞ i o̞ u].

**Stress** is the extra force a language puts on one syllable of a word, like the first syllable of English "mother". In Georgian it is very weak. Aronson says it is so weak that linguists "have not been able to agree on exactly where it falls". The descriptions he and Hewitt report say this:

- In words of two or three syllables, the first syllable is usually stressed: *déda*.
- In longer words, the third syllable from the end usually carries the stress, and Aronson says a long word can have two, on the first syllable and on the third from the end. Some longer words prefer the first syllable: Hewitt gives *mégobari* "friend".
- Surnames in *-ʒe* stress the syllable before it: Hewitt gives *ševardnáʒe*.

Because stress is weak, it never changes a vowel. This guide does not mark it.

What is strong is **intonation**, the rise and fall of the voice across a sentence. A yes-or-no question changes pitch on its last syllable, and that vowel is often lengthened. Hewitt describes a rise-fall on the last syllable of the verb; Aronson describes a sharp rise at the end of the sentence. Lesson 2-8 uses it.

## Watch out

**Doubled vowels.** When two identical vowels meet, the sources describe the result differently. Hewitt says sequences of vowels "are pronounced as such", and counts *ga-a-advil-eb* "you will make it easier" as five syllables. Bolkvadze and Kiziria say two identical vowels are said as one long vowel, as in *meekvse*. Both agree that Georgian has no long vowels as separate sounds. In practice, say both vowels and do not rush them; you will be understood either way.

**Stress is not a guide to vowel quality.** Russian and English speakers expect unstressed *o* or *a* to weaken. In Georgian they never do.

**Do not insert vowels.** Faced with *ʒma*, an English speaker wants to say "dzuma". The word has one syllable. Lesson 1-4 shows how to say long clusters without adding vowels.

## Connections

Spanish and Japanese give you the five vowels almost ready-made; only Georgian *o* and *e* are a little more open than Spanish ones. Japanese, unlike Georgian, has long vowels and devoices *i* and *u* between voiceless consonants: do neither in Georgian.

## Exercises

1. Count the syllables of *megobari*, *ia*, *ʒma* and *okro*, and say what rule you used.
2. An English speaker says *ezo* to rhyme with "say so". Name the two mistakes.
3. Why can *ia* not be said as one syllable, like English "ya"?
4. A learner stresses the second syllable of *deda* strongly and reduces the first to a murmur. Which two things has he got wrong, and which matters more?
5. *da* appears twice in the word list. What are its two meanings, and how will you tell them apart?
6. How do Hewitt and Bolkvadze and Kiziria differ on *meekvse*, and what do they agree on?
7. What does a Georgian speaker use, more than stress, to mark a yes-or-no question?

## Worked answers

1. Four, two, one, two. A Georgian word has as many syllables as vowels: me-go-ba-ri, i-a, ʒma, o-kro. Consonants never make a syllable.
2. The *e* has been turned into the glide of "say", and the final *o* into the glide of "so". Georgian *e* and *o* are steady: [ɛzɔ].
3. Because Georgian has no diphthongs. Each vowel is its own syllable, so *ia* is i-a, two beats.
4. He has stressed the wrong syllable (the first is usual in a two-syllable word), and he has weakened an unstressed vowel. The second matters more: stress is weak and disputed, but a murmured vowel changes the word.
5. "Sister" (a noun, plural *debi*) and "and" (a conjunction). Context tells them apart, as it does for English "can". As a conjunction, *da* stands between the things it joins.
6. Hewitt counts every vowel as a syllable, so he would say both e's (me-e-kvse); Bolkvadze and Kiziria say the two merge into one long e. Both agree that Georgian has no long vowels as a separate kind of sound.
7. Intonation: a change of pitch on the last syllable (a rise-fall for Hewitt, a sharp rise for Aronson), often with that vowel lengthened.
