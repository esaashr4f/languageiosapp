## The idea

Lesson 1-2 gave you three triples: voiced, aspirated and ejective *b p ṗ*, *d t ṭ*, *g k ḳ*. Georgian has two more triples of the same shape, made of affricates, and one lone ejective at the very back of the mouth.

An **affricate** is a stop that opens into a hiss: the *ts* of "cats", the *ch* of "church", the *j* of "judge". Georgian treats each as one sound and gives it the same three kinds as its stops.

- The *ts* series: voiced *ʒ* [dz], aspirated *c* [tsʰ], ejective *c̣* [tsʼ].
- The *ch* series: voiced *ǯ* [dʒ], aspirated *č* [tʃʰ], ejective *č̣* [tʃʼ].

A **fricative** is a consonant made by narrowing the mouth so that the air hisses through: *s*, *z*, *f*, *v*, "sh". Bolkvadze and Kiziria describe Georgian's fricatives as **pairs**, voiced against voiceless: *z* and *s*, *ž* [ʒ] and *š* [ʃ], *ɣ* and *x*. Only *v* and *h* have no partner.

The **uvula** is the small flap that hangs at the back of the soft palate. Three Georgian consonants are made near it: *x*, *ɣ* and *q̇*. A **uvular** consonant is one made there.

Why does *q̇* stand alone, with no voiced or aspirated partner? Part of the answer is history. Aronson notes that Old Georgian had an aspirated partner, written ჴ and transcribed *q*, which the modern standard has lost; it survives in some dialects. Bolkvadze and Kiziria call this the "deficient" triple. The voiced member is missing even in Proto-Kartvelian as Gamkrelidze and Ivanov reconstruct it.

## How it is built

**The affricates.** Make *t* and *s* at the same spot, behind the upper teeth, as one movement: that is [ts]. Now give it the three kinds you learnt for stops. *ʒ* is voiced, like the *ds* of "beds" at the start of a word. *c* has a puff after the hiss. *c̣* starts with the throat closed and pops. The *ch* series works the same way further back: *ǯ* as in "judge", *č* as in "church" with a puff, *č̣* with the closed throat.

The letters need care: *ʒ* is [dz], while the sound of French *j* is written *ž*, and *ǯ* is English *j*.

**q̇.** Raise the back of the tongue against the uvula, close the throat, and release with a pop. Aronson says the sound varies from a clean uvular ejective stop [qʼ] through [qχʼ] to an ejective fricative [χʼ]; Hewitt says it is "regularly accompanied by strong friction". Wiktionary gives [χʼ] for *q̇ava* "coffee". Aim for a pop with a rasp after it.

**x and ɣ.** *x* is a rasping hiss at the back, like German *ach* or the Spanish *j* of Spain, but further back still: Aronson places it behind the velar position. *ɣ* is the same with voice. Hewitt notes that both shift between a velar and a uvular position with the sounds around them, which is why some books write them [x ɣ] and others [χ ʁ]. Both describe the same Georgian sounds.

**v** has several pronunciations, and Aronson and Hewitt agree on the pattern:

1. At the start of a word and between vowels it is [v]: *vašli* "apple".
2. After a consonant it is often [w], a rounding of the lips: *ɣvino* "wine" is close to [ʁwinɔ].
3. Before a voiceless consonant it becomes [f]: in *bavšvi* "child", the first *v* is said [f].

**r, l, h.** *r* is a single flap of the tongue, as in Spanish *pero*. *l* is clear before *i* and *e*, as in French, and dark elsewhere, as in English "full" (Aronson). *h* is an English *h*. Bolkvadze and Kiziria note that words with *h* in their root are borrowed, such as *haeri* "air".

## Watch out

**c is not k, and x is not ks.** In this guide *c* is always [tsʰ], *c̣* [tsʼ] and *ʒ* [dz]; *x* is the back hiss [χ].

**Two letters, two sounds: ž and ʒ.** *ž* is [ʒ], the sound of French *j*. *ʒ* is [dz]. They look alike in Latin letters; in Georgian they are unrelated letters, ჟ and ძ.

**v before o and u.** In native words *v* does not usually stand next to *b*, *p*, *ṗ*, *m* or before *o* and *u* (Aronson; Hewitt). Later lessons show forms in which a *v* disappears for this reason.

**Silent h.** In some verb forms an *h* is written before a stop, as a grammatical marker. Aronson says it is "generally not pronounced except in very careful speech". Part 4 explains it.

## Connections

French speakers already own *ɣ*: the uvular French *r* of *Paris* is the same kind of sound, written [ʁ] in the IPA, as Bolkvadze and Kiziria transcribe ღ. Spanish speakers from Spain have a head start on *x* with their *j* in *jamón*, which Georgian makes a little further back. Korean ㅊ is close to Georgian aspirated *č*.

## Exercises

1. Write the three ts-sounds in this guide's letters and in IPA, and give a word for each.
2. A reader pronounces *ca* "sky" as "ka". What has gone wrong, and what is the right sound?
3. Explain the difference between *ž* and *ʒ*, and give a word containing each.
4. How is *v* pronounced in *vašli*, and how in *bavšvi*? Give the rule.
5. Why does *q̇* have no aspirated partner in modern Georgian?
6. Count the syllables of *zɣva*, *čai* and *haeri*.
7. Which Georgian sound is the nearest to French *r*, and how is it written?

## Worked answers

1. *ʒ* [dz] in *ʒili* "sleep", *c* [tsʰ] in *ca* "sky", *c̣* [tsʼ] in *c̣eli* "year".
2. He has read the Latin letter *c* as in English. In this guide *c* is the aspirated affricate [tsʰ], so *ca* is [tsʰa].
3. *ž* is [ʒ], the sound of "measure", as in *žurnali*. *ʒ* is the voiced affricate [dz], as in *ʒili*. They are different letters in Georgian: ჟ and ძ.
4. [v] at the start of *vašli*. In *bavšvi*, the first *v* stands before the voiceless *š* and is said [f]; the second follows a consonant and is said [w]. The rule: [v] at the start and between vowels, [w] after a consonant, [f] before a voiceless consonant.
5. Old Georgian had one, ჴ *q*, but the standard language lost it; Aronson notes it survives in some dialects. No voiced partner existed even in Proto-Kartvelian, in Gamkrelidze and Ivanov's reconstruction.
6. One (*zɣva*: one vowel), two (*ča-i*), three (*ha-e-ri*). One syllable per vowel, as in lesson 1-1.
7. *ɣ*, written ღ: both are voiced back fricatives, [ʁ] in the IPA.
