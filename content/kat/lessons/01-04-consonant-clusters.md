## The idea

A **cluster** is a run of consonants with no vowel between them, like the *str* of "street". English allows three at the start of a word. Georgian allows far more. Hewitt reports Hans Vogt's count of the clusters that can begin a Georgian word: 740 in all, of which 148 have four consonants, 21 have five and 4 have six. Bolkvadze and Kiziria give *brdɣvna* "plucking" as a six-consonant cluster inside a single root.

Clusters grow longer when pieces of words meet. *c̣vrtna* "training" already starts with four consonants; add the prefix *m-* that makes "one who does it" and you get *mc̣vrtneli* "trainer", with five. They also arise when a vowel drops out of a word: *mgeli* "wolf" has the form *mglis* "of the wolf". Part 3 explains that drop.

The rule from lesson 1-1 holds throughout: consonants never make a syllable. Hewitt says sonants, the consonants *m*, *n*, *l*, *r*, have "no syllable-forming capacity" in clusters. *mc̣vrtneli* has three vowels and three syllables. Do not add vowels to break a cluster up.

## Two kinds of cluster

Most clusters are said one consonant after another, each with its own release. Aronson's example is *tbilisi*: you hear an aspirated *t* with its puff, then a voiced *b*.

A special set behaves differently. A **harmonic cluster** is a pair of consonants that is said with a single release, as if it were one sound. Hewitt quotes Gamkrelidze's definition: a stop or affricate made at the front of the mouth, followed by a consonant made at the back, velar or uvular, *of the same kind*. Voiced goes with voiced, aspirated with aspirated, ejective with ejective. Gamkrelidze adds that in the structure of roots such a pair is "functionally identical with a simple consonant". That is why it is said as one.

Bolkvadze and Kiziria set the harmonic clusters out in two systems.

- With a velar stop second: voiced *bg*, *dg*, *ʒg*, *ǯg*; aspirated *pk*, *tk*, *ck*, *čk*; ejective *ṗḳ*, *ṭḳ*, *c̣ḳ*, *č̣ḳ*.
- With a uvular second: voiced *bɣ*, *dɣ*, *ʒɣ*, *ǯɣ*; aspirated *px*, *tx*, *cx*, *čx*; ejective *ṗq̇*, *ṭq̇*, *c̣q̇*, *č̣q̇*.

In the second system *ɣ* and *x* fill the voiced and aspirated slots, because the uvular triple has only its ejective, *q̇* (lesson 1-3).

The order matters: front consonant first, back consonant second. A pair the other way round, or two consonants of different kinds, is not harmonic.

## How it is built

To say a cluster, take three steps.

1. Find any harmonic pairs. Treat each as one sound with one release.
2. Give every consonant its own kind. A voiced one stays voiced, an aspirate keeps its puff, an ejective keeps its closed throat. Aronson notes that clusters show "little or no assimilation" of these features.
3. Say the consonants in order without any vowel in between, then the vowel.

For a harmonic pair, place both tongue positions first, then release them together. In *dɣe* "day", put the tongue for *d* and the back of the tongue for *ɣ* at the same time, then open into the vowel. In an ejective pair like *ṭq̇e* "forest", the throat closes once for both and pops once.

Build long clusters from the vowel backwards: *-e*, *ne-*, *tne-*, *rtne-*, *vrtne-*, *c̣vrtne-*, *mc̣vrtne-*. Each step adds one consonant to a sequence you can already say.

## Watch out

**No helping vowels.** The reflex of English and Spanish speakers is to say *mgeli* as "muh-geli" or *ṭq̇e* as "tuh-qe". Each added vowel adds a syllable, and the word becomes a different shape. Japanese speakers, used to a vowel after almost every consonant, must resist the same urge.

**Harmonic does not mean easy to see.** *ṭḳbili* begins with a harmonic pair and then a non-harmonic *b*. *ḳbili* has no harmonic pair at all, because its back consonant comes first.

**r and l can weaken.** Aronson notes that *r* and *l* between voiceless consonants may lose their voice or disappear. That is a habit of speech, not of spelling: the letter stays.

## Connections

Korean and Japanese allow almost no clusters, so this lesson is new ground for both. Spanish helps a little: *tr*, *pl* and *bl* are clusters, and Spanish speakers already avoid adding vowels inside them. Russian, the main European language Georgians learn, also allows long clusters, which is one reason Georgian sounds less strange to Russian ears.

## Exercises

1. Count the consonants before the first vowel and the syllables in *mc̣vrtneli*.
2. Is *dɣe* harmonic? Explain using the definition.
3. Is *ḳbili* harmonic? Why or why not?
4. In *ṭḳbili*, which pair is harmonic and which consonant is released separately?
5. Describe how *tbilisi* begins, release by release.
6. Which clusters would you expect to be harmonic: *px*, *xp*, *ṭq̇*, *tq̇*? Say why for each.
7. Why does the uvular system use *ɣ* and *x* where the velar system uses *g* and *k*?
8. A learner says *mgeli* as three syllables. What has he done, and what is the right count?

## Worked answers

1. Five consonants (*m c̣ v r t*), three syllables (*mc̣vr-tne-li*), because there are three vowels.
2. Yes. *d* is a voiced stop at the front of the mouth; *ɣ* is a voiced consonant at the back. Front then back, same kind: harmonic.
3. No. *ḳ* is made at the back and comes first. Harmonic clusters put the front consonant first.
4. *ṭḳ* is harmonic: two ejectives, front then back. The *b* is released on its own.
5. An aspirated *t* is released with its puff of breath, then a voiced *b* is released separately before the vowel. Two releases, because *t* and *b* are not of the same kind and *b* is not a back consonant.
6. *px*: harmonic (aspirated front + the voiceless back fricative of the aspirated slot). *xp*: no, back first. *ṭq̇*: harmonic (two ejectives). *tq̇*: no, an aspirate with an ejective.
7. Because the uvular triple is incomplete: only the ejective *q̇* exists, so the voiced and aspirated slots are filled by the fricatives *ɣ* and *x*.
8. He has inserted a vowel between *m* and *g*. *mgeli* has two vowels and so two syllables: *mge-li*.
