## The idea

Lesson 1-2 gave you nine stops: three kinds, voiced, aspirated and ejective, at three places in the mouth. Each has its own letter. This lesson teaches the nine, and with them the number of Georgian words you can read jumps.

The letters do not look like a system. Nothing in the shape of ბ *b* tells you that ფ *p* and პ *ṗ* are its relatives. The order of the alphabet does not group them either: *b* is the second letter, *ṗ* the fifteenth and *p* the twenty-first. The reason lies in where the alphabet came from.

Aronson shows that the first twenty-two letters of the Georgian alphabet follow the order of the Greek alphabet. Georgian *a, b, g, d, e* match Greek alpha, beta, gamma, delta, epsilon, and so on down to Greek chi. The letters for sounds Greek did not have come after that, at the end. So each Georgian stop letter stands where its Greek counterpart stood.

That also explains which Georgian sound each Greek letter was matched with. Greek had plain *p*, *t*, *k* without a puff, and aspirated *ph*, *th*, *kh* with one. Georgian put its ejectives in the slots of the plain Greek letters, and its aspirates in the slots of the Greek aspirates:

- *ḳ* sits where Greek kappa sits, *ṗ* where pi sits, *ṭ* where tau sits;
- *t* sits where theta sits, *p* where phi sits, *k* where chi sits.

The same match is alive today. Aronson notes that Georgian usually takes the plain *p*, *t*, *k* of European words as ejectives: *ṗoliṭiḳa* "politics", borrowed from Russian. Greek *th* and *ph* come through as aspirates, as in *teaṭri* "theatre" and *pilosopia* "philosophy".

## How it is built

Here are the nine letters. Read each with its height first, as in lesson 1-5.

**Lips**

- ბ *b*: a round body on the line with a stalk rising above it and curling over. Name: *ban*.
- ფ *p*: two round loops side by side, joined at a stem that hangs below the line. Name: *par*.
- პ *ṗ*: a 3-like shape that rises above the line. Name: *ṗar*.

**Teeth**

- დ *d*: a wide double loop whose left end sweeps below the line. Name: *don*.
- თ *t*: a double arch like a rounded m, between the lines. Name: *tan*.
- ტ *ṭ*: a circle with a straight stroke through it, rising above and hanging below. Name: *ṭari*.

**Soft palate**

- გ *g*: a round loop hanging below the line with a small hook at the top. Name: *gan*.
- ქ *k*: a straight stem from above the line to below it, with a hook on the left. Name: *kan*.
- კ *ḳ*: the same 3-like shape as პ, but hanging below the line. Name: *ḳan*.

The pair კ and პ is the clearest case of height deciding the letter: the shape is the same, and only its position tells you whether it is *ḳ* or *ṗ*. Wikipedia's article on the Georgian scripts makes the same point. The other 3-like letters are ვ *v*, which hangs below, and ჰ *h*, which rises above with a stroke across its top.

To read a new word:

1. Sort each letter by height.
2. Among the round letters, look for the stem or stroke that decides the shape.
3. Say the sounds, giving each stop its kind: voiced, puff, or pop.

## Watch out

**კ and პ, ვ and ჰ.** Four 3-like letters. Below the line: კ *ḳ* and ვ *v*. Above the line: პ *ṗ* and ჰ *h*.

**თ, ო and ი.** All three sit between the lines. ი is a single arch, ო an arch curled in on the left, თ a double arch.

**Transliteration tells you the kind.** If you write *p* for პ, you have turned bread (*ṗuri*) into a cow (*puri*). When you transliterate, the dot is the ejective.

## Connections

Korean borrowing works the opposite way: Korean usually takes European *p*, *t*, *k* as its aspirated ㅍ ㅌ ㅋ (*keopi* "coffee"), while Georgian usually takes them as ejectives. Each language picks the sound that its own ears hear as closest.

## Exercises

1. Read aloud and transliterate: დილა, ტბა, ბინა, ქალაქი.
2. Write in Mkhedruli: *ṗuri*, *puli*, *didi*, *titi*.
3. What single feature tells კ from პ?
4. Why does თ stand eighth in the Georgian alphabet, between ზ *z* and ი *i*?
5. A Georgian word borrowed from French begins with French *t*. Which Georgian letter would you expect, and why?
6. In *teaṭri*, why are the two *t*-sounds written with different letters?
7. Sort by height: ბ ფ პ დ თ ტ გ ქ კ.

## Worked answers

1. *dila* "morning", *ṭba* "lake", *bina* "flat", *kalaki* "city".
2. პური, ფული, დიდი, თითი.
3. Height: კ hangs below the line and is *ḳ*; პ rises above it and is *ṗ*. The shape is the same.
4. Because the first twenty-two letters follow the Greek order. After ზ (Greek zeta) Old Georgian had a letter in the slot of Greek eta, now dropped (lesson 1-8); then came თ in the slot of theta. თ is the aspirate *t* because theta was an aspirate.
5. ტ, the ejective *ṭ*. Aronson notes that Georgian usually renders the plain voiceless stops of European languages as ejectives, as *ṗoliṭiḳa* shows.
6. They were two different Greek sounds: theta, an aspirate, became თ *t*; tau, a plain *t*, became ტ *ṭ*. The alphabet's own order pairs them the same way.
7. Between the lines: თ. Above: ბ პ. Below: ფ დ გ კ. Above and below: ტ ქ.
