## The idea

Twelve letters remain: the six affricates, the fricatives *z*, *ž*, *š*, *ɣ* and *x*, and the uvular ejective *q̇*. Learn them and you can read any modern Georgian word.

Most of these letters stand at the end of the alphabet, and that is not chance. Lesson 1-6 showed that the first twenty-two letters follow the Greek order. Greek had no letters for sounds such as *ɣ*, *q̇*, *š*, *č*, *c*, *ʒ*, *c̣*, *č̣*, *x* or *ǯ*, so these were added after the Greek part, from position 23 on. Aronson's table shows exactly this, and Bolkvadze and Kiziria describe the same principle: the sounds of the borrowing language are placed at the end of the borrowed series. Only *z* and *ž* sit earlier, in slots of the Greek sequence.

So the alphabet has two halves. The first half is Greek in order. The second half is the Georgian sounds Greek lacked, and in it the affricates come together: *č c ʒ c̣ č̣*. This lesson teaches the letters, then the whole order.

## How it is built

**Fricatives and q̇**

- ზ *z*: like an 8, rising above the line. Name: *zen*.
- ჟ *ž*: like ე with an extra notch at its top, hanging below the line. Name: *žan*.
- შ *š*: like მ with a second curl on top; rises above. Name: *šin*.
- ღ *ɣ*: a wide double loop with a tail below the line, close in shape to დ; compare them carefully. Name: *ɣan*.
- ხ *x*: a tall stem on the left with a closed bowl, like a Latin b; rises above. Name: *xan*.
- ყ *q̇*: two arms meeting in a tail below the line, like a Latin y. Name: *q̇ar*.

**The ts-series**

- ძ *ʒ*: a bowl with a straight stem rising on the right, like a Latin d. Compare მ, whose stem curls over at the top. Name: *ʒil*.
- ც *c*: a hook opening to the right, hanging below the line. Name: *can*.
- წ *c̣*: like ნ with an extra hook at the top of its stem; rises above. Name: *c̣il*.

**The ch-series**

- ჯ *ǯ*: crossed strokes, like a hooked x, hanging below the line. Name: *ǯan*.
- ჩ *č*: an arch with a tall stem on the left, like a Latin h; rises above. Name: *čin*.
- ჭ *č̣*: a stem with a crossbar and a hook, rising above and hanging below. Name: *č̣ar*.

**The order of the alphabet**

Here are all thirty-three letters in order, with this guide's transliteration. The first twenty-two follow Greek; the last eleven are the additions.

ა a, ბ b, გ g, დ d, ე e, ვ v, ზ z, თ t, ი i, კ ḳ, ლ l, მ m, ნ n, ო o, პ ṗ, ჟ ž, რ r, ს s, ტ ṭ, უ u, ფ p, ქ k; then ღ ɣ, ყ q̇, შ š, ჩ č, ც c, ძ ʒ, წ c̣, ჭ č̣, ხ x, ჯ ǯ, ჰ h.

To learn it, take it in three chunks: *a* to *n* (thirteen letters, with the familiar Greek start *a b g d e*), *o* to *k* (nine letters, ending the Greek part), and *ɣ* to *h* (eleven letters, the Georgian additions). Dictionaries, indexes and the vocabulary of this guide all use this order, so you will need it to look anything up.

**Reading any word.** You now have every letter. The method is the one from lessons 1-5 and 1-6: height first, then shape, then sound. Watch for harmonic clusters (lesson 1-4): წყ, ცხ, დღ and their kind are very common.

## Watch out

**Latin look-alikes lie.** ჩ looks like h and is *č*; ხ looks like b and is *x*; ყ looks like y and is *q̇*; ი looks like n and is *i*. Read the Georgian letter, not the Latin one it resembles.

**მ, ძ, შ.** Three bowls with a stem on the right. მ curls over at the top; ძ has a straight stem; შ has two curls.

**ნ and წ.** წ is ნ with an extra hook at the top.

**დ and ღ.** Both are wide with a tail below the line. Typefaces differ in how clearly they separate them; learn the difference in the font you read.

**Two words for "cow".** *ʒroxa* is the everyday word; *puri* (lesson 1-2) means a cow or the female of a bovine animal.

## Connections

The Armenian alphabet, created about the same time as the Georgian, also follows Greek in part, but Bolkvadze and Kiziria note that it scatters its own new letters through the sequence instead of placing them at the end. Coptic and Gothic, like Georgian, kept the Greek order and the Greek number values.

## Exercises

1. Read aloud and transliterate: ძაღლი, ჩიტი, ყველი, ხორცი.
2. Write in Mkhedruli: *saxli*, *c̣igni*, *zamtari*, *ǯibe*.
3. Which letters look like Latin h, b and y, and what do they stand for?
4. Why do ღ, ყ, შ, ჩ and the other later letters stand at the end of the alphabet?
5. Put in alphabetical order: ხორცი, ბინა, ყველი, ჩიტი, ეზო.
6. How do you tell მ from ძ?
7. Find the harmonic cluster in წყალი and say which two letters make it.
8. Recite the last eleven letters of the alphabet in transliteration.

## Worked answers

1. *ʒaɣli* "dog", *čiṭi* "bird", *q̇veli* "cheese", *xorci* "meat".
2. სახლი, წიგნი, ზამთარი, ჯიბე.
3. ჩ looks like h and is *č* [tʃʰ]; ხ looks like b and is *x* [χ]; ყ looks like y and is *q̇*.
4. Because the first twenty-two letters copy the Greek order, and Greek had no letters for these sounds. They were added after the Greek part, as Aronson's table shows.
5. ბინა (ბ, 2), ეზო (ე, 5), ყველი (ყ, 24), ჩიტი (ჩ, 26), ხორცი (ხ, 31).
6. მ's stem curls over at the top; ძ's stem is straight.
7. წყ, *c̣q̇*: two ejectives, front then back, released together (lesson 1-4).
8. *ɣ, q̇, š, č, c, ʒ, c̣, č̣, x, ǯ, h*.
