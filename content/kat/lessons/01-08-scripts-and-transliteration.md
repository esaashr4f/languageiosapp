## The idea

Mkhedruli is the third Georgian script, not the first. Georgian has been written in three, one after the other. Bolkvadze and Kiziria describe them as stages of one development: the same letters, in the same order, with the same names, changing shape over time.

1. **Asomtavruli**, also called **Mrgvlovani** "rounded": the oldest script, of round, equal-height letters. Hewitt dates its use from the fifth to the ninth century. The two oldest dated Georgian inscriptions, of about 430 in the Holy Land and of 494 at Bolnisi, are in this script.
2. **Nuskhuri**, also called **Kutkhovani** "angular": a smaller, angular book hand that developed from Asomtavruli. Hewitt gives its first appearance as a manuscript of 864 and says it was used for most manuscripts of the tenth and eleventh centuries, with Asomtavruli letters as capitals. The church kept this pair, called **Khutsuri** "ecclesiastical", for sacred texts.
3. **Mkhedruli**: the modern script, which Hewitt and Aronson say developed in the eleventh century. Bolkvadze and Kiziria note that it took the round shapes of Asomtavruli and the four-line layout of Nuskhuri.

That history explains why Georgian has **no capital letters**. Hewitt notes that each of the three scripts, on its own, is a single set of letters with no capitals. Only in church books was one script used as capitals for another: Asomtavruli initials in Nuskhuri text. Mkhedruli never acquired a capital set of its own. One attempt to change this failed: Wikipedia reports that the linguist Akaki Shanidze proposed, in the 1950s, using Asomtavruli letters as sentence capitals in Mkhedruli, and the idea did not catch on.

What Mkhedruli does have is **Mtavruli** (*mtavruli* "capital, main"): forms of the same letters drawn all at one height. Today they are used for whole words in capitals, on headings, signs and covers. Wikipedia notes that around 1900 they were occasionally used as initial capitals for names and sentences, as in Latin script; that use has gone. Unicode encodes them as separate characters.

### Who made the alphabet?

The origin of the Georgian alphabet is debated, and the sources disagree. Here is what each claims.

- **King Parnavaz.** A medieval Georgian chronicle, *The Life of the Kings*, says that Parnavaz, the first king of Kartli, created Georgian writing in the third century BC. Bolkvadze and Kiziria note that no written records of that period survive to support it; Hewitt calls it a legend.
- **Mesrop Mashtots.** Armenian sources say that Mashtots, who created the Armenian alphabet around 400 AD, also created the Georgian one. Hewitt lists this as the second legend. Bolkvadze and Kiziria report that Georgian scholars reject it: the Georgian alphabet's order follows Greek and puts Georgian sounds at the end, while Armenian scatters its new letters, and the claim may be a later addition to the Armenian text. Wikipedia notes that some scholars outside Georgia have repeated it.
- **A Semitic model.** Ivane Javakhishvili, Giorgi Tsereteli and Ramaz Pataridze argued for an origin in Aramaic or Phoenician writing.
- **A Greek model.** Thomas Gamkrelidze's comparison of alphabets argues that Georgian was built on Greek. Hewitt, Aronson and Bolkvadze and Kiziria all call this the most widely accepted view.

Most scholars, Hewitt writes, place the creation in the fourth century, after Christianity came to Kartli in the 330s. That is the probable view, not a proven one.

## How it is built

The **transliteration** of this guide is the scheme Wiktionary uses, the standard one among Caucasus specialists. It follows three principles.

1. One Georgian letter, one Latin letter. There are no pairs like "sh" or "ts". So the Georgian can always be rebuilt from the transliteration, letter by letter.
2. Ejectives carry a dot: *ḳ ṗ ṭ c̣ č̣* below, *ṗ q̇* above. Aspirates are plain: *k p t c č*.
3. Everything is lower case, because Georgian has no capitals.

To convert either way, go letter by letter with the table at the end of this lesson. Mind the marks: *c*, *č*, *c̣* and *č̣* are four different letters.

You will meet other systems. The **national system** of 2002, used on maps, road signs and passports, writes the aspirates plain and the ejectives with an apostrophe, and uses pairs of letters: *sh*, *ch*, *ts*, *dz*, *zh*, *kh*, *gh*, and *j* for *ǯ*. It is easier to type and harder to reverse: *ts* could be ც or თ followed by ს. Hewitt's grammar marks ejectives with an apostrophe (*k'aci*). Aronson uses dots, as this guide does, but leaves *q* undotted because modern Georgian has no plain *q* to confuse it with.

Old texts also use five letters that modern Georgian has dropped: ჱ, ჲ, ჳ, ჴ and ჵ. Three more letters, ჶ, ჷ and ჸ, were added for other languages written in the script, such as Svan and Mingrelian.

## Watch out

**Mtavruli is not a capital letter.** Do not start a Georgian sentence or name with a Mtavruli letter. Write the whole word in Mtavruli or none of it.

**Apostrophes and dots mean the same thing.** *k'* in Hewitt or on a map is *ḳ* here. A plain *k* is the aspirate in all three systems.

**National-system pairs can mislead.** In *kh* or *gh* on a map, the pair is usually one letter (ხ, ღ), but could in principle be two (ქ + ჰ, გ + ჰ).

## Connections

Japanese uses several scripts at once; Georgian's three are one alphabet in three historical styles, and only Mkhedruli is in everyday use. *anbani*, named from its first two letters, is the same idea as Greek *alphabētos* and Arabic *abjad*.

2. წიგნი, ძროხა, ჩაი, ყავა.
3. *c̣q̇ali* "water" and *ḳaci* "man".
4. Because none of the Georgian scripts has capitals on its own. Capitals existed only when one script, Asomtavruli, was used as initials for another, Nuskhuri, in church books. Mkhedruli never acquired a capital set, and Shanidze's proposal to add one in the 1950s did not catch on.
5. Mtavruli. No: in modern use Mtavruli writes whole words in capitals, not a single initial letter.
6.
