## The idea

The simplest sentence in any language says that one thing is another: "this is a house", "Tbilisi is a city", "the room is cold". Georgian builds it from two pieces you can learn today: a noun in its naming form, and a short verb meaning "is".

**Case** is a form of a noun that shows what job it does in the sentence. Georgian has seven cases, each with its own ending; part 3 lays them out. This lesson needs only one of them, the **nominative**. It is the form you find in a dictionary, the form for naming a thing, and the form for saying what something is. Its Georgian name, *saxelobiti*, is built on *saxeli* "name". The nominative of most nouns ends in -ი *-i*: *saxli* "house", *c̣igni* "book", *kalaki* "city".

The verb is არის *aris*, "is". A verb that links a thing to what it is, rather than describing an action, is called a **copula**. The noun or adjective after it, the one that says what the subject is, is called the **predicate**. Bolkvadze and Kiziria call the pair a compound predicate: a noun or adjective plus "be".

Why is the predicate in the nominative? Because nothing happens to it. In "this is a house" the house is not acted on; it is the same thing as "this", seen from another side. Bolkvadze and Kiziria note that the noun part of a compound predicate is "mostly in the nominative". The same is true in Latin and in older English ("it is I").

Georgian has a second, shorter "is": the ending -ა *-a*, attached to the end of the predicate. Wiktionary describes it as a contraction of *aris*. *Es saxlia* and *es saxli aris* both mean "this is a house". A word that cannot stand alone and leans on the word before it is called an **enclitic**; the glosses mark the join with `=`.

## How it is built

**The nominative.** Take the noun's stem and add -ი *-i*. The **stem** is the part of the noun that stays the same while endings change: *saxl-*, *kalak-*, *c̣ign-*. Every stem in the examples so far ends in a consonant, and every one of them adds *-i*.

Nouns whose stem ends in a vowel add nothing: *deda* "mother", *ʒma* "brother", *xe* "tree", *ezo* "yard". Bolkvadze and Kiziria state it plainly: these nouns have no case ending in the nominative. So the dictionary form *deda* is already the nominative, and the gloss shows no NOM for it. Part 3 explains what happens to these nouns in the other cases.

**The full copula.** Put the subject first, the predicate second, *aris* last:

1. I want to say "Tbilisi is a city".
2. The subject is *tbilisi*, in the nominative (stem *tbilis-* plus *-i*).
3. The predicate is "city", *kalaki*, also in the nominative.
4. "Is" comes last: *tbilisi kalaki aris*.

**The short copula.** Drop *aris* and attach *-a* to the last word of the predicate. The shape depends on how that word ends:

- After a vowel, add *-a*: *kalaki* → *kalakia*; *deda* → *dedaa*; *xe* → *xea*. The *-i* of the nominative stays: *kalakia*, never *kalaka*.
- After a consonant, the vowel is written twice: *-aa*. Bolkvadze and Kiziria give *avadaa* "is ill" from *avad*, and *esaa* "is this" from *es*.

So "Tbilisi is a city" is also *tbilisi kalakia*. Both forms are correct Georgian. Bolkvadze and Kiziria give them as equivalents (*ekimia* = *ekimi aris* "is a doctor") and note that with a third person singular subject the short form is often all there is. This guide uses both.

The short form exists only for "is", the third person singular. The other persons (I am, you are, they are) have their own forms, taught in the next lesson. In the glosses below, *aris* and *-a* are labelled `be.3SG`, "be, third person singular". That label is a preview of lesson 2-2, which explains persons.

## Where it applies

- **Naming and labelling.** A bare nominative names a thing, on its own, as on a sign or in a list: *saxli*, *deda*. Bolkvadze and Kiziria count such one-word sentences as "noun-sentences", and they are in the nominative.
- **Identity and kind.** "X is a Y" or "X is the Y": *tbilisi kalakia*, *mama ekimia*. Both nouns are nominative.
- **Description.** "X is big, cold, beautiful": *es otaxi ʒalian civia*. The adjective is nominative, with *-i*.
- **A noun with its description.** The predicate can be an adjective plus a noun: *sakartvelo lamazi kveq̇anaa*. The adjective goes first and keeps its *-i* in the nominative; *-a* goes on the last word.
- **States and conditions.** *mama avadaa* "Father is ill". Where Spanish needs *estar*, Georgian uses the same copula.
- **Place.** With a place word: *mama ak aris* "Father is here", *saxli ik aris* "the house is there". Phrases like "in the house" need the postpositions of lesson 3-2.
- **Existence.** "There is": *ak ṭq̇ea*. Bolkvadze and Kiziria have the same pattern for "everywhere there are flowers" and "now it is sunny" (literally "now sun-is").
- **With any word that ends the predicate.** *-a* attaches to a noun, an adjective, an adverb (*avadaa*) or a pronoun (*esaa*). Later lessons add more: the genitive "of" (lesson 3-3) and participles (part 4).
- **Not yet covered.** "I am", "you are" and "they are": lesson 2-2. "Is not": lesson 2-7. "Is it?": lesson 2-8. Polite forms that replace *aris* in formal speech: part 7.

## Watch out

**Do not use both copulas at once.** *\*Es saxlia aris* says "is" twice. Choose one: *es saxlia* or *es saxli aris*.

**Do not drop the copula.** Russian or Arabic speakers leave "is" out in the present. In Georgian, *es saxli* without a copula is a phrase, "this house", not a sentence. Say *es saxlia*.

**Do not cut off the nominative *-i*.** The short copula goes after the whole nominative: *ekimia*, not *\*ekima*. The *-i* belongs to the noun; the *-a* is added to it.

**Do not double the *a* after a vowel.** *-aa* is for words that end in a consonant (*avadaa*, *esaa*). After a vowel there is one *a*: *kalakia*, *ḳaṭaa*. In *ḳaṭaa* the first *a* is the noun's own.

**Do not start a sentence with *-a*.** It is an enclitic and needs a word before it. Only the full *aris* can stand apart: *es aris saxli* "this is a house" is also possible, with *aris* in the middle. Bolkvadze and Kiziria note that the order of the two parts of a compound predicate is not fixed.

**Do not use *-a* for "I am" or "you are".** *\*Me ekimia* mixes "I" with "is". The short form is only for a third person singular subject. The next lesson gives *var* "am" and *xar* "are".

**Do not put the predicate in another case.** Latin learners know this one. Nothing is acted on, so nothing goes into an object case: the predicate stays nominative, like the subject.

**"It is cold" is not *civia*.** *Civia* means that a thing is cold: *es otaxi civia*. Weather uses a separate verb, ცივა *civa* "it is cold (out)". Bolkvadze and Kiziria list it among verbs that grew out of compound predicates. It is noted here so that you do not build *\*civia* for the weather.

**No word for "a" or "the".** *Saxli* is "a house" or "the house". Lesson 2-3 explains why Georgian needs no articles.

## Connections

Japanese です and Korean 이다 also come at the end and also attach closely to the noun; Korean 이다 even shortens after a vowel, as *-a* is shaped by what it follows. Spanish needs two verbs, *ser* and *estar*, where Georgian has one: *mama ekimia* and *mama avadaa* use the same copula.

## Exercises

1. Give the nominative of the stems *ḳac-*, *sḳam-* and *c̣ign-*, and say why *deda* has no ending.
2. Say "This is a chair" with the short copula and with the full one.
3. Say "Tbilisi is a beautiful city".
4. Add the short copula to *kuča*, *ekimi*, *ʒveli* and *es*, and explain each shape.
5. Take apart *es magidaa* and translate it.
6. A learner writes *\*Es otaxi civi* for "This room is cold". What is missing, and what does the learner's version mean?
7. Say "The teacher is ill".
8. Say "There is a cat here".

## Worked answers

1. Each stem ends in a consonant, so each adds *-i*: *ḳaci* "man", *sḳami* "chair", *c̣igni* "book". *Deda* has a stem ending in a vowel, and vowel stems take no ending in the nominative.
2. "This" is *es*. "Chair" is the predicate, nominative *sḳami*. Short: *es sḳamia*, with *-a* after the *-i*. Full: *es sḳami aris*.
3. The subject is *tbilisi*. The predicate is "beautiful city": the adjective first, *lamazi kalaki*, both nominative. The copula goes on the last word: *tbilisi lamazi kalakia*.
4. *kučaa* (a vowel stem: the noun's *a*, then the copula's). *ekimia* (the nominative *-i* stays, *-a* follows). *ʒvelia* (an adjective in the nominative, then *-a*). *esaa* (a consonant at the end, so *-aa*): "it is this one".
5. *es* "this"; *magida* "table", a vowel stem with no nominative ending; *=a* "is". "This is a table."
6. The copula. Without it the words only name a room and a quality; nothing links them, so there is no sentence. Say *es otaxi civia*, or *es otaxi civi aris*.
7. "Teacher" is the subject, *masc̣avlebeli*. "Ill" is *avad*, which ends in a consonant, so the short copula is *-aa*: *masc̣avlebeli avadaa*. With the full form: *masc̣avlebeli avad aris*.
8. Existence uses the copula with the place word first: *ak ḳaṭaa*. The vowel stem *ḳaṭa* takes a single *-a*.
