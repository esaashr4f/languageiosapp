## The idea

Spanish and French make you decide two things about every noun before you can use it: is it masculine or feminine, and is it "a" or "the"? Georgian asks neither question. A noun has one form for "a house" and "the house", and nothing in the sentence changes because the noun names a man, a woman or a thing.

**Grammatical gender** is a sorting of nouns into classes, such as masculine and feminine, that other words must agree with: Spanish *la casa blanca*, *el libro blanco*. Georgian has none. The World Atlas of Language Structures (WALS) lists Georgian with "no gender" (feature 30A). No noun is masculine, no adjective changes its ending to match, and no pronoun or verb shows sex. You saw part of this in lesson 2-2: *is* is "he", "she" and "it" at once.

An **article** is a small word that marks a noun as known or new: English "the" and "a", French *le* and *un*. Modern Georgian has none either. *Saxli* is "a house" or "the house"; the context decides. Bolkvadze and Kiziria note that older Georgian did mark this difference, and that the modern language expresses it by other means when it needs to. The main one is the word ერთი *erti* "one", which can mean "a certain", "a".

Georgian does sort nouns, but along a different line. Bolkvadze and Kiziria divide them into nouns for people, which answer the question ვინ *vin* "who?", and nouns for everything else, animals included, which answer რა *ra* "what?". This split matters later: Georgian has two verbs "to have", one for people and animals and one for things (lesson 4-19). It never matters for agreement in the way gender does in Spanish.

So where English and Spanish carry sex in grammar, Georgian carries it only in the words themselves: *deda* "mother" and *mama* "father", *coli* "wife" and *kmari* "husband". When it needs to say the sex of a person or animal explicitly, it builds a compound. Bolkvadze and Kiziria list these: ქალიშვილი *kališvili* "daughter" is *kali* "woman" plus *švili* "child"; ვაჟიშვილი *važišvili* "son" is *važi* "son, young man" plus *švili*; მამაკაცი *mamaḳaci* "man, male" is *mama* "father" plus *ḳaci* "person". The word *kalbaṭoni* "Ms" from lesson 2-2 is the same kind of compound: *kali* "woman" plus *baṭoni* "master". Wiktionary gives a striking old one: დედოფალი *dedopali* "queen" is *deda* "mother" plus *upali* "lord".

## How it is built

**Gender.** There is nothing to build. Take the noun or adjective in its nominative and use it. An adjective has the same form whatever it describes:

1. I want to say "Nino is a pretty girl" and "Giorgi is a handsome boy".
2. The adjective is *lamazi* for both. It does not change.
3. *Nino lamazi gogoa*, *giorgi lamazi bič̣ia*. English needs two adjectives; Georgian uses one twice.

**"A" and "the".** Use the bare noun for both. Whether the reader takes it as new or known depends on the situation. To mark a noun clearly as new, put *erti* in front of it:

1. I want to say "There is a house here. The house is very old."
2. The house is new to the listener. Put *erti* in front: *ak erti saxlia*.
3. Now the house is known. Use the bare noun: *saxli ʒalian ʒvelia*.

*Erti* before a noun takes no extra ending in the nominative: *erti saxli*, *erti ḳaci*. It is the same word as the number "one", and it keeps that meaning when you count. Bolkvadze and Kiziria say *erti ḳaci* means literally "one man", but in some contexts "a man", "a certain man" or "a person".

**"One ... the other".** *Erti* pairs with მეორე *meore* "second, the other": *erti ʒma ekimia, meore msaxiobia* "one brother is a doctor, the other is an actor". Wiktionary derives *meore* from *ori* "two" with a circumfix for ordinal numbers, part 5.

**"This" and "that".** When Georgian needs to point, it uses *es* "this" and *is* "that" before the noun, as in lesson 2-1: *es oǯaxi* "this family". These are pointing words, not articles. Lesson 3-9 adds a third.

## Where it applies

- **Every noun, in every case.** No noun has a gender, so no rule anywhere in the grammar depends on one. Part 3 adds six more cases and part 4 the verb; none of them ever asks whether a noun is masculine or feminine.
- **Every adjective.** *Lamazi*, *ʒveli*, *didi* have one set of forms for all nouns. They do change with case, which is a different matter (lesson 3-10).
- **Every pronoun and verb.** *Is* and *isini* cover men, women and things; *aris* and *arian* are the same for all of them.
- **Nouns for people of either sex.** Most words for people by job, role or nationality cover both: *ekimi*, *masc̣avlebeli*, *msaxiobi*, *mezobeli*, *mosc̣avle*, *sṭudenṭi*, *kartveli*.
- **Words that do name a sex.** Pairs of separate words (*deda* and *mama*, *coli* and *kmari*, *bebia* and *babua*, *mepe* and *dedopali*, *puri* "cow" and *xari* "ox") and compounds (*kališvili*, *važišvili*, *mamaḳaci*, *kalbaṭoni*).
- **The bare noun as "a" or "the".** In naming (*saxli*), in "X is a Y" (*mama ekimia*), and for something already known (*saxli ʒalian ʒvelia*).
- **erti for something new.** To bring a person or thing into a conversation or a story: *ak erti saxlia*. It is a natural way to begin a story about someone.
- **erti as the number.** "One house", contrasting with two or three: lesson 2-4.
- **erti ... meore.** "One ... the other" for two people or things.

## Watch out

**Do not make the adjective agree.** A Spanish speaker may want *\*lamaza gogo* for a girl, by analogy with *bonita*. Georgian adjectives have no feminine form: *lamazi gogo*, *lamazi bič̣i*.

**Do not read *-a* as feminine.** In Spanish and Italian *-a* often means a feminine noun. In Georgian *mama* "father", *biʒa* "uncle" and *babua* "grandfather" all end in *-a*. The ending of a noun tells you how it is declined, never its sex.

**Do not invent feminine forms of jobs.** Spanish has *maestro* and *maestra*. *\*Masc̣avlebela* does not exist. *Masc̣avlebeli* is a man or a woman.

**Do not use *erti* for every "a".** English puts "a" before almost every singular noun. Georgian uses *erti* only when it wants to bring something in as new, or to count. *Es saxlia* is "this is a house". *Es erti saxlia* puts weight on "one": "this is one house".

**Do not use *erti* in "I am a doctor".** French and English say *un médecin*, "a doctor", in some patterns. Georgian says *ekimi var*. Adding *erti* turns it into counting or into "a certain doctor".

**Do not use *is* as "the".** *Is saxli* means "that house". "The house" is *saxli*. Pointing words point; they are not articles.

**Two people make a plural verb.** With two singular nouns joined by *da* "and", the verb is plural: *\*bebia da babua ak aris* has a singular verb for two people. Say *bebia da babua ak arian*. Bolkvadze and Kiziria give the rule: more than one subject, a plural predicate.

**Do not look for "he" or "she" in the Georgian.** Translating, take it from the context. If the context does not say, the Georgian does not either.

## Connections

Japanese and Korean also have neither gender nor articles, so a reader who knows them already thinks this way: 本 and 책 are "a book" or "the book" by context. Spanish *un* and French *un* also grew out of the word for "one", as English "a" did from "one"; Georgian *erti* does part of that job without having become an article. Arabic marks gender even on "you" and on verbs; Georgian marks it nowhere.

## Exercises

1. Say "Nino is a good teacher" and "Giorgi is a good teacher". What changes between them?
2. Say "There is a cat here. The cat is very small." Explain where you use *erti* and where you do not.
3. Translate *is mezobelia* in all the ways English allows.
4. Take apart *važišvili* and *kališvili*, and say what each part means.
5. A learner writes *\*Deida lamaza kalia* for "The aunt is a beautiful woman". Find the mistake and correct it.
6. Say "The husband and the wife are here."
7. Say "One grandfather is a writer, the other is a painter."
8. What is the difference between *es saxlia*, *es erti saxlia* and *is saxlia*?

## Worked answers

1. *Nino ḳargi masc̣avlebelia*; *giorgi ḳargi masc̣avlebelia*. Only the name changes. The adjective *ḳargi* has no gender, and *masc̣avlebeli* covers a man or a woman.
2. *Ak erti ḳaṭaa. Ḳaṭa ʒalian ṗaṭaraa.* The cat is new in the first sentence, so *erti* introduces it. In the second it is known, so the bare noun serves as "the cat". *Ḳaṭa* and *ṗaṭara* end in a vowel, so each takes a single *-a*.
3. "He is a neighbour", "she is a neighbour", "that is a neighbour". *Is* has no gender and also means "that"; *mezobeli* covers both sexes.
4. *važi* "son, young man" + *švili* "child" = "son". *kali* "woman" + *švili* "child" = "daughter". The first part says the sex; *švili* says the relation.
5. *Lamaza* is a Spanish-style feminine. Georgian adjectives do not agree in gender: *deida lamazi kalia*.
6. Two people, so the plural verb: *kmari da coli ak arian*.
7. *Erti babua mc̣eralia, meore mxaṭvaria.* *Erti ... meore* is "one ... the other"; each part has its own short copula.
8. *Es saxlia* is "this is a house". *Es erti saxlia* stresses the number: "this is one house". *Is saxlia* is "that is a house" or "it is a house".
