## The idea

Georgian makes a plural by adding one suffix, -ებ- *-eb-*, to the noun's stem. The case ending then follows it: *saxl-i* "house", *saxl-eb-i* "houses". It is the same suffix for every noun, people and things alike, and it never changes for case. Bolkvadze and Kiziria describe the noun as **agglutinative**: each meaning has its own piece, and the pieces are strung together in a fixed order. Stem, then plural, then case. You already know this order from Japanese and Korean, where 들 *-deul* or the case particles also follow the noun one after another.

Two facts make the Georgian plural different from Spanish or English, and both come from the same logic.

First, the plural is used only when nothing else in the phrase says "more than one". After a number, or a word like "many" or "several", the noun stays singular: *ori saxli* "two houses", literally "two house". Bolkvadze and Kiziria state it as a rule of the literary language: nouns preceded by numerals and quantifiers are always singular. A **quantifier** is a word for an amount, such as "many", "few", "several", "all". The number already carries the plurality, so the noun does not repeat it. That is a description, not a history: the sources consulted do not say how the rule arose, and in older Georgian (lesson 3-12) numbers and nouns did agree.

Second, a plural noun does not always make the verb plural. The verb agrees in number with people. With things, it usually stays singular: Bolkvadze and Kiziria give "there are round tables in the room" with a singular verb, and say a plural verb with things belongs to poetry and figurative style. So *eseni c̣ignebia* "these are books" has the singular short copula, but *eseni sṭudenṭebi arian* "these are students" has the plural.

## How it is built

**Consonant stems.** Add *-eb-*, then the case ending. In the nominative that is *-i*:

1. "Books": stem *c̣ign-*.
2. Add the plural: *c̣ign-eb-*.
3. Add the nominative: *c̣ignebi*.

**Stems in -a.** The final *-a* drops before *-eb-*: *deda* → *dedebi*, *ʒma* → *ʒmebi*, *magida* → *magidebi*, *ḳaṭa* → *ḳaṭebi*. Losing the last vowel of the stem like this is called **truncation**. Bolkvadze and Kiziria say nouns in *-a* truncate in every plural form.

**Stems in -e, -o, -u and -i.** The vowel stays and *-eb-* follows it: *xe* → *xeebi*, *mosc̣avle* → *mosc̣avleebi*, *mepe* → *mepeebi*, *ezo* → *ezoebi*. Nouns in *-e* never truncate (Bolkvadze and Kiziria).

**Nouns that lose a vowel inside.** Some nouns drop the vowel of their last syllable when an ending is added: *megobari* → *megobrebi*, *kmari* → *kmrebi*, *mezobeli* → *mezoblebi*, *kveq̇ana* → *kveq̇nebi*. This is called **syncope**. Lesson 3-6 explains which nouns do it and why. Until then, the vocabulary lists give the plural whenever it is not regular.

**Numbers and quantifiers.** Put the number before a singular noun. Both take the nominative ending: *or-i c̣ign-i* "two books". Numbers that end in a vowel, *rva* "eight" and *cxra* "nine", take no ending, like *deda*. The quantifiers work the same way: *bevri c̣igni* "many books", *ramdenime c̣igni* "several books", *q̇vela sṭudenṭi* "all the students".

**The verb.**

1. Is the subject a plural noun for people? The verb is plural: *sṭudenṭebi ak arian*.
2. Is it a plural noun for things? The verb is usually singular: *xeebi didia*.
3. Is it a singular noun after a number or quantifier? The noun is singular, and so is the verb: *xuti sṭudenṭi ak aris*. Bolkvadze and Kiziria's examples have singular verbs even with people here ("several men stood", "there were five students in each group").

**"These" and "those".** *Es* "this" has the plural ესენი *eseni* "these". *Is* has *isini*, which you know as "they" and which also means "those".

## Where it applies

- **Any noun that can be counted.** People, animals and things all take *-eb-*: *ḳacebi*, *ʒaɣlebi*, *saxlebi*.
- **In every case.** *-eb-* comes first and the case ending after it. Lesson 2-6 adds the dative: *saxl-eb-s*. Part 3 adds the rest; *-eb-* never changes.
- **After the pronouns eseni and isini.** "These are ..." and "those are ...": *eseni c̣ignebia*, *isini megobrebi arian*.
- **As the predicate.** A plural subject of people has a plural predicate: *čven kartvelebi vart* (lesson 2-2). One person addressed politely with *tkven* keeps a singular predicate.
- **Not after numbers.** *ori*, *sami* ... *ati* and every higher number take a singular noun: *ati c̣igni* "ten books".
- **Not after quantifiers.** *bevri* "many, much", *coṭa* "a little, few", *ramdenime* "several", *q̇vela* "all": *coṭa puli* "a little money", *ramdenime megobari* "several friends".
- **Collective nouns.** A noun for a group, such as *xalxi* "people" or *oǯaxi* "family", is singular and takes a singular verb, as Bolkvadze and Kiziria note.
- **Mass nouns.** Nouns for substances (*c̣q̇ali* "water", *ṗuri* "bread", *šakari* "sugar", *ɣvino* "wine") are not normally plural. Bolkvadze and Kiziria note one exception: the plural for kinds, as in "Georgian wines are famous".
- **The verb with plural things.** Usually singular (*xeebi didia*). A plural verb with things is poetic.
- **Not yet covered.** The old plural in *-n-* and *-t-* of formal style: lesson 3-12. Plural verbs other than "be": lesson 2-5.

## Watch out

**No plural after a number.** *\*Ori saxlebi* says "two" twice, as English and Spanish do. Say *ori saxli*. The same for *\*bevri c̣ignebi*: say *bevri c̣igni*.

**No plural verb after a number either.** *\*Xuti sṭudenṭi ak arian* makes the verb plural because there are five people. The noun is singular, so the verb is too: *xuti sṭudenṭi ak aris*.

**Plural things usually take a singular verb.** *\*Eseni c̣ignebi arian* sounds poetic or odd, as if the books were people. Say *eseni c̣ignebia*. With people, the opposite: *\*eseni sṭudenṭebia* treats students as things. Say *eseni sṭudenṭebi arian*.

**Drop the -a, keep the -e.** *\*Dedaebi* keeps a vowel that truncates. Say *dedebi*. But *\*xebi* drops a vowel that stays: say *xeebi*, with two e's, each in its own syllable.

**Watch for syncope.** *\*Megobarebi* keeps a vowel the noun loses. Say *megobrebi*. When in doubt, check the vocabulary note.

**Do not pluralise xalxi for "people".** English "people" is plural, so *\*xalxi arian* looks natural. *Xalxi* is a singular collective noun: *ik bevri xalxia*. For several individual persons, use *adamianebi* "human beings" or *ḳacebi*.

**"These two" is es, not eseni.** English "these" is plural, so *\*eseni ori c̣igni* looks natural, but the counted noun is singular and the pointer before it is too: *es ori c̣igni* "these two books". Bolkvadze and Kiziria have the same pattern in *es xutive c̣igni* "all these five books". Lesson 3-9 covers pointing words before nouns.

**The plural -t of verbs is a different thing.** *Vart* and *xart* end in *-t* for "we" and "you all". In everyday modern Georgian nouns never take *-t* for the plural; their plural is *-eb-*. (The formal old plural of lesson 3-12 is the exception.)

## Connections

Korean and Japanese also leave the noun bare after a number (책 두 권, 本二冊), and Korean 들 is optional where Georgian *-eb-* is required. Arabic is the closest match for the verb rule: in Modern Standard Arabic a plural of things takes a singular (feminine) verb, while a plural of people takes a plural one. *Xalxi* itself is a loan from Arabic *ḵalq* "creation, people" (Wiktionary).

## Exercises

1. Make the plural of *kalaki*, *ḳaṭa*, *mepe* and *ezo*, and say what happens to each stem.
2. Say "These are flowers" and "These are teachers". Why do the verbs differ?
3. Say "three books", "many friends" and "all the children".
4. Correct *\*Ak ori ḳacebi arian*.
5. Take apart *mtebi* and *mdinareebi* piece by piece.
6. Say "There are a lot of people here."
7. Say "The pens and the pencils are new."
8. A learner writes *\*Megobarebi ak arian*. What is wrong?

## Worked answers

1. *kalakebi*: a consonant stem, so *-eb-* and *-i*. *ḳaṭebi*: an *-a* stem truncates. *mepeebi*: an *-e* stem keeps its vowel. *ezoebi*: an *-o* stem keeps its vowel.
2. *Eseni q̇vavilebia*: flowers are things, so the verb stays singular and the short copula is fine. *Eseni masc̣avleblebi arian*: teachers are people, so the verb is plural. *Masc̣avlebeli* syncopates, as its vocabulary note says.
3. *Sami c̣igni*, *bevri megobari*, *q̇vela bavšvi*. Each noun is singular, because the number or quantifier already says "more than one".
4. After *ori* the noun is singular, and the verb follows the noun: *ak ori ḳacia*, or *ak ori ḳaci aris*. "There are two men here."
5. *mt-eb-i*: stem *mt-* (from *mta*, with the *-a* truncated), plural *-eb-*, nominative *-i*. *mdinare-eb-i*: the stem keeps its *-e*, then *-eb-* and *-i*.
6. *Ak bevri xalxia*. *Xalxi* is a singular collective noun, and *bevri* takes a singular noun anyway.
7. Pens and pencils are things, so the verb is singular: *ḳalmebi da pankrebi axalia*. Both nouns syncopate (*ḳalami* → *ḳalmebi*, *pankari* → *pankrebi*).
8. *Megobari* syncopates: its plural is *megobrebi*. The verb *arian* is right, because friends are people: *megobrebi ak arian*.
