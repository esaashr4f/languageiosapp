## The idea

Georgian negates a sentence with a small word in front of the verb. There are two everyday ones, and they do not mean the same thing:

- არ *ar* "not": *ar vc̣er* "I do not write, I am not writing"
- ვერ *ver* "cannot": *ver vc̣er* "I cannot write, I am unable to write"

Bolkvadze and Kiziria describe the difference exactly: *ar* is a plain, categorical negation of the statement; *ver* says the person is unable to do the action. English and Spanish need a separate verb for "can" (*poder*) and put "not" with it. Georgian puts the whole idea of "not able" into one particle, and the verb stays as it was. You can say "I can't see the road" in Georgian before you know any verb for "can".

A word like *ar* that does a grammatical job, does not change form and stands on its own, is called a **particle**. WALS classes Georgian negation this way: a negative particle (feature 112A), placed before the verb (143A). The order is fixed: the particle comes directly before the verb, with nothing between them.

Two more negative particles are built from these. Wiktionary derives აღარ *aɣar* "no longer, not any more" from *ar* plus the particle *-ɣa*, and ვეღარ *veɣar* "can no longer" from *ver* plus *-ɣa*. Bolkvadze and Kiziria explain their use: they say that something that used to be, or was intended, has stopped. And არც *arc*, from *ar* plus *-c* "also, too" (Wiktionary), means "not ... either"; used twice, *arc ... arc* is "neither ... nor".

A third, ნუ *nu*, is for "don't!" in commands. It belongs with commands in lesson 4-17. The word არა *ara* "no", as a whole answer, is in the next lesson.

## How it is built

**With a verb.** Build the positive sentence, then put *ar* or *ver* right before the verb:

1. Positive: *giorgi c̣igns ḳitxulobs* "Giorgi is reading a book".
2. "Not": *giorgi c̣igns ar ḳitxulobs* "Giorgi is not reading a book".
3. "Cannot": *giorgi c̣igns ver ḳitxulobs* "Giorgi cannot read the book".

Nothing else changes: same cases, same person markers.

**With "be".** The copula is a verb, so the particle goes right before it: *ar var*, *ar xar*, *ar aris*, *ar vart*, *ar xart*, *ar arian*. The short copula *-a* cannot be negated, because it is not a separate word that *ar* could stand in front of. Use the full *aris*:

1. Positive: *es saxlia* "this is a house".
2. Negative: *es saxli ar aris* "this is not a house".

The predicate may also follow the negated verb: *es ar aris saxli*. Bolkvadze and Kiziria have examples of both orders, with the predicate before and after *ar aris*.

**"There is no ...".** The copula of existence negates the same way: *ak ṭq̇e ar aris* "there is no forest here".

**aɣar, veɣar.** They take the place of *ar* and *ver*: *aɣar vc̣er* "I no longer write"; *veɣar vxedav* "I can no longer see (it)".

**arc ... arc.** Put *arc* before each of the two things you deny and join the two halves with *da* "and". When the first *arc* phrase stands before the verb, as here, the verb takes no further *ar* in Bolkvadze and Kiziria's examples or in Wiktionary's: *arc kalaks vxedav da arc sopels* "I see neither the town nor the village". (When *arc ... arc* or *verc ... verc* pairs two subjects before the verb, Bolkvadze and Kiziria also have the verb keeping its own particle: "neither I nor you can help this person" is *verc me da verc šen ... ver vušvelit*. You will not need that pattern yet.)

## Where it applies

- **Any verb in the present.** *ar* or *ver* before it: *ar vc̣er*, *ver vxedav*, *ar mɣerian*.
- **The copula in every person.** *ar var*, *ar xar*, *ar aris*, *ar vart*, *ar xart*, *ar arian*, always with the full form.
- **Existence.** "There is no ...": *ak ṭq̇e ar aris*.
- **Correcting.** "Not X but Y": *ekimi ar var, masc̣avlebeli var*. The positive half needs no word for "but", though *magram* "but" can join two clauses.
- **Inability of any kind.** Not knowing how, not being able, not managing: *ver c̣ers* "can't write", *ver vxedav* "I can't see it". Bolkvadze and Kiziria's examples include failing to find something and being unable to help.
- **Not any more.** *aɣar* and *veɣar*: *aɣar xaṭavs*.
- **Neither ... nor.** *arc ... arc*.
- **Not yet.** *ǯer* "yet" with *ar*: *ǯer ar vc̣er* "I am not writing yet".
- **Not at all.** *saertod* "at all" with *ar*: *saertod ar vc̣er* "I don't write at all".
- **In later tenses.** *ar* and *ver* stand before every tense of the verb; part 4 uses them throughout.
- **Not here.** Commands ("don't write!"): lesson 4-17. "Nobody", "nothing": lesson 3-11.

## Watch out

**The particle goes before the verb, not after it.** English "I write not" is archaic, but German *ich schreibe nicht* and French *je n'écris pas* put the negation after the verb. *\*Vc̣er ar* is wrong. Say *ar vc̣er*.

**Nothing between the particle and the verb.** *\*Ar c̣igns ḳitxulobs* puts the object between *ar* and the verb, as English "not a book" might suggest. To deny the sentence, *ar* must touch the verb: *c̣igns ar ḳitxulobs*.

**No negated short copula.** *\*Es ar saxlia* tries to negate *-a*. Say *es saxli ar aris* or *es ar aris saxli*.

**Do not use ar for "can't".** *Ar vxedav* is "I don't see it" (for example, I am not looking). If you mean that you are unable to, say *ver vxedav*. English and Spanish blur this in "I can't see it" and *no lo veo*; Georgian does not.

**Do not look for a verb "can" yet.** Spanish *no puedo escribir* suggests "can" plus a verb. In the present, Georgian says *ver vc̣er*. A verb meaning "can, be able" exists, but it is an indirect verb (lesson 4-18) and you will rarely need it for simple sentences.

**Do not add ar after an arc that already stands before the verb.** In *arc kalaks vxedav da arc sopels*, the first *arc* sits right before the verb's phrase and carries the negation; the examples in Bolkvadze and Kiziria and in Wiktionary of this pattern have no second *ar*, so do not add one. (Bolkvadze and Kiziria do have a negated verb after a pair of negated subjects, *verc me da verc šen ... ver vušvelit*, so the rule is about this pattern, not every *arc*.)

**Do not use ar for a command.** *Ar c̣er* is a statement, "you don't write", not "don't write!". Commands use *nu* or another form (lesson 4-17).

**Do not confuse aɣar with ar.** *Ar xaṭavs* says she does not paint; *aɣar xaṭavs* says she used to and has stopped. Use *aɣar* only when there is a change.

## Connections

Japanese and Korean also have a separate "can't" form, though built differently: Korean 못 *mot* before the verb (못 써요 "I can't write") is a close match for Georgian *ver*, set against 안 *an* "not", which matches *ar*. Spanish *ya no* is the equivalent of *aɣar*: *ya no pinta* "she no longer paints".

## Exercises

1. Negate *nino ṗurs č̣ams* with *ar*, then with *ver*, and translate both.
2. Say "I am not a student" and "They are not here".
3. Correct *\*Es ar ḳaṭaa* and *\*Vxedav ar gzas*.
4. Say "There is no telephone here."
5. Say "Giorgi no longer works here."
6. Say "We drink neither tea nor coffee."
7. Translate *Ǯer ar mɣeris* and *Uḳve aɣar mɣeris*. What is the difference?
8. Translate *Kartulad ver vḳitxulob* and explain why it is not *ar*.

## Worked answers

1. *Nino ṗurs ar č̣ams* "Nino does not eat bread" or "is not eating bread". *Nino ṗurs ver č̣ams* "Nino cannot eat bread". The particle goes directly before *č̣ams*; *ṗurs* stays dative.
2. *Sṭudenṭi ar var*: *ar* before *var*. *Isini ak ar arian*: *ar* before *arian*.
3. The short copula cannot be negated: *es ḳaṭa ar aris*. The particle goes before the verb, and the object usually before both: *gzas ar vxedav*.
4. *Ak ṭeleponi ar aris*: the negated copula of existence.
5. *Giorgi ak aɣar mušaobs*. *Aɣar* replaces *ar* and says that he used to.
6. *Arc čais vsvamt da arc q̇avas*. *Arc* before each object; no *ar* on the verb; *v-* ... *-t* for "we". *Čai* and *q̇ava* are vowel stems and add *-s*.
7. "He/she is not singing yet" (but will) and "he/she is no longer singing" (but was). *Ǯer ar* looks forward; *uḳve aɣar* looks back.
8. "I cannot read Georgian." It is about ability, not about whether I am reading now, so *ver*. *Kartulad ar vḳitxulob* would say "I am not reading in Georgian" or "I don't read in Georgian".
