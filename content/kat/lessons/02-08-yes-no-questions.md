## The idea

A **yes-no question** is one that can be answered with "yes" or "no": "Are you a doctor?", "Is Nino here?". Languages mark them in different ways. French can turn the verb round (*est-il médecin?*), English adds "do" or moves "is" to the front, Japanese and Korean add an ending (か, -까). Georgian does none of these. It takes the statement as it is and changes only the voice.

WALS lists Georgian among the languages that mark yes-no questions by "interrogative intonation only" (feature 116A) and have "no question particle" (92A). Bolkvadze and Kiziria make the same point: a question without a question word can have exactly the same words, in the same order, as a statement. In writing, the question mark is the difference. In speech, it is the way the sentence is said.

They place the main stress of such a question on the **last syllable of the verb**, wherever the verb stands in the sentence. Aronson describes a rise in pitch at the end of a yes-no question, often with the last vowel lengthened. Both descriptions point at the same thing: the voice goes up on the verb, which is usually the last word. *Ekimi xar.* "You are a doctor." *Ekimi xar?* "Are you a doctor?"

Georgian also has small particles that add an expectation to a question without being needed to make one. ხომ *xom* asks for agreement, like English "..., aren't you?" or Spanish "*¿verdad?*". Wiktionary notes that it is used when the expected answer is yes. განა *gana* expects a "no" and adds surprise; ნუთუ *nutu* adds doubt or astonishment ("can it really be that ...?"). And თუ *tu* is the "or" of questions: "tea or coffee?".

The answers are short. *Diax* is the polite "yes", ḳi the everyday one, *ho* (also written *xo*) colloquial "yeah"; Bolkvadze and Kiziria say *diax* is required in polite speech. "No" is არა *ara*. Bolkvadze and Kiziria note that in conversation *ara* can stand for a whole negative sentence.

## How it is built

**A plain question.** Build the statement, keep the words and the order, and raise the voice on the verb:

1. Statement: *nino ak aris* "Nino is here".
2. Question: *nino ak aris?* "Is Nino here?", with the stress and rise on *aris*.

The same works with negatives: *ar xar daɣlili?* "Aren't you tired?"

**A question with xom.** Put *xom* at the front, or after the whole statement with a comma:

1. *Šen sṭudenṭi xar* "you are a student".
2. *Xom sṭudenṭi xar?* or *šen sṭudenṭi xar, xom?* "You are a student, aren't you?"

With the negator, *xom ar* asks "you aren't ..., are you?": *xom ar xar daɣlili?* Bolkvadze and Kiziria give the same pattern with "he hasn't left, has he?". When *xom* is last, the stress falls on *xom* itself.

**gana and nutu** go at the front: *gana kartuls sc̣avlob?* "Surely you're not learning Georgian?"; *nutu bavšvebi uḳve c̣eren?* "Can it be that the children are already writing?"

**An either-or question.** Join the two choices with *tu*: *q̇avas svam tu čais?* "Do you drink coffee or tea?" The answer names one: *čais*.

**Answering.**

1. "Yes": *ḳi*, *diax* or *ho*, usually followed by the verb repeated: *diax, ekimi var*. Repeating the verb alone is also a full answer: *ekimi xar? — var.*
2. "No": *ara*, usually followed by the negative statement: *ara, ak ar aris*.
3. Answer in the person the question implies. A polite *tkven* question to one person gets a singular answer: *kartulad laṗaraḳobt? — diax, vlaṗaraḳob.*

## Where it applies

- **Any statement.** Copula sentences (*ekimi xar?*), verbs with or without objects (*q̇avas svam?*), and negatives (*ar xar daɣlili?*).
- **Any word order.** Bolkvadze and Kiziria show the verb at the start, middle or end of a question; the stress follows the verb wherever it is.
- **Checking what you think is true.** *xom* at the start or end: *šen sṭudenṭi xar, xom?*
- **Checking a negative.** *xom ar* "you aren't ..., are you?": *xom ar xar daɣlili?*
- **Disbelief.** *gana* (expecting no) and *nutu* (can it be?). *Martla* "really" also works as a one-word reaction: *martla?* "really?".
- **Choices.** *tu* between the options: *q̇avas tu čais?*
- **Answers.** *ḳi*, *diax*, *ho* "yes"; *ara* "no"; or the verb on its own: *var*, *ar var*. *Albat* "probably" is a common hedge: *albat ak aris* "he's probably here".
- **Courtesy around questions.** *bodiši* "sorry, excuse me" before asking; *gmadlobt* "thank you" (to several people, or politely to one) or *madloba* "thanks" after the answer.
- **Not here.** Questions with "who", "what", "where": lesson 3-11. Reported questions ("he asks whether ..."): lesson 6-3.

## Watch out

**Do not turn the verb round to ask.** French *est-il médecin?* and English "is he a doctor?" move the verb. In Georgian moving it changes the emphasis (lesson 2-9), not the type of sentence. *Ekimi aris?* is already the question.

**Do not add a question particle.** Japanese *isha desu ka* and Korean *의사예요?* or *-까* end in a marker. Georgian has none: *\*ekimi xar ka?*, with Japanese *ka* tacked on, is not Georgian. The voice does the work.

**xom is not a question word.** Wiktionary notes it is not used with question words: *\*xom vin aris?* is wrong. "Who is it?" has no *xom* (lesson 3-11).

**Do not use gana for a neutral question.** *Gana kartuls sc̣avlob?* shows surprise and expects "no". To ask neutrally, say *kartuls sc̣avlob?*

**Do not answer with ar.** As a one-word answer, "no" is *ara*. *Ar* needs a verb after it: *ara, ar var*.

**Do not answer a polite question in the plural.** *Kartulad laṗaraḳobt?* to one person is polite, not plural. The answer is *vlaṗaraḳob*, not *\*vlaṗaraḳobt*, unless you speak for a group.

**Mind the register of "yes".** *Ho* and *xo* are casual. To a stranger, a teacher or an older person, say *diax*. *Ḳi* sits between them. *Ḳi* also means "but" between clauses (lesson 5-6): read it from its place.

**Answering a negative question.** In Japanese, "yes" to "aren't you tired?" agrees with the negative ("yes, I'm not"); in English, "no" does. The sources consulted do not say which pattern Georgian follows. Avoid the question: answer with the full verb, *ara, ar var* or *ḳi, daɣlili var*.

**Do not use tu as the plain "or" of statements.** Bolkvadze and Kiziria say *tu* replaces the ordinary "or" in questions; a statement offering alternatives uses another word, taught with the conjunctions in lesson 5-6. *Tu* does appear in statements in paired phrases such as "man or woman, girl or boy, everyone celebrated", where it means "X and Y alike" (also lesson 5-6).

## Connections

Spanish works like Georgian here: *¿eres médico?* differs from *eres médico* only in the voice and the punctuation. Spanish *¿verdad?* and *¿no?* at the end match *xom*. Korean 네 and 예 versus 응 show the same split as *diax* versus *ho*: a polite "yes" and a casual one.

## Exercises

1. Turn *giorgi mxaṭvaria* into a question, and give a "yes" answer and a "no" answer.
2. Ask a friend "Are you reading the newspaper?", and ask a stranger the same.
3. Ask "You're Georgian, aren't you?" in two ways with *xom*.
4. Ask "Is the shop here or there?"
5. Translate *Nutu nino uḳve aɣar mɣeris?*
6. A learner asks a stranger *Ḳargad laṗaraḳob kartulad?* and answers a polite question with *Vlaṗaraḳobt*. Correct both.
7. Answer *Ar xar sṭudenṭi?* without using the ambiguous one-word "yes" or "no".
8. Explain the difference between *kartuls sc̣avlob?* and *gana kartuls sc̣avlob?*

## Worked answers

1. The words stay; the question mark and the rising voice on the verb make the question: *giorgi mxaṭvaria?* Yes: *ḳi, mxaṭvaria* (or *diax* to be polite). No: *ara, mxaṭvari ar aris*. The negated answer needs the full *aris*.
2. To a friend, second person singular: *gazets ḳitxulob?* To a stranger, polite *tkven* with *-t*: *gazets ḳitxulobt?* Both keep the object in the dative.
3. *Xom kartveli xar?* or *kartveli xar, xom?* In the second, the stress falls on *xom*.
4. *Maɣazia ak aris tu ik?* *Tu* joins the two choices; *aris* is said once.
5. "Can it be that Nino no longer sings?" *Nutu* shows disbelief; *aɣar* says she used to.
6. To a stranger, use polite *tkven* and the plural verb: *ḳargad laṗaraḳobt kartulad?* A polite question to one person is answered in the singular: *vlaṗaraḳob*.
7. *Ḳi, sṭudenṭi var* or *ara, sṭudenṭi ar var*. Saying the whole verb makes the answer clear whatever "yes" and "no" mean after a negative question.
8. Both ask "are you learning Georgian?". The first is neutral. The second, with *gana*, expects "no" and shows surprise.
