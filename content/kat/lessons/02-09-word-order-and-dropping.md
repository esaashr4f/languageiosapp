## The idea

You now have two kinds of marking in every sentence. The nouns carry case: nominative for the subject, dative for the object. The verb carries person: *v-*, *-s*, *-t*, *-en*. Between them, they say who does what to whom. English has neither, so it needs a fixed order, subject before verb before object, to say the same thing. Georgian does not need the order for that, and so it can use the order for something else.

The ordinary order is **subject, object, verb**: *nino c̣erils c̣ers* "Nino is writing a letter". WALS lists Georgian as SOV (feature 81A), with the object before the verb (83A). Bolkvadze and Kiziria say that a sentence most often begins with its subject, but that where the subject goes is up to the speaker, as long as the meaning stays clear. Each order keeps the same structure and the same facts; what changes is the speaker's angle on them and which part is stressed. Their own illustration moves the subject of one sentence through four positions.

The same marking lets Georgian leave words out. A language that can drop a pronoun because the verb already shows the person is called **pro-drop**. Spanish is one (*escribo*, not *yo escribo*); English and French are not. Georgian drops subject pronouns as freely as Spanish: *vc̣er* is "I am writing", and *me vc̣er* says more than that. Bolkvadze and Kiziria note that an omitted subject can be recovered from the verb's person and number. Some verbs have no subject at all: their examples include წვიმს *c̣vims* "it is raining" and თოვს *tovs* "it is snowing". There is no "it" in Georgian to fill the gap.

The object can go too. A third-person object is not marked on the verb (lesson 2-6), but when the listener knows what you mean, it can be left out: *vxedav* "I see it, I see him".

Not everything moves. Some orders are fixed, and those are the ones to learn: the negator directly before the verb (lesson 2-7); numbers and adjectives before the noun (WALS 89A, 87A); the short copula at the end of the predicate word (lesson 2-1). Georgian has no prepositions at all; Bolkvadze and Kiziria say so outright, and its little words of place are attached at the end of the noun (lesson 3-2).

## How it is built

**Start from the neutral order.** Subject, object, verb, with adverbs of time or manner before the verb or at the front:

1. "Giorgi is reading the newspaper today."
2. Subject *giorgi*, object *gazets*, verb *ḳitxulobs*, adverb *dɣes*.
3. Neutral: *giorgi dɣes gazets ḳitxulobs*, or *dɣes giorgi gazets ḳitxulobs*.

**Move a word to change the angle.** Put first what the sentence is about, what you are picking up from before:

- *gazets giorgi ḳitxulobs*: starting from the newspaper ("the newspaper, Giorgi is reading").
- *giorgi ḳitxulobs gazets*: the verb before the object. This is also possible; Bolkvadze and Kiziria's variants include verb-middle and verb-first orders.

The endings do not change, so the meaning of who does what does not change. The sources consulted describe the effect only as a difference of emphasis and attitude, without a strict rule for each position; this guide does not give one either.

**Drop the subject pronoun.** Leave it out unless you need it:

1. Is the person clear from the verb? *Vc̣er* says "I"; drop *me*.
2. Is the third person clear from the context? *C̣ers* "he, she writes" is enough once you know who.
3. Keep the pronoun for contrast or emphasis: *me vc̣er, šen xaṭav* "I write, you draw".

**Drop the object.** Leave it out when it is obvious: *ninos xedav? — vxedav* "Do you see Nino? — I see her."

**Weather.** Use the verb alone: *c̣vims*, *tovs*, *civa* "it is cold", *cxela* "it is hot", *tbila* "it is warm".

## Where it applies

- **Any sentence with a subject and an object.** The cases keep the roles apart, so the three parts can come in any order: *nino c̣erils c̣ers*, *c̣erils nino c̣ers*, *nino c̣ers c̣erils*.
- **Copula sentences.** The predicate and *aris* can swap: *es saxli aris* and *es aris saxli* (lesson 2-1).
- **Questions.** A yes-no question keeps any of these orders; the stress follows the verb (lesson 2-8). Question words usually come first, directly before the verb (lesson 3-11).
- **Dropping "I", "you", "we".** Always possible, because the verb shows the person: *vc̣er*, *c̣er*, *vc̣ert*.
- **Dropping "he", "she", "they".** Possible once the context makes the person clear: *giorgi ak aris? — ak aris.*
- **Dropping the object.** When the context makes it clear: *vxedav*.
- **Weather and nature.** Verbs with no subject: *c̣vims*, *tovs*, *civa*, *cxela*, *tbila*.
- **Keeping pronouns.** For contrast (*me vc̣er, šen xaṭav*), for emphasis, and in answers that pick out one person.
- **Fixed orders.** Negator before verb; number and adjective before noun; the short copula on the last word of the predicate; endings attached to the noun.
- **A stylistic exception.** Bolkvadze and Kiziria note that an adjective may also follow its noun, with full case agreement, as a matter of style. You will meet this in poetry (part 8); do not use it yourself yet.

## Watch out

**Do not read the first noun as the subject.** English habits say the first noun does the action. In *c̣erils nino c̣ers*, the first noun is the object: it has the dative *-s*. Always read the endings.

**Do not add "it" for weather.** *\*Is c̣vims* translates English "it rains" word for word. Georgian, like Spanish *llueve*, has no subject here: *c̣vims*.

**Do not keep every pronoun.** *Me vc̣er c̣erils, me vxedav ninos, me vsvam q̇avas* is correct but sounds insistent, as *yo escribo, yo veo, yo bebo* does in Spanish. Drop *me* unless you are contrasting.

**Do not drop a pronoun the hearer cannot recover.** "He" and "she" are not on the verb; *c̣ers* is only "he, she, it writes". If no one has been mentioned, say who: *giorgi c̣ers*.

**Do not move the negator with the words.** When you reorder, *ar* stays glued to the verb: *c̣erils nino ar c̣ers*, not *\*ar c̣erils nino c̣ers*.

**Do not put the adjective after the noun by default.** *Saxli didi* is not the ordinary phrase "a big house"; with the copula, *saxli didia* is a sentence, "the house is big". The phrase is *didi saxli*. The postposed adjective is stylistic and needs agreement rules you have not yet learnt.

**Do not split the number from its noun.** *\*Ori ak saxlia* for "there are two houses here" breaks the phrase. Keep it together: *ak ori saxlia*.

**Do not expect a fixed meaning for each position.** It is tempting to learn "first word = stressed" or "word before the verb = new information". The sources consulted describe word order in Georgian as a matter of the speaker's emphasis, without such a rule. Use the neutral order unless you have a reason to change it.

## Connections

Japanese and Korean share the verb-final order and the freedom to front the object (手紙をニノが書く), and both drop pronouns even more readily than Georgian, since their verbs show no person. Spanish is the closest match for pro-drop and for weather verbs without a subject (*llueve*, *nieva*), but it keeps the object after the verb where Georgian puts it before.

## Exercises

1. Give three orders for "The boy is drawing a horse", and say what stays the same in all of them.
2. Who sees whom in *ʒaɣls bič̣i xedavs*? Explain how you know.
3. Say "It is raining today, and it is cold."
4. Drop every pronoun that is not needed: *me ṗurs vč̣am da me q̇avas vsvam*.
5. Say "I am reading; you are writing," keeping the contrast.
6. Answer *ʒaɣls xedav?* with "Yes, I see it", as briefly as Georgian allows.
7. Correct *\*Is tovs* and *\*Ar gazets giorgi ḳitxulobs*.
8. Why is *saxli didia* a sentence while *didi saxli* is not?

## Worked answers

1. *Bič̣i cxens xaṭavs* (neutral), *cxens bič̣i xaṭavs*, *bič̣i xaṭavs cxens*. In each, *bič̣i* is nominative, *cxens* dative and *xaṭavs* third person singular, so the boy draws and the horse is drawn.
2. The boy sees the dog. *Bič̣i* is nominative, so it is the subject; *ʒaɣls* has the dative *-s*, so it is the object. Putting the dog first only changes the angle: the sentence starts from the dog.
3. *Dɣes c̣vims da civa*. Neither verb has a subject, so there is no word for "it".
4. *Ṗurs vč̣am da q̇avas vsvam*. Both verbs have *v-*, so *me* adds nothing without a contrast.
5. *Me vḳitxulob, šen c̣er*. The pronouns stay, because the point is the contrast between the two people.
6. *Ḳi, vxedav*. *V-* gives "I"; the dog is clear from the question, so the object goes.
7. Weather verbs have no subject: *tovs*. *Ar* must stand right before the verb: *gazets giorgi ar ḳitxulobs*.
8. In *saxli didia*, *-a* "is" links the subject *saxli* to the predicate *didi*: "the house is big". *Didi saxli* is a phrase, adjective before noun, with no verb: "a big house".
