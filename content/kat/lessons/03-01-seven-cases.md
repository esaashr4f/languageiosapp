## The idea

Part 2 gave a noun two forms. *Saxli* is the nominative: the subject, the thing named. *Saxls* is the dative: the object in the present. A Georgian noun has seven such forms, and this part teaches all of them. A **case** is one of these forms, made by an ending, that tells you what job the noun does in the sentence.

The seven cases are not seven separate rules. They are one set of endings added to one stem. Bolkvadze and Kiziria describe the Georgian noun as agglutinative (lesson 2-4): every case has its own marker, the plural has its own marker *-eb-*, and the pieces follow each other in a fixed order. Stem, then plural, then case. Nothing inside the ending changes when the noun is plural, as it does in Spanish or Latin; you add *-eb-* and then the same case ending you would use in the singular.

The cases fall into two groups, and this is the most useful thing to know about them.

Three cases, the nominative, the **ergative** and the dative, mark the people and things the verb is about: who does it, what it is done to, who feels or receives it. Which of the three a noun takes depends on the verb, not on the noun's meaning. You saw the first sign of this in lesson 2-6: the object of *c̣ers* "writes" is dative in the present, but nominative in the past. The verb's tense group and its class choose the case. This is the organising idea of the whole guide, that the verb is the sentence (lesson 0-2), seen from the noun's side. Part 4 explains it fully; until then, examples stay in the present, with nominative subjects and dative objects.

The other four, the **genitive**, **instrumental**, **adverbial** and **vocative**, have stable meanings of their own, whatever the verb: "of", "with", "as", and calling someone. They are the ones the next lessons teach.

On top of these sit the **postpositions**. A postposition is a word for "in", "on", "for", "from" and so on that comes after its noun instead of before it, as Japanese に or Korean 에 do. In Georgian most of them are glued to the end of a case form: *saxl-ši* "in the house". Each attaches to one particular case. That is why you need the cases first, and why lesson 3-2 starts on them at once.

## How it is built

Find the **stem** first. The stem is the part of the noun that the endings attach to. Bolkvadze and Kiziria give a test: put the noun in the ergative or the dative, where the stem never changes, and take the ending off. *C̣igni* "book" has the ergative *c̣ign-ma*, so the stem is *c̣ign-*, a consonant stem; the *-i* of *c̣igni* is the nominative ending, not part of the stem. *Magida* "table" has *magida-m*, so the stem is *magida-*, a vowel stem.

Then add the case ending. For a consonant stem such as *ḳac-* "man" (Wiktionary):

1. nominative *-i*: *ḳaci*
2. ergative *-ma*: *ḳacma*
3. dative *-s*: *ḳacs*
4. genitive *-is*: *ḳacis*
5. instrumental *-it*: *ḳacit*
6. adverbial *-ad*: *ḳacad*
7. vocative *-o*: *ḳaco*

A vowel stem such as *deda* "mother" takes shorter endings, because a vowel is already there: *deda*, *dedam*, *dedas*, *dedis*, *dedit*, *dedad*, *dedav*. Two things happen. The nominative has no ending (lesson 2-1). And the final *-a* disappears before the vowel of *-is* and *-it*: *ded-is*, not *\*deda-is*. Lesson 3-5 sorts the vowel stems out; lesson 3-6 explains why *megobari* "friend" loses a vowel in *megobris*.

For the plural, put *-eb-* in front of the same endings: *ḳac-eb-i*, *ḳac-eb-ma*, *ḳac-eb-s*, *ḳac-eb-is*, *ḳac-eb-it*, *ḳac-eb-ad*, *ḳac-eb-o*.

Then, if you need one, add a postposition. It goes after the case ending, so the full order of a Georgian noun is: stem, plural, case, postposition. *Saxl-eb-is-tvis* is "for the houses": house, plural, genitive, "for". Bolkvadze and Kiziria note that postpositions attach to every case except the ergative and the vocative.

**Long forms.** Wiktionary prints some endings with a vowel in brackets: dative *ḳacs(a)*, genitive *ḳacis(a)*, instrumental *ḳacit(a)*, adverbial *ḳacad(a)*. The longer forms *-sa*, *-isa*, *-ita*, *-ada* mean the same as the short ones. Hewitt notes that they appear before *da* "and" and when a genitive follows its noun; they also belong to formal and older writing. You need to recognise them; you will rarely need to produce them. They are glossed exactly like the short forms.

### What each case does

- **Nominative** (*-i*): the subject in the present (*ḳaci c̣ers*), the thing named or described (*es saxlia*), and, in the past, the object.
- **Ergative** (*-ma*, *-m*): the subject of most verbs in the aorist, the simple past (lesson 4-12).
- **Dative** (*-s*): the object in the present (*c̣erils c̣ers*), the time when (*dilas*), the person to or for whom (lesson 4-20), the person who feels or has (lesson 4-18), and the base for *-ši*, *-ze*, *-tan* (lesson 3-2).
- **Genitive** (*-is*): "of": the owner, the material, the purpose (lesson 3-3), and the base for *-tvis*, *-gan*, *-ḳen*.
- **Instrumental** (*-it*): "with, by": the tool, the means of transport (lesson 3-4), and the base for *-dan* "from".
- **Adverbial** (*-ad*): "as, into", and the adverbs you already know, *ḳargad* and *kartulad* (lesson 3-4); the base for *-mde* "up to".
- **Vocative** (*-o*, *-v*): calling someone (lesson 3-7).

## Where it applies

- **Every noun.** People, animals, things, places, names: all have the seven cases. *Tbilisi*, *nino* and *sakartvelo* decline like any other noun of the same stem type (lesson 3-5).
- **The plural.** The same seven endings after *-eb-*: *saxleb-ši* "in the houses", *bavšvebo* "children!".
- **Pronouns.** *Is* has its own case forms, some of which you know: *mas* (lesson 2-6). Lesson 3-8 gives them all.
- **Words that describe a noun.** Adjectives, numbers and pointing words before a noun take shortened case endings (lessons 3-9 and 3-10): *did saxls* in lesson 2-6 was one.
- **Adverbs.** Several words you know are case forms that became adverbs: *dɣes*, *dilas*, *saɣamos* are datives; *ḳargad*, *kartulad*, *ertad* are adverbials. Their case endings explain their shapes.
- **Under a postposition.** The case still matters when a postposition is added, because each postposition takes one case: *-ši* and *-ze* the dative, *-tvis* the genitive, *-dan* the instrumental, *-mde* the adverbial.

## Watch out

**The case does not tell you the role by itself.** Spanish and French speakers, and readers of Latin, expect "nominative" to mean "subject". In Georgian it does not. In *masc̣avlebelma c̣erili dac̣era* the nominative *c̣erili* is the object. Read the verb first, then decide what each case means. Part 4 gives the full table.

**The ergative is not a past tense of the noun.** It looks like one, because it appears with past verbs. But it marks the doer, and it appears only with certain verbs: "the guest arrived" keeps its subject in the nominative (lesson 4-14). Do not add *-ma* to every subject in the past.

**The dative is not only "to".** The Latin name means "giving", and Georgian uses it for "to whom" too. But its commonest job is the object in the present: *c̣erils c̣ers*, not "writes to a letter".

**Do not put the postposition before the noun.** *\*Ši saxli* copies English "in the house". The postposition follows the noun and is written as one word with it: *saxlši*.

**Do not stack the plural after the case.** *\*Saxl-is-eb* puts the pieces in the wrong order. Plural first, case second: *saxl-eb-is* "of the houses".

**Do not take the stem from the nominative.** The nominative of a vowel stem has no ending, and the nominative of a consonant stem ends in *-i*, so *ḳaci* and *xe* hide which kind of stem they are. Check the ergative or dative: *ḳac-ma*, but *xe-m*.

**Do not use the long forms as a different case.** *Saxlisa* and *saxlis* are the same genitive. If you see *-sa* or *-isa*, read it as the short form.

## Connections

Korean and Japanese build the same chain, noun then particle, and Korean even shows a similar layering: 학교에서부터 "from school" stacks two particles, as *saxl-is-tvis* stacks case and postposition. The difference is that Georgian particles are fused case endings that change with the stem (*ḳacis*, *dedis*), not separate words. Basque, the other well-known ergative language of Europe, also has an ergative case that marks the doer, but Basque uses it in every tense.

## Exercises

1. Put *saxli* into all seven cases, singular and plural.
2. Find the stem of *c̣q̇ali* "water" from its dative *c̣q̇als*, and of *mta* "mountain" from its ergative *mtam*. Which is a consonant stem?
3. Name the case of each form: *ḳacit*, *dedam*, *saxlebis*, *bavšvebo*, *ʒaɣls*, *mʒɣolad*.
4. Take apart *ḳacebistvis* piece by piece and translate it.
5. Which case would you expect for: the owner of a book; the pen you write with; the role someone works in; the friend you are calling; the object of "reads" in the present?
6. Explain why *c̣erili* is nominative in *masc̣avlebelma c̣erili dac̣era* but dative in *masc̣avlebeli c̣erils c̣ers*.
7. A learner writes *\*Ši sadguri*. Correct it.
8. What do *dilas* and *ḳargad* have in common with *saxls* and *mʒɣolad*?

## Worked answers

1. The stem is *saxl-*, a consonant stem. Singular: *saxli*, *saxlma*, *saxls*, *saxlis*, *saxlit*, *saxlad*, *saxlo*. Plural: insert *-eb-*: *saxlebi*, *saxlebma*, *saxlebs*, *saxlebis*, *saxlebit*, *saxlebad*, *saxlebo*. The endings are the same in both numbers.
2. Remove the dative *-s*: *c̣q̇al-*, a consonant stem (its nominative adds *-i*). Remove the ergative *-m*: *mta-*, a vowel stem. The ergative tells you which: consonant stems take *-ma*, vowel stems *-m*.
3. *ḳacit* instrumental; *dedam* ergative; *saxlebis* genitive plural; *bavšvebo* vocative plural; *ʒaɣls* dative; *mʒɣolad* adverbial.
4. *ḳac-eb-is-tvis*: stem "man", plural *-eb-*, genitive *-is*, postposition *-tvis* "for": "for the men". The order is always stem, plural, case, postposition.
5. Owner: genitive. Tool: instrumental. Role: adverbial (*mʒɣolad mušaobs*). Calling: vocative. Object in the present: dative.
6. The case depends on the verb. *C̣ers* is present, so its object is dative. *Dac̣era* is aorist, a past form, and in the aorist the object of this kind of verb is nominative while the subject becomes ergative (lesson 4-12). The noun has the same meaning in both sentences.
7. The postposition follows the noun and fuses with it: *sadgurši* "in the station". The dative *-s* disappears before *-ši* (lesson 3-2).
8. All four are case forms. *Dilas* and *saxls* are datives in *-s*; *ḳargad* and *mʒɣolad* are adverbials in *-ad*. Some case forms became fixed adverbs, but their endings are ordinary case endings.
