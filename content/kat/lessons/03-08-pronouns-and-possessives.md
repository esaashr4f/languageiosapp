## The idea

Lesson 2-2 gave you the six personal pronouns, and lesson 2-6 the datives *mas* "him, her, it" and *mat* "them". This lesson completes them, and adds the words for "my", "your" and the rest.

Start with a surprise. The pronouns *me* "I", *šen* "you", *čven* "we" and *tkven* "you" have no case endings. Bolkvadze and Kiziria give *me* as the nominative, the ergative and the dative alike: "I", "I (did)" and "me" are all *me*. How does a listener know which it is? From the verb. A Georgian verb marks its subject and its object with prefixes and suffixes (part 4), so the pronoun does not need to. You saw the first sign of this in lesson 2-9: the pronoun can be left out because the verb already says who is meant. The first- and second-person pronouns lean on the verb so completely that they have stopped declining.

The third person is different. *Is* is also a pointing word, "that" (lesson 3-9), and it has a full set of case forms built on a second stem: *man*, *mas*, *mis-*, *mit*. *Isini* has *mat*.

Now the possessives. Bolkvadze and Kiziria explain where they come from: each is a genitive of a pronoun, made into an adjective by adding the nominative *-i*. *Mis* is "of him, of her" (the genitive of *is*); add *-i* and you get *misi* "his, her". *Mat* "of them" gives *mati* "their". *Šen-* and *čven-* give *šeni* "your" and *čveni* "our". The first person is the one irregular member: its genitive has a different stem, *čem-*, so "my" is *čemi*. That is why a possessive behaves like an adjective: it goes before the noun and takes the shortened endings that adjectives take there.

## How it is built

### The personal pronouns

**First and second person.** *Me*, *šen*, *čven* and *tkven* are the same in the nominative, ergative and dative. With a postposition, they use a stem: *čem-* for *me*, and *šen-*, *čven-*, *tkven-* for the others. The same stem serves the dative and the genitive postpositions (Bolkvadze and Kiziria):

- *čemze* "on me, about me", *čemši* "in me", *čemtan* "at my place, with me"
- *čemtvis* "for me", *čemgan* "from me", *čemḳen* "towards me"
- *šentan*, *šentvis*; *čventan*, *čventvis*; *tkventan*, *tkventvis*.

**Third person.** *Is* has the ergative *man*, the dative *mas*, the genitive *mis-* (only before a postposition), the instrumental *mit* and the adverbial *imad* (Wiktionary). *Isini* has *mat* for the ergative, dative and genitive. With postpositions: *mastan*, *masze*, *mistvis*, *misgan*; *mattan* (two *t*'s), *matze*, *mattvis*, *matgan*. Bolkvadze and Kiziria also give *maze* and *maši*, with the *-s* dropped.

### The possessives

There are six, one for each person, plus three special ones:

- *čemi* "my", *šeni* "your" (one person, familiar)
- *misi* "his, her, its"
- *čveni* "our", *tkveni* "your" (several, or polite to one)
- *mati* "their"
- *tavisi* "his own, her own", *tavianti* "their own"; *sxvisi* "someone else's".

**Before a noun.** A possessive declines like an adjective (lesson 3-10), with one difference that Bolkvadze and Kiziria point out: in the dative and adverbial it keeps an *-s*. So:

- nominative *čemi megobari*, ergative *čemma megobarma* (lesson 4-12)
- dative *čems megobars*, and so *čems saxlši*, *čems megobartan*
- genitive *čemi megobris*, instrumental *čemi megobrit*, and so *čemi megobristvis*
- adverbial *čems megobrad*, vocative *čemo megobaro*.

*Misi* has the dative *mis* (Wiktionary): *mis ʒmas*, *mis saxlši*. *Tavisi* has the dative *tavis*: *tavis ʒmastan*.

**Alone, as a predicate.** A possessive can stand for "mine", "yours": *es c̣igni čemia* "this book is mine". It is then an ordinary noun-like word with the copula.

**After the noun, fused.** With close relatives, the possessive is very often written as one word after the noun, and the whole word declines at its end: *dedačemi* "my mother", *mamačemi* "my father", *mamamisi* "his or her father" (Wiktionary). *Dedačems vxedav* "I see my mother"; *mamačemis saxli* "my father's house".

**Choosing the possessive.**

1. "Giorgi lives with his brother": whose brother? If Giorgi's own, use *tavisi*: *giorgi tavis ʒmastan cxovrobs*.
2. If someone else's (a brother of a man mentioned before), use *misi*: *giorgi mis ʒmastan cxovrobs*.
3. Then put the noun in its case (*ʒmas* + *-tan*) and the possessive in the matching form (*tavis*, *mis*).

## Where it applies

**Personal pronouns**

- **As subject, with any verb:** *me vc̣er*, *šen c̣er*, usually only for emphasis (lesson 2-9).
- **As object in the present:** *mas vxedav*, *mat vxedav*. For "me" and "you" as objects the verb changes too (lesson 4-3).
- **With postpositions:** *čemtan*, *šentvis*, *čvenze*, *mastan*, *mattvis*, *misgan*.
- **In the past and perfect:** *me* and *šen* do not change; *is* becomes *man* (lesson 4-12) or *mas* (lesson 4-22).

**Possessives**

- **Before a noun, in every case:** *čemi saxli*, *čems saxlši*, *čemi saxlis*, *čemo megobaro*.
- **As a predicate:** *es čemia*, *es tkvenia* "this is yours".
- **Polite address:** *tkveni* to one person you call *tkven*: *es tkveni c̣ignia, baṭono?*
- **"Own":** *tavisi* when the owner is the third-person subject of the same clause, *tavianti* when that subject is plural: *bavšvebi taviant otaxši arian* "the children are in their own room".
- **"Someone else's":** *sxvisi*.
- **Fused with relatives:** *dedačemi*, *mamačemi*, *mamamisi*; Bolkvadze and Kiziria use such forms freely in their examples.
- **In the vocative:** *čemo* (lesson 3-7): *ɣmerto čemo*, *čemo ḳargo* "my dear".

## Watch out

**Do not use misi for the subject's own thing.** English "his" covers both, and so does Spanish *su*. Georgian does not. *\*Giorgi mis ʒmastan cxovrobs* means Giorgi lives with someone else's brother. For his own, use *tavisi*: *giorgi tavis ʒmastan cxovrobs*. Bolkvadze and Kiziria make the same distinction: *tavisi* is "his or her own", *misi* someone else's.

**Do not use tavisi with "I" or "you".** *Tavisi* is only for a third-person owner. "I live with my brother" is *čems ʒmastan vcxovrob*, not *\*tavis ʒmastan vcxovrob*.

**Do not drop the -s of a possessive before a dative noun.** Adjectives drop their ending there (*did saxls*), so *\*čem megobars* looks consistent. Possessives keep it: *čems megobars*, *čems saxlši*.

**Do not put a postposition on me itself.** *\*Metan* and *\*metvis* add the postposition to the nominative. Use the stem: *čemtan*, *čemtvis*.

**Do not add the genitive -is to a pronoun stem before a postposition.** *\*Čemistvis* and *\*šenistvis* copy *saxlistvis*. The pronoun stems take the postposition directly: *čemtvis*, *šentvis*. (For *is*, the genitive is *mis-*: *mistvis*.)

**Do not decline me in the ergative or dative.** *\*Memtan* and *\*mes* do not exist. *Me* is the same form in all three main cases; the verb shows the role.

**Do not make the possessive plural.** *\*Čemebi megobrebi* copies the noun's plural. Before a noun the possessive stays singular: *čemi megobrebi*.

**Do not confuse šeni and tkveni with the verb's person.** To one person addressed politely, the possessive is *tkveni* and the verb plural, but the noun stays singular: *tkveni ʒma ak aris?* "is your brother here?"

**Do not decline both halves of dedačemi.** *\*Dedas čems* splits the fused word. It declines once, at the end: *dedačems*.

## Connections

Latin had the same "own" distinction in the third person, *suus* against *eius*; Spanish *su* and French *son* lost it and cover both. Korean and Japanese build "my" the way Georgian does, from the pronoun plus a genitive (나의, contracted 내; 私の), though the result is not declined.

## Exercises

1. Say "your (familiar) house", "in your house", "for your house".
2. Say "Nino is at our place" and "This is for them."
3. Say "Giorgi reads his own letters" and "Giorgi reads her letters" (someone else's).
4. Say "My father is a doctor" in two ways.
5. Correct *\*Metan cxovrobs*, *\*Čem megobars vxedav* and *\*Čemistvis*.
6. Translate *es mati saxli ar aris, sxvisia*.
7. Say politely to a woman, "Ms Nino, is this your book?"
8. Why do *me* and *šen* have no ergative or dative forms, when *is* does?

## Worked answers

1. *Šeni saxli*; *šens saxlši*: the possessive keeps *-s* before a dative-based noun; *šeni saxlistvis*: before a genitive noun it is *šeni*.
2. *Nino čventan aris*: *čven-* + *-tan*. *Es mattvis aris*: *mat-* + *-tvis*.
3. *Giorgi tavis c̣erilebs ḳitxulobs*: *tavisi* for the subject's own, dative *tavis* before the dative plural object. *Giorgi mis c̣erilebs ḳitxulobs*: *misi* for someone else's, dative *mis*.
4. *Čemi mama ekimia* and *mamačemi ekimia*. Both are correct; the fused form is very common with relatives.
5. Pronoun stems take postpositions: *čemtan cxovrobs*. A possessive keeps *-s* before a dative noun: *čems megobars vxedav*. A pronoun stem takes *-tvis* directly: *čemtvis*.
6. "This isn't their house; it's someone else's." *Mati* before *saxli*; *sxvisia* is *sxvisi* + the copula.
7. *Kalbaṭono nino, es tkveni c̣ignia?* Polite address uses *tkveni*; the title takes the vocative (lesson 3-7); the question is the statement with a rising tone (lesson 2-8).
8. Because the verb marks the first and second person itself (part 4), so the pronoun does not need to show its role; Bolkvadze and Kiziria say their function is shown by the verb. *Is* is also a pointing word, "that", used with nouns, and it keeps a full set of forms.
