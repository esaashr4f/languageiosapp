## The idea

Lesson 4-3 left two loose ends. "I see you" is *gxedav*, with no *v-*. And nothing yet says how to make "you" plural as an object: *g-* is "you" for one person and for several.

Both come from the same fact: the verb has one slot in front and, at the very end, one *-t*. Four people (I, you, we, you all) compete for them. Georgian settles the competition with fixed rules of precedence. This lesson calls them the **person hierarchy**: the rules that decide which person a marker shows when two persons want the same slot.

There are two contests.

**The front slot.** Only one prefix fits there. When a first- or second-person subject meets a first- or second-person object, Bolkvadze and Kiziria state that the modern verb never marks both: the object gets the slot and the subject's prefix is dropped. *V-* and *g-* never stand together. The sources consulted describe this rule but do not explain its history, so this guide gives none.

**The -t.** There is one plural *-t*, and it is shared. Bolkvadze and Kiziria list its jobs: it makes the first and second person subject plural ("we", "you all"), and it makes the second-person object plural ("you all" as object). The first-person object does not need it, because "us" has its own prefix, *gv-*.

So *-t* means "somebody first or second person is plural". It does not say which. In *gxedavt*, the *-t* could belong to the subject or the object. You work out which from what is left.

## How it is built

**Step 1: read the front slot.** It tells you the object if it is *m-*, *gv-* or *g-*; it tells you the subject is "I/we" if it is *v-*. If it is empty, the subject is "you" or third person and the object is third person.

**Step 2: read the end.** *-s* or *-en* (and the other third-person suffixes) say the subject is third person. No such suffix means the subject is first or second person.

**Step 3: read the -t.** It adds plural to a first- or second-person subject, or to a "you" object, whichever the rest allows.

**Step 4: reason out the gaps.** A verb cannot have "you" as both subject and object (that is "yourself", a different construction), nor "I" as both. So if the object is "you" and the subject is not third person, the subject must be "I" or "we". If the object is "me" or "us", the subject must be "you".

Now take *gxedavt*:

1. Front: *g-*, so the object is "you".
2. End: no *-s*, no *-en*, so the subject is first or second person; with a "you" object it must be first person.
3. *-t*: some first or second person is plural.
4. So *gxedavt* is "we see you", "I see you all" or "we see you all".

Georgian accepts the three-way ambiguity and clears it with pronouns when it matters: *čven gxedavt* "we see you", *me tkven gxedavt* "I see you all".

**A third-person subject with "you all".** Here the two suffixes meet at the end. Bolkvadze and Kiziria's paradigm of *xaṭavs* gives *g-xaṭav-t* for "he paints you (all)": the *-t* appears and the *-s* does not. Their note adds that *-s* "may not appear", with *is tkven gaxarebt* "he makes you happy". So "he sees you all" is *gxedavt*, the same form as "we see you".

**"They" with "you all".** The third-person plural suffix is not given up. Bolkvadze and Kiziria's paradigm of a two-person verb has *g-erč-i-t* "he assails you (all)" but *g-erč-i-an* "they assail you (all)": with *-an* there is no *-t*. So "they see you" and "they see you all" are both *gxedaven*.

**The full grid**, built from these rules for *xedavs* "sees":

- I see you: *gxedav*. I see you all: *gxedavt*.
- We see you, we see you all: *gxedavt*.
- You see me: *mxedav*. You all see me: *mxedavt*.
- You see us, you all see us: *gvxedav*, *gvxedavt*.
- He sees you: *gxedavs*. He sees you all: *gxedavt*.
- They see you, they see you all: *gxedaven*.

**Polite "you".** Plural *tkven* is also the polite form for one person (lesson 2-2). So *mxedavt?* to a stranger is "can you see me?", and *gxedavt* "I see you" said to a stranger is the polite version of *gxedav*.

## Where it applies

- **Every verb that marks two persons.** The same rules hold for every transitive verb in every tense of part 4, and for the indirect objects of lesson 4-20.
- **Verbs of feeling and the perfect.** In *miq̇vars* "I love" and in the perfect, the person who loves or did the action is shown with *m-*, *g-*, *gv-* (lessons 4-18, 4-22). The same hierarchy decides the *-t* there (lesson 4-18).
- **Politeness.** Every *-t* form doubles as the polite singular.
- **Pronouns as tie-breakers.** Whenever *-t* is ambiguous, keep the pronouns: *čven*, *tkven*, *me*.
- **Third-person objects.** They never compete: they have no marker. *Vxedavt* "we see him" is unambiguous.

## Watch out

**Do not put v- and g- together.** By analogy with *v-* in every "I" form, *\*vgxedav* or *\*gvxedav* for "I see you" look logical. The second is a real form, but it means "you see us". "I see you" is *gxedav*.

**Gv- is not v- + g-.** Learners sometimes read *gvxedavs* as "g- + v-": "you ... I". It is one marker, "us": "he sees us".

**Do not double the -t.** "We see you all" has two plural persons but one *-t*: *gxedavt*, never *\*gxedavtt*.

**Do not keep -s before -t.** *\*Gxedavst* or *\*gxedavts* for "he sees you all" try to show both persons. Georgian keeps only *-t*: *gxedavt*.

**Do not add -t after -en.** *\*Gxedavent* for "they see you all" is wrong. *-En* stays alone: *gxedaven*.

**Do not add -t for "us".** *\*Gvxedavst* for "he sees us" adds a plural that *gv-* already carries. Say *gvxedavs*.

**Do not read -t as "they".** It never marks a third-person plural (lesson 2-5). In *gxedavt* the plural is always "we" or "you".

## Connections

Many languages with object marking on the verb have such a hierarchy. Spanish avoids the clash differently: it puts subject and object in different places (*te veo*, object before, subject in the ending), so both are always visible. Georgian puts both in front and lets the object win. Korean and Japanese avoid the issue by leaving persons off the verb and relying on context and politeness levels, which is roughly what Georgian does when *-t* is ambiguous.

## Exercises

1. Say "I see you" and "I see you all".
2. Say "He is looking for you" and "he is looking for you all".
3. Say "They are looking for you all."
4. Give every possible meaning of *gṗaṭižebt*.
5. Say politely to a stranger "Are you looking for me?"
6. Say "You (one friend) are deceiving us."
7. Correct *\*Me šen vgxedav* and *\*Isini tkven gxedavent*.
8. Correct *\*Is čven gvxedavt*.

## Worked answers

1. *Gxedav*; *(me tkven) gxedavt*. The object *g-* takes the slot and *v-* goes; *-t* makes "you" plural.
2. *Geʒebs*; *geʒebt*. With a plural "you", the *-t* replaces the *-s*.
3. *Isini geʒeben.* The third-person plural *-en* stays and no *-t* is added.
4. *G-* "you", no third-person suffix, *-t*: "I invite you all", "we invite you", "we invite you all". Also, said to one person politely, "I invite you" and "we invite you".
5. *Meʒebt?* *M-* "me"; *-t* for polite *tkven*; rising intonation for the question.
6. *(Šen čven) gvaṭq̇ueb.* *Gv-* "us"; "you" singular has no marker.
7. Only the object goes in front: *me šen gxedav*. After *-en* there is no *-t*: *isini tkven gxedaven*.
8. "Us" is fully marked by *gv-*, and the subject is third person singular: *is čven gvxedavs*.
