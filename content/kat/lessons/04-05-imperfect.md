## The idea

Everything so far has been in the present. The first past tense to learn is the one built most directly on it: the **imperfect**, the form for an action seen from inside, while it was going on or as a habit. *Vc̣er* "I write, I am writing" becomes *vc̣erdi* "I was writing, I used to write".

Bolkvadze and Kiziria describe the imperfect as covering two English tenses at once: the past continuous ("I was writing when Mother came in") and the past habit ("I wrote to my parents almost every day"). What joins them is that the action is not presented as finished. You are inside it, or it keeps recurring. That is what "imperfective" means: the action is viewed as unfinished or ongoing.

The other past, for a single completed event ("I wrote the letter"), is the aorist (lesson 4-12). Spanish and French make the same split: *escribía* / *écrivais* against *escribí* / *j'ai écrit*. If you know when to use the Spanish imperfect, you know most of when to use the Georgian one.

Why is it built on the present? Because it belongs with the present. Georgian groups its tenses by stem: the present and the imperfect share the present stem, thematic suffix and all, and they share the case pattern too. The subject of the imperfect is nominative and the object dative, exactly as in the present. Nothing about the nouns changes; only the verb adds a past marker.

## How it is built

**The marker.** Bolkvadze and Kiziria describe the imperfect as the present form extended with *-d-* (for transitive and activity verbs; a variant *-od-* is used by some other verbs). After it come endings:

- *-i* for "I" and "you": *v-c̣er-d-i*, *c̣er-d-i*
- *-a* for "he, she": *c̣er-d-a*
- *-nen* for "they": *c̣er-d-nen*
- and the plural *-t* after *-i*: *v-c̣er-d-i-t*, *c̣er-d-i-t*

This guide glosses the *-d-* with its vowel as one piece, IMPF, with the third person fused in where it is: *v-c̣er-di* `1SBJ-write-IMPF`, *c̣er-da* `write-IMPF.3SG`, *c̣er-dnen* `write-IMPF.3PL`.

**The recipe.**

1. Start from the present stem, with its thematic suffix: *xaṭav-*, *ašeneb-*, *mušaob-*, *xsni-*.
2. Add *-di*, *-da* or *-dnen* by person.
3. Put *v-* in front for "I" and "we", and *-t* at the end for "we" and "you all".

So: *vxaṭavdi* "I was drawing", *ašenebda* "he was building", *mušaobdnen* "they were working", *xsnida* "he was opening".

A chain of thought for "the children were playing in the yard":

1. The verb is *tamašobs*; the present stem is *tamašob-*.
2. It was going on: imperfect.
3. The subject is "the children", plural people: third person plural, *-dnen*.
4. The subject stays nominative, as in the present: *bavšvebi*.
5. *Bavšvebi ezoši tamašobdnen.*

**Verbs in -i that lose it.** A few verbs of the *ṭiris* type replace the *-i* with *-od-*. Wiktionary gives *ṭiris* → *ṭiroda* "was crying", *mɣeris* → *mɣeroda* "was singing", *icinis* → *icinoda* "was laughing", *q̇viris* → *q̇viroda*. Bolkvadze and Kiziria list *icinoda* as their example of this group. Transitive *-i* verbs keep the *-i*: *xsnida*, *zrdida*, *gzavnida*.

**Object markers stay.** The prefixes of lesson 4-3 work unchanged: *mxedavda* "he was looking at me", *gvxaṭavdnen* "they were drawing us". The person hierarchy too: *gxedavdi* "I was watching you". One detail differs from the present: with a "you all" object and a single third-person subject, the *-a* stays and *-t* follows it. Bolkvadze and Kiziria give *g-ǯobn-i-d-a-t* "he was surpassing you (all)". So "he was drawing you all" is *gxaṭavdat*.

**The habit particle.** To make a habit explicit, Georgian adds ხოლმე *xolme* right after the verb. Bolkvadze and Kiziria describe it as marking a repeated action, always after its verb: *vcuravdit xolme* "we used to swim".

## Where it applies

- **An action in progress at a past moment.** *Vc̣erdi*, "I was writing (when ...)". Clauses with "when" come in part 6.
- **A past habit.** *Q̇oveldɣe ṗurs acxobda*; with *xolme* to underline it.
- **A past state that lasted.** *Bavšvobaši sopelši vcxovrobdit*; *mteli dɣe tovda*.
- **Background in a story.** Description and setting go in the imperfect; the events that move the story on go in the aorist (lesson 4-12).
- **Every verb that has a present.** Transitive (*c̣erda*), activity verbs (*mušaobda*), weather verbs (*c̣vimda* "it was raining", lingua.ge), and verbs of happening (lesson 4-10, with *-od-*).
- **The base of the conditional.** Add a preverb to the imperfect and you have "would do" (lesson 4-7).
- **Negation.** *Ar*, *ver* stand before it as before the present: *ar vc̣erdi* "I wasn't writing".

## Watch out

**The subject does not go into the ergative.** Lesson 0-2 mentioned that the subject changes case in the past. That happens in the aorist, not here. The imperfect keeps the present pattern: *\*bavšvma c̣erils c̣erda* is wrong; *bavšvi c̣erils c̣erda* "the child was writing the letter".

**Do not use the imperfect for one completed event.** English "I wrote the letter yesterday" is a single finished act. *\*Gušin c̣erils vc̣erdi* means "yesterday I was writing a letter" (and maybe did not finish). The finished act needs the aorist (lesson 4-12). Spanish speakers make this choice already: *escribía* against *escribí*.

**Do not drop the thematic suffix.** *\*Vxaṭdi* is wrong: the imperfect is built on the present stem, *vxaṭavdi*.

**Do not use -di for "he".** *\*Is c̣erdi* mixes persons. "I" and "you" share *-di*; "he, she" is *-da*.

**Do not use -den or -dan for "they".** The *-en* / *-an* choice of the present (lesson 4-2) does not carry over. Every imperfect "they" is *-dnen* (or *-odnen*): *xaṭavdnen*, *xsnidnen*.

**Do not put xolme before the verb.** *\*Xolme vcuravdit* is wrong; it always follows.

**Do not keep the -i of ṭiris.** *\*Ṭirida* follows the *xsnida* pattern, but the verbs of crying, singing, laughing and shouting take *-od-*: *ṭiroda*, *mɣeroda*, *icinoda*, *q̇viroda*.

## Connections

The Georgian imperfect lines up closely with the Spanish and French imperfects (*escribía*, *écrivais*): ongoing or habitual, against a completed past. The particle *xolme* does the work of English "used to" or of Japanese *-ta mono da* for remembered habits. Korean *-곤 했다* is another close match.

## Exercises

1. Put into the imperfect: *vašeneb*, *xsnis*, *mušaoben*, *vḳitxulobt*.
2. Say "We were looking for the station."
3. Say "Grandfather often used to sing in the evening."
4. Say "Last year I lived in Tbilisi and worked at the university."
5. Say "He was waking me every morning" and "they were seeing us off".
6. Correct *\*Bavšvebi ṭiridnen* and *\*Deda sadils amzadebden*.
7. Correct *\*Ninom c̣erils c̣erda*.
8. Translate *Adre xširad vxaṭavdi xolme, axla ḳi aɣar vxaṭav.*

## Worked answers

1. *Vašenebdi*, *xsnida*, *mušaobdnen*, *vḳitxulobdit*. The present stem stays whole; add *-di*, *-da*, *-dnen*, and keep *v-* and *-t*.
2. *Sadgurs veʒebdit.* First person plural: *v-* ... *-di-t*. The object stays dative.
3. *Babua saɣamos xširad mɣeroda (xolme).* "Sing" belongs to the *-od-* group: *mɣeroda*, not *\*mɣerida*. *Xolme* may be added after the verb to stress the habit.
4. *Šaršan tbilisši vcxovrobdi da universiṭeṭši vmušaobdi.* Two lasting states, both imperfect, first person singular.
5. *Q̇oveldɣe dilas maɣviʒebda.* *M-* "me", the version vowel and stem *aɣviʒeb-*, *-da* "he". "They were seeing us off": *gvacilebdnen*.
6. The crying verb takes *-od-*: *bavšvebi ṭirodnen*. "Was preparing" is singular: *deda sadils amzadebda*. There is no *\*-den*.
7. The imperfect keeps the subject in the nominative: *nino c̣erils c̣erda* "Nino was writing a letter".
8. "I used to draw often (as a habit), but now I no longer draw." *Xolme* marks the habit; *aɣar* "no longer" (lesson 2-7) goes with the present.
