## The idea

You have now met four verb forms: present (*c̣ers*), imperfect (*c̣erda*), future (*dac̣ers*) and conditional (*dac̣erda*). Before going further it is worth seeing the whole map, because the Georgian verb is organised in a way that English and Spanish are not, and the organisation explains most of what is still to come.

So far this guide has said "tense". Georgian forms mix three things: time (past, present, future), aspect (complete or not, lesson 4-6) and mood (stated as fact, wished, ordered). Bolkvadze and Kiziria call each such combination a "tense", and note that many scholars use instead the term **screeve**, an English rendering of Georgian მწკრივი *mc̣ḳrivi* "row", introduced by the Georgian grammarian Akaki Shanidze. This guide uses "screeve" from now on, defined as follows: **a screeve is one set of six person forms of a verb that share a single combination of time, aspect and mood.** The present (*vc̣er*, *c̣er*, *c̣ers*, *vc̣ert*, *c̣ert*, *c̣eren*) is one screeve; the imperfect is another. The Georgian name fits: each screeve is one row of the verb's table.

There are eleven screeves. They fall into three groups called **series**. A series is a group of screeves that are built on related stems and, above all, share one pattern of cases for the subject and object. That second point is why the series matter more than the individual screeves.

In every screeve you have learnt, the subject is nominative and the object dative. That is the pattern of **series I**. In **series II** (the aorist and optative) the subject of a transitive verb goes into a new case, the ergative, and the object into the nominative. In **series III** (the perfect and its relatives) the subject goes into the dative and the object into the nominative. Wiktionary's conjugation tables print this directly: above the series I rows they put the pronouns *me, šen, is* ("nominative subject"), above series II *me, šen, man* ("ergative subject"), above series III *me, šen, mas* ("dative subject").

So the question "which screeve?" always carries a second question with it: "which series, and so which cases?"

## How it is built

**The eleven screeves.** Here they are in Bolkvadze and Kiziria's order, with the Georgian school names (as lingua.ge labels them) and the lesson where each is taught:

Series I, present subseries:
1. present, აწმყო *ac̣mq̇o*: *c̣ers* (lesson 2-5)
2. imperfect, უწყვეტელი *uc̣q̇veṭeli* "unbroken": *c̣erda* (lesson 4-5)
3. present subjunctive, აწმყოს კავშირებითი: *c̣erdes* (lesson 6-7)

Series I, future subseries:
4. future, მყოფადი *mq̇opadi*: *dac̣ers* (lesson 4-6)
5. conditional, ხოლმეობითი *xolmeobiti*: *dac̣erda* (lesson 4-7)
6. future subjunctive, მყოფადის კავშირებითი: *dac̣erdes* (lesson 6-7)

Series II:
7. aorist, წყვეტილი *c̣q̇veṭili* "broken off": *dac̣era* "wrote" (lesson 4-12)
8. optative, მეორე კავშირებითი "second subjunctive": *dac̣eros* "let him write" (lesson 4-16)

Series III:
9. perfect, პირველი თურმეობითი "first *turme* form" (from *turme* "apparently"): *dauc̣eria* "he has (apparently) written" (lesson 4-22)
10. pluperfect, მეორე თურმეობითი "second *turme* form": *daec̣era* "he had written; he should have written" (lesson 4-23)
11. perfect subjunctive, მესამე კავშირებითი "third subjunctive": *daec̣eros* (lesson 4-23)

**How series I is organised.** Look at the first six. Each subseries has a base screeve and two more made from it with *-d-*:

- base: present *c̣ers* / future *dac̣ers*
- base + *-d-* + *-i/-a* (the "was" or "would" endings): imperfect *c̣erda* / conditional *dac̣erda*
- base + *-d-* + *-e* (the subjunctive endings): *c̣erdes* / *dac̣erdes*

And the future subseries is the present subseries with a preverb added. Bolkvadze and Kiziria build all three future-subseries screeves on their present-subseries partners. So once you know the present stem and the preverb, all six series I screeves follow.

**Aspect across the map.** Bolkvadze and Kiziria tie aspect to the preverb: verbs with a preverb are perfective (complete), verbs without one imperfective. The present subseries has no preverb (except for direction): imperfective. The future subseries has one: perfective. Series II and III mostly have one too: *dac̣era* "wrote (it, completely)". An aorist without a preverb exists and is imperfective: *c̣era* "he kept writing, was writing for a while".

**Mood across the map.** Most screeves state facts (indicative). The subjunctives and the optative present an action as wished, possible or required; the optative is also used for commands to "him" or "us" (lessons 4-16, 4-17).

**The changing cases.** The same sentence in three series, with *Nino* writing a poem:

- series I: *nino leks c̣ers*: Nino nominative, poem dative
- series II: *ninom leksi dac̣era*: Nino ergative (*-m*), poem nominative
- series III: *ninos leksi dauc̣eria*: Nino dative, poem nominative

Learning a new screeve is always two jobs: its form, and the case pattern of its series.

## Where it applies

- **Every verb has the eleven slots,** though not every verb fills all of them (some have no imperfect or no future of their own; lesson 4-25).
- **Choosing a screeve** means choosing time, aspect and mood together. "Was writing" is imperfect; "wrote (and finished)" is aorist; "would write" is conditional.
- **Choosing the cases** follows from the series. This is the rule to apply in every sentence from now on.
- **The series also decide other things.** Series II has its own endings (lesson 4-12); series III marks the subject with the object-set prefixes (lesson 4-22).
- **Dictionaries and Wiktionary** print the eleven screeves in this order, with the subject pronouns above each series. Lesson 4-26 shows how to read such a table.

## Watch out

**A screeve is not the same as an English tense.** English "I wrote" can be the imperfect (*vc̣erdi*, "I used to write") or the aorist (*davc̣ere*, "I wrote it"). The screeve depends on aspect as well as time.

**Series I is not "the present tenses".** The conditional, which looks back on an unreal past, and the future both belong to series I. The series is defined by stem and case pattern, not by time.

**Do not carry the case pattern across series.** *\*Nino leksi dac̣era* keeps the series I subject case in series II; *\*ninom lekss dac̣era* keeps the series I object case. In the aorist both change: *ninom leksi dac̣era*.

**"Subjunctive" is three different screeves.** Present subjunctive, future subjunctive and perfect subjunctive belong to different series and are used differently. Do not treat them as one form.

**The Georgian names are not translations of the English ones.** *Xolmeobiti* sounds like "habitual" but is the conditional; *uc̣q̇veṭeli* "uninterrupted" is the imperfect. Use the English names in this guide and recognise the Georgian ones.

## Connections

The Georgian series resemble the Spanish split between the indicative and the subjunctive only loosely; the real parallel for the case shift is in ergative languages such as Basque, where the subject of a transitive verb has its own case. What makes Georgian unusual is that the case pattern depends on the screeve: Hindi-Urdu does something similar, with an ergative subject only in the perfective past.

## Exercises

1. Name the screeve of each: *vxaṭavdi*, *davxaṭav*, *davxaṭavdi*, *vxaṭav*.
2. Which series does each of the four forms in exercise 1 belong to, and what case is the subject in?
3. Which two screeves of series I are built with *-d-* plus *-i/-a*?
4. How do you make the future subseries from the present subseries?
5. In *ninom leksi dac̣era*, which words show that this is series II, apart from the verb?
6. Is *dac̣era* perfective or imperfective? And *c̣era*? Why?
7. Give the Georgian names of the present and the aorist, and say what *uc̣q̇veṭeli* and *c̣q̇veṭili* mean.
8. Correct *\*Nino leksi dac̣era*.

## Worked answers

1. Imperfect; future; conditional; present.
2. All four are series I, so the subject is nominative and the object dative.
3. The imperfect (present stem) and the conditional (future stem). The subjunctives use *-d-* with *-e*.
4. Add the verb's preverb (or, for activity verbs, *i-* ... *-eb*) to each screeve of the present subseries: *c̣ers* → *dac̣ers*, *c̣erda* → *dac̣erda*.
5. *Ninom*, with the ergative *-m*, and *leksi*, with the nominative *-i*. In series I it would be *nino lekss*.
6. *Dac̣era* has a preverb, so it is perfective: he wrote it, completely. *C̣era* has none, so it is imperfective: he was writing, kept writing.
7. *Ac̣mq̇o* (present) and *c̣q̇veṭili* (aorist). Both names in the question are built on the root *c̣q̇veṭ* "break off": *c̣q̇veṭili* is "interrupted, discontinuous" (Wiktionary), and *uc̣q̇veṭeli*, with the negative *u-*, is "uninterrupted" (compare Wiktionary's *uc̣q̇veṭi*). The names contrast a cut-off, complete action (aorist) with an ongoing one (imperfect).
8. In series II the subject is ergative: *ninom leksi dac̣era* (lesson 4-12).
