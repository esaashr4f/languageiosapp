## The idea

Lesson 4-8 showed that the cases of subject and object change from series to series. But not for every verb. In *bavšvi ṭiris* "the child is crying" the subject is nominative, and in the past it becomes ergative: *bavšvma iṭira* "the child cried" (Bolkvadze and Kiziria's example). In *c̣erili ic̣ereba* "the letter is being written" the subject is nominative, and in the past it stays nominative: *c̣erili daic̣era* "the letter was written". Both verbs have only a subject. Yet they behave differently.

The difference is the verb's **class**: the conjugation type it belongs to, which fixes how it forms its screeves and which cases its subject and objects take in each series. Georgian has four. This guide calls them:

1. **transitive (class 1)**: a doer acting on an object: *c̣ers* "writes", *ašenebs* "builds".
2. **unaccusative or "happening" (class 2)**: something happens to the subject, or the subject comes to be in a state: *ic̣ereba* "is being written", *tbeba* "warms up". "Unaccusative" is the linguists' term for a verb whose subject undergoes rather than acts.
3. **medial (class 3)**: a doer doing an activity, usually without an object: *ṭiris* "cries", *mušaobs* "works", *cxovrobs* "lives". Linguists call such verbs **unergative**: their single participant acts.
4. **indirect (class 4)**: verbs of feeling, wanting and having, where the person who feels is in the dative: *uq̇vars* "loves", *akvs* "has" (lesson 4-18).

Bolkvadze and Kiziria number the same four conjugations I to IV and describe them by voice: active (I), dynamic passive (II), medio-active (III), and a fourth group of static and "medio-passive" verbs that includes about forty verbs of feeling (IV). Their fourth group is a little wider than this guide's class 4: it also takes in verbs of position like "is standing". Nothing below depends on the difference.

Why does this matter so much? Because the case rules of lesson 4-8 are really rules for classes. The subject of an intransitive verb, Bolkvadze and Kiziria write, never changes its case; it is always nominative. The one exception is the medio-active (medial) verbs, whose subject changes case like a transitive subject. That exception is what makes the Georgian system unusual. WALS classifies Georgian alignment as "active-inactive": a doer-like subject takes the ergative even without an object, while an undergoer stays nominative.

## How it is built

**The case pattern of each class.** The subject is the noun the verb agrees with in lessons 2-5 and 4-3; the object is what is acted on.

- Class 1: subject nominative (I), ergative (II), dative (III); object dative (I), nominative (II, III).
- Class 2: subject nominative in every series. There is no direct object.
- Class 3: subject nominative (I), ergative (II), dative (III), like class 1. Usually no object.
- Class 4: the person who feels is dative, the thing felt nominative, in every series.

**Telling the classes apart.** Meaning helps but does not decide: there are many lexical exceptions, and the vocabulary notes in this guide give the class of each verb. The forms are the real test:

1. **Does it take a direct object?** If you can say "X does it to Y" and the present has Y in the dative, it is class 1. Its future usually has a preverb: *c̣ers* → *dac̣ers*.
2. **Does the "he, she" present end in -a, with i- or -d- or -eb-a?** *Ic̣er-eb-a*, *tb-eb-a*, and "I" ending in *-i*: *vic̣erebi*. Then it is class 2. Many class 2 verbs are made from class 1 verbs (lesson 4-10).
3. **Is it an activity with no object, whose future has i- and -eb instead of a preverb?** *Tamašobs* → *itamašebs*, *nadirobs* → *inadirebs*. Then it is class 3.
4. **Is the feeler marked with m-, g-, u- and put in the dative?** *Me miq̇vars* "I love (him)". Then it is class 4.

**One root, several classes.** The classes are often related by derivation. Bolkvadze and Kiziria give pairs like *atbobs* "warms (something)" (class 1) and *tbeba* "warms up, gets warm" (class 2), *ašrobs* "dries (something)" and *šreba* "gets dry". From *c̣ers*: *c̣ers* (class 1) and *ic̣ereba* (class 2). The class 1 verb has a doer and an object; the class 2 verb has only the object, now as its subject.

A chain of thought for "the water is warming up" and "Mother is warming the water":

1. In the first, the water does nothing; something happens to it. One participant, an undergoer: class 2, *tbeba*. Subject nominative: *c̣q̇ali tbeba*.
2. In the second, Mother acts on the water: class 1, *atbobs*. Present: subject nominative, object dative: *deda c̣q̇als atbobs*.
3. In the past (lesson 4-12) the class 1 subject turns ergative; the class 2 subject stays nominative.

## Where it applies

- **Every verb belongs to one class,** and the class is part of what you learn with it. The vocabulary notes in this guide say "class 1", "class 3" and so on.
- **Class 1** covers the verbs of parts 2 and 4 with objects: *c̣ers*, *xaṭavs*, *ašenebs*, *xsnis*, *q̇idis* and the rest.
- **Class 2** covers "is being done" (passives), "becomes, gets" (*tbeba* "gets warm"), and many verbs of motion and change (*midis* "goes", lesson 4-11).
- **Class 3** covers most activities without an object: *mušaobs*, *tamašobs*, *cxovrobs*, *ṭiris*, *icinis*, *mɣeris*, *ceḳvavs*, *curavs*, *prinavs*, *nadirobs*, *čkarobs* "hurries". Some take an object in a looser sense: Bolkvadze and Kiziria give *man pexburti itamaša* "he played football" and *man simɣera imɣera* "he sang a song".
- **Class 4** covers feelings, wants, possession, and bodily states: *uq̇vars*, *mosc̣ons*, *unda*, *akvs*, *hq̇avs* (lessons 4-18 to 4-19).
- **Series I hides the differences.** In the present, imperfect, future and conditional, classes 1 to 3 all have a nominative subject. Only in series II and III do they part company.

## Watch out

**Do not classify by English transitivity alone.** "Cry", "work", "live" are intransitive in English, so you might expect them to keep a nominative subject like class 2. They are class 3 and take the ergative in the aorist: *bavšvma iṭira*, not *\*bavšvi iṭira*.

**Do not treat every one-participant verb alike.** "The letter was written" has one participant too, but it is class 2: *c̣erili daic̣era*, not *\*c̣erilma daic̣era*.

**Do not take meaning as a rule.** "Most verbs that describe an activity are class 3" is a guide, not a law; some verbs of activity are class 2 (*midis* "goes", lesson 4-11). Check the class in the notes.

**Do not confuse class 4 with class 1.** *Ninos musiḳa uq̇vars* looks as if *ninos* were an object. It is not: Nino is the one who loves, in the dative because class 4 puts the feeler there (lesson 4-18). *\*Nino musiḳas uq̇vars*, built like a class 1 verb, is wrong.

**Do not expect a class 2 verb to have an object in the dative.** *\*Deda c̣q̇als tbeba* tries to give "warm up" an object. "Warm something" is the class 1 verb: *deda c̣q̇als atbobs*.

**Bolkvadze and Kiziria's numbers are not quite this guide's.** Their IV conjugation includes "stands", "sits", "lies". In this guide those verbs come in lesson 7-7, and class 4 means the verbs with a dative feeler.

## Connections

French draws a similar line inside its intransitive verbs: *il est arrivé* "he has arrived" takes *être*, but *il a travaillé* "he has worked" takes *avoir*. Undergoers get "be", doers get "have". That split is a fair model for the Georgian class 2 / class 3 split: arriving, being born and dying are "happening" verbs in both languages. Spanish, with *ha* in both, has lost the distinction.

## Exercises

1. Give the class of *xedavs*, *ic̣ereba*, *mušaobs*, *tbeba*, *uq̇vars*.
2. Which of these keep a nominative subject in every series: *c̣ers*, *tbeba*, *ṭiris*, *ic̣ereba*?
3. Say "Mother is drying the laundry" and "the laundry is drying" (use *ašrobs*, *šreba*).
4. Why is *bavšvma iṭira* correct although "cry" has no object?
5. Is *ninos musiḳa uq̇vars* a sentence about Nino doing something to music? Explain the cases.
6. Correct *\*Deda c̣q̇als tbeba*.
7. Correct *\*C̣erilma daic̣era*.
8. Sort into "doer" and "undergoer": the hunter hunts; the grass gets dry; the child laughs; the water warms up.

## Worked answers

1. *Xedavs*: class 1 (it has an object). *Ic̣ereba*: class 2. *Mušaobs*: class 3. *Tbeba*: class 2. *Uq̇vars*: class 4.
2. *Tbeba* and *ic̣ereba*, the class 2 verbs. *C̣ers* (class 1) and *ṭiris* (class 3) take the ergative in series II and the dative in series III.
3. *Deda sarecxs ašrobs* (class 1: subject nominative, object dative). *Sarecxi šreba* (class 2: the laundry is the subject, nominative).
4. *Ṭiris* is class 3. Class 3 subjects change case like class 1 subjects, so in the aorist the child is ergative.
5. No. *Uq̇vars* is class 4: the one who loves is in the dative (*ninos*), and the thing loved is in the nominative (*musiḳa*). The literal sense is "music is dear to Nino".
6. Warming something needs the class 1 verb: *deda c̣q̇als atbobs*.
7. *Daic̣era* is class 2, and a class 2 subject stays nominative: *c̣erili daic̣era*.
8. Doers (class 3): the hunter hunts, the child laughs. Undergoers (class 2): the grass gets dry, the water warms up.
