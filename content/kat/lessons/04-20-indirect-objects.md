## The idea

Many actions involve a third participant: you write a letter *to* a friend, ask your mother *for* help, show a child a picture. The person to whom or for whom something is done is the **indirect object**. English marks it with "to" or by position ("show the child a picture"); Spanish with *a* and a clitic (*le escribo a mi amigo*).

Georgian marks it on the verb, and in the noun with the dative. Bolkvadze and Kiziria set out the markers. For the first and second persons they are the same as the direct object markers of lesson 4-3: *m-* "to me", *g-* "to you", *gv-* "to us". The difference is the third person. A third-person direct object has no marker; a third-person indirect object does: *h-*, *s-* or nothing, depending on the sound that follows.

- *h-* before *b, p, ṗ, g, k, ḳ, q̇*: Bolkvadze and Kiziria's *h-ḳitx-a* "he asked him"
- *s-* before *d, t, ṭ, ʒ, c, c̣, ǯ, č, č̣*: *s-c̣er-s* "he writes to him", *s-txov-s* "he asks him for (it)"
- nothing before vowels and other consonants

So *c̣ers* is "he writes (it)", and *sc̣ers* is "he writes (it) to him/her". The *s-* is the only difference. Bolkvadze and Kiziria note that in a sentence like *bič̣i megobars c̣erils sc̣ers* "the boy writes a letter to a friend", the verb shows only two persons (the subject in *-s*, the indirect object in *s-*), although three nouns are involved: the letter, as a third-person direct object, is not marked.

Why is the third-person indirect object marked when the direct object is not? The sources describe the system but do not explain its origin. In practice, the marker is what tells you a verb has a recipient.

The case of the indirect object noun is the dative, in series I and II alike: *megobars sc̣ers* "writes to a friend", *megobars misc̣era* "wrote to a friend" (Bolkvadze and Kiziria). Only in series III does it change, to a phrase with *-tvis* (lesson 7-4).

## How it is built

**The verb.**

1. Take the verb that has a recipient: *sc̣ers* "writes to", *stxovs* "asks (someone) for", *hḳitxavs* "will ask (someone)", *ačvenebs* "shows to", *asc̣avlis* "teaches (someone)".
2. For "to me, you, us", put *m-*, *g-*, *gv-* in the prefix slot: *m-c̣ers* "he writes to me", *g-txovs* "he asks you", *gv-ačvenebs* "he shows us".
3. For "to him, her, them", keep *s-* or *h-* (or nothing, before a vowel: *ačvenebs*).
4. The subject markers are as usual, with one adjustment: Wiktionary's tables show that after *v-* the *s-* or *h-* is not written: *vc̣er* "I write to him", *vtxov* "I ask him", but *sc̣er* "you write to him", *sc̣ers* "he writes to him".

**The nouns.** The indirect object is dative; the direct object is dative in series I and nominative in series II. In the present both are dative: *bič̣i megobars c̣erils sc̣ers*. In the aorist they differ: *bič̣ma megobars c̣erili misc̣era*.

**Word order.** Usually indirect object, then direct object, then verb, but any order is possible; the verb and the cases keep the roles clear.

**The future and aorist** keep the marker, after the preverb: *mi-s-c̣ers* "will write to him", *mi-s-c̣era* "wrote to him" (Wiktionary); *mo-s-txova* "asked him for, demanded" (Wiktionary, lingua.ge; Bolkvadze and Kiziria give the *mo-* form the sense "demand"). With a first- or second-person recipient: *mi-m-c̣era* "he wrote to me", *mo-g-txova* "he asked you".

**Two persons in one slot.** "He writes to me" and "he writes me (a letter)" are both *mc̣ers*: the prefix slot holds the recipient, and the letter, a third person, needs no marker. What if both objects were first or second person ("she introduces me to you")? Bolkvadze and Kiziria show that such verbs need extra machinery; this guide leaves them aside.

**A chain of thought** for "Nino showed the children the photos":

1. "Show" is *ačvenebs*; aorist *ačvena* (Wiktionary; no preverb).
2. Series II: subject ergative *ninom*; direct object nominative *poṭoebi*; indirect object dative *bavšvebs*.
3. The recipient is third person, and the stem begins with a vowel: no marker.
4. *Ninom bavšvebs poṭoebi ačvena.*

## Where it applies

- **Writing, sending, giving, showing to someone:** *sc̣ers*, *ačvenebs*, and "give" (*aʒlevs*, lesson 4-25).
- **Asking:** *stxovs* "asks (someone) for (something)", *hḳitxavs* "will ask (someone a question)".
- **Teaching:** *asc̣avlis* "teaches (someone something)".
- **Every screeve of series I and II**, with the indirect object in the dative. Series III is different (lesson 7-4).
- **With version vowels.** Many verbs introduce their recipient with the vowel *u-* or *i-* instead of, or as well as, *s-*/*h-*: *uc̣ers* "writes for him", *mic̣ers* "writes for me" (Wiktionary's "objective versioner"). That is the next lesson.
- **With class 2 and class 4 verbs.** The dative experiencer of *uq̇vars* and the owner of *hq̇avs* are marked with the same set: the *h-* of *hq̇avs* is this third-person marker (lesson 4-19). Class 2 verbs with an indirect object (*exmareba* "helps") come in lesson 7-3.

## Watch out

**Do not leave the third-person marker out.** *\*Bič̣i megobars c̣erils c̣ers* is understood but wrong: it is "writes" without a recipient on the verb. Say *sc̣ers*.

**Do not put s- or h- after v-.** *\*Vsc̣er* and *\*vstxov* follow the third-person pattern too far. After *v-* the marker is not written: *vc̣er*, *vtxov*.

**Do not mix up s- and h-.** The choice follows the next sound (Bolkvadze and Kiziria): *s-c̣ers*, *s-txovs*, but *h-ḳitxa*. *\*Hc̣ers* and *\*sḳitxa* are wrong.

**Do not use a postposition for "to".** Spanish *a* and English "to" suggest *\*megobartan c̣erils c̣ers* ("writes a letter at the friend's"). *-Tan* means "at, near". The recipient is a plain dative: *megobars sc̣ers*.

**The recipient stays dative in the aorist.** By analogy with the direct object, you might write *\*megobari misc̣era*. Only the direct object becomes nominative: *megobars c̣erili misc̣era*.

**One marker for two objects.** *Mc̣ers* can be "he writes to me" or "he writes it to me". Do not try to add a marker for the letter: *\*mc̣ersr* does not exist.

**Not every English "ask" is stxovs.** *Stxovs* is "ask for, request"; asking a question is *hḳitxavs*. *\*Gza stxova* would be "he asked for a road".

## Connections

Spanish is again close: *le escribo* "I write to him" has a clitic for the third-person recipient, *lo escribo* "I write it" a different one, while *me escribe* serves for both "writes me" and "writes to me". Georgian keeps the same three-way logic: *s-* for "to him", nothing for "it", *m-* for both. Korean and Japanese mark the recipient only on the noun (*-ege*, *ni*).

## Exercises

1. Say "I write to you" and "you write to me".
2. Say "He often writes to his mother."
3. Say "We asked the teacher for advice" (aorist *vtxovet*).
4. Say "Show me the map, please!"
5. Why is it *vc̣er* but *sc̣er* for "I/you write to him"?
6. Correct *\*Nino megobartan c̣erils c̣ers* (meaning "writes a letter to her friend").
7. Correct *\*Ninom megobari c̣erili misc̣era*.
8. Translate *Bebiam bavšvebs ʒveli suratebi ačvena.*

## Worked answers

1. *Gc̣er*: *g-* "to you" takes the slot and *v-* goes. *Mc̣er*: "you" has no marker; *m-* "to me".
2. *Dedas xširad sc̣ers.* Dative recipient; *s-* before *c̣*. (For "his own mother", *tavis dedas*, lesson 3-8.)
3. *Masc̣avlebels rčeva vtxovet.* Aorist: the thing asked for, *rčeva*, is nominative; the teacher, dative; no *s-* after *v-*. Lingua.ge also gives *movtxovet* here; Bolkvadze and Kiziria translate the *mo-* form "we demanded", so the bare form is the safer "asked".
4. *Ruḳa mačvene, gtxov!* Command = aorist second person; *m-* "to me"; *ruḳa* nominative (series II).
5. Wiktionary's tables show the third-person *s-* written only when no *v-* precedes: *sc̣er*, *sc̣ers*, but *vc̣er*.
6. The recipient is a plain dative, and the verb marks it: *nino megobars c̣erils sc̣ers*.
7. Only the direct object becomes nominative in the aorist: *ninom megobars c̣erili misc̣era*.
8. "Grandmother showed the children old pictures." *Bavšvebs* dative recipient; *suratebi* nominative object; *bebiam* ergative subject; no marker for the recipient before the vowel of *ačvena*.
