## The idea

A **conjunction** is a word that joins words or clauses: "and", "or", "but". A **particle** (lesson 2-8) is a small word or suffix that adds a shade of meaning, such as "also", "even", "isn't it?", without being a noun, verb or adverb. You have used several since part 2: *da*, *magram*, *ḳi*, *tu*, *xom*, *arc*. This lesson puts them into a system and adds the rest of the everyday set.

Bolkvadze and Kiziria divide the joining words by what they do. **Correlative** ones add (*da* "and"). **Separating** ones offer a choice (*an* "or", *tu* "or" in questions). **Contrastive** ones oppose (*magram* "but", *ḳi* "while, but", *xolo* "whereas", *aramed* "but rather"). **Equating** ones restate (*anu* "that is"). Knowing the group tells you which one fits.

Two points of logic make Georgian different from English. First, Georgian has two words for English "or": *an* in statements and commands, *tu* in questions. Bolkvadze and Kiziria: *tu* replaces *an* in questions: *čai tu q̇ava?* "tea or coffee?", but *an čai mogviṭane an q̇ava!* "bring us either tea or coffee!". A question offers the choice to the listener; a statement makes it for the world.

Second, several English words are particles glued to a word in Georgian. "Also", "too", "even" are *-c*, attached to whatever it is that is "also": *mec* "me too", *esṗanuric* "Spanish too". "But, as for" is *ḳi*, which stands after the word it contrasts. Because they attach to a word, they point exactly at what is added or contrasted, where English "also" floats.

## How it is built

**da "and"** joins words and clauses. In a list it comes before the last item only (Bolkvadze and Kiziria): *bebia, ṗaṗa, deda da mama*. Before *da*, a dative or genitive noun often takes its long form in *-a* (*mtasa da barši*; lesson 3-3).

**an "or"** in statements; **an ... an** "either ... or". *Anda* "or else" is a stronger *an* (Wiktionary).

**tu "or"** in questions: *čai tu q̇ava?* Bolkvadze and Kiziria also show *tu* for "whether ... or" lists: *tetri tu c̣iteli* "white or red, whichever".

**magram "but"** opens a contrasting clause: *vecade, magram ver ...* "I tried, but could not ...".

**ḳi "while, but, as for"** comes *after* the first word of its clause, the word being contrasted:

1. I want "I am Georgian, and he is German."
2. Two people are contrasted: *me* and *is*.
3. *Ḳi* goes after *is*: *me kartveli var, is ḳi germaneli.*

Bolkvadze and Kiziria: *ḳi* and *xolo* do the same job, but *xolo* stands at the start of its clause and the two are never used together. *Xolo* is literary (Wiktionary).

**ara ... aramed "not ... but rather"**, and *ara marṭo ... aramed ...-c* "not only ... but also".

**anu "that is"** restates or narrows the word before.

**-c "also, too, even"** attaches to the word it adds. After a vowel it is *-c*: *me* → *mec* "me too" (Wiktionary). After a consonant Bolkvadze and Kiziria insert *-a-*: *saidan* → *saidanac*. It goes outside the case ending: *megobrebma* → *megobrebmac* "the friends too (ERG)" (Bolkvadze and Kiziria). For "even", add *ḳi* after it: *-c ḳi* (Bolkvadze and Kiziria list *-c ḳi* among the emphasising particles).

**xom "isn't it?"** (lesson 2-8) either opens a question or tags it: *c̣avida, xom?* "he left, didn't he?" (Bolkvadze and Kiziria).

**Glossing.** *-c* is a clitic, written after `=`: *me=c* `1SG=also`. Conjunctions and *ḳi*, *xom* are glossed by a lower-case word.

## Where it applies

- **da** between any two equal parts: nouns, adjectives, verbs, whole clauses; in lists before the last item.
- **an / an ... an / anda** for alternatives in statements and commands.
- **tu** for alternatives in questions, and in "whether X or Y" lists (*tetri tu c̣iteli*); as "if", lesson 6-8.
- **magram** for "but" between clauses.
- **ḳi** after the contrasted word, for "while, whereas, as for": *is ḳi germaneli*. Also "yes" in answers (lesson 2-8). As *-c ḳi* "even".
- **xolo** at the head of a contrasting clause, in writing.
- **ara ... aramed**, **ara marṭo ... aramed** for corrections and "not only ... but".
- **anu** "that is"; **ese igi** "that is, so, therefore" (Bolkvadze and Kiziria), abbreviated *e.i.*
- **-c** for "also, too": on pronouns (*mec*, *šenc*), nouns in any case (*megobrebmac*), adverbs (*dɣesac*). In pairs: *mec da šenc* "both I and you".
- **arc ... arc**, **verc ... verc** "neither ... nor" (lesson 2-7).
- **xan ... xan** "sometimes ... sometimes".
- **xom** opening or closing a question that expects agreement.
- **aseve** and **agretve** "also, likewise" as independent adverbs, often in writing (Wiktionary).

## Watch out

**Not an in questions.** From English "or", *\*čai an q̇ava?* looks right. In a question Georgian uses *tu*: *čai tu q̇ava?*

**Ḳi does not open its clause.** Translating "and he ...", *\*ḳi is germaneli* puts *ḳi* first, like *magram*. It follows the contrasted word: *is ḳi germaneli*.

**Do not use ḳi and xolo together.** *\*Xolo čven ḳi* doubles the contrast; Bolkvadze and Kiziria say they are never used together.

**-c is not a separate word.** *\*Me c* or *\*me agretve* for "me too" are wrong or stiff. Attach it: *mec*.

**-c goes on the right word.** English "I also saw Nino" is ambiguous; Georgian is not. *Mec vnaxe nino* is "I too saw Nino"; *ninoc vnaxe* is "I saw Nino too".

**-c goes after the case ending.** It is always the last piece of the word: *megobrebmac*, *dedasac*. Never put it before the ending or drop the ending: *\*megobrebc*.

**Do not join two clauses with -c alone when you mean "and".** *-C* adds an item to something already said. For two separate events, use *da*.

**"Neither ... nor" uses arc, not ar.** *\*Ar mšia da ar mc̣q̇uria* is two flat denials; the pair needs *-c*: *arc mšia, arc mc̣q̇uria*.

**Magram is not "but rather".** After a negative, "not X but Y" is *aramed*: *ara ... aramed*. *Magram* means "but nevertheless".

## Connections

Japanese and Korean also attach "also" to the word it adds (Japanese *watashi mo*, Korean *na-do*), exactly like Georgian *mec*. Spanish and French separate "or" in questions only by intonation; Georgian's *tu* versus *an* is closer to the Arabic distinction between *am* (in questions) and *aw* (in statements).

## Exercises

1. Translate: "Do you want tea or coffee?" (*ginda*, lesson 4-18)
2. Translate: "Nino is a doctor, and Giorgi is a teacher." Use *ḳi*.
3. Say "me too" and "you too".
4. Translate: "I know neither Spanish nor French."
5. Find and fix the error: *\*Ḳi čven saxlši davbrundit.*
6. Translate: "Even the teacher doesn't know." (*icis*)
7. Rewrite with *xolo*: *Me c̣erils davc̣er, man ḳi gaagzavnos.*
8. Translate: "He speaks not only Georgian but English too."

## Worked answers

1. A question, so *tu*: *Čai ginda tu q̇ava?* The objects of *unda* are nominative (lesson 4-18).
2. *Ḳi* after the contrasted *giorgi*: *Nino ekimia, giorgi ḳi masc̣avlebeli.* The second copula may be dropped, as in Bolkvadze and Kiziria's example.
3. *-C* on the pronoun: *mec*, *šenc*.
4. *Arc ... arc* with *-c* on the languages: *Arc esṗanuri vici da arc pranguli.* *Vici* "I know" (lesson 4-25).
5. *Ḳi* follows the contrasted word: *Čven ḳi saxlši davbrundit* (lesson 5-6).
6. "Even" is *-c ḳi*. The subject of *icis* is ergative even in the present (Bolkvadze and Kiziria: *q̇velam icis*), so *-c* follows *-ma*: *Masc̣avlebelmac ḳi ar icis.*
7. *Xolo* opens the clause and *ḳi* goes: *Me c̣erils davc̣er, xolo man gaagzavnos* (Bolkvadze and Kiziria).
8. *Ara marṭo ... aramed* + *-c*: *Is ara marṭo kartulad laṗaraḳobs, aramed inglisuradac.*
