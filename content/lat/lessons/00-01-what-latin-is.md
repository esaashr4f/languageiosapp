## A town's language

Latin began as the speech of one small region. Latium is the plain south of the lower Tiber in central Italy, and Rome was one of its towns. In the sixth century BC a traveller crossing Italy would have heard Latin for perhaps a day's walk, then something else.

The something else was often a relative. Latin belongs to the Italic branch of the Indo-European family, the huge family that also includes Greek, Sanskrit, the Germanic languages (English among them), the Celtic and the Slavonic languages. Its closest known relatives were neighbours such as Faliscan, and, a step further off, Oscan and Umbrian. All of those died out as Rome's power spread. Latin took their place and the place of Etruscan, and then of many of the languages of western Europe.

## Two thousand seven hundred years of writing

The oldest Latin we have is a handful of short inscriptions from the seventh and sixth centuries BC. Latin that old was already hard for later Romans. The Greek historian Polybius, writing in the second century BC, says of a treaty he dates to the first year of the Republic that its language could be made out only in part, and only by the best scholars.

Literature starts in the third century BC. The comedies of Plautus (died about 184 BC) are the first long texts that survive whole. Their Latin is called **Early Latin**. It is lively, close to speech, and full of forms that later writers dropped.

**Classical Latin** is the polished written standard of roughly 80 BC to AD 120. Its models in prose are Cicero and Caesar. Its great poets are Catullus, Vergil, Horace and Ovid. Later writers such as Seneca, Pliny and Tacitus stretch the standard but stay inside it. When people say "Latin" without a label, they mean this.

A standard is a choice. Classical authors chose one form where speech had several, fixed the spelling, and settled which constructions counted as correct. The spoken language of the streets kept moving. Over several centuries it became the Romance languages: Italian, Spanish, Portuguese, Catalan, French, Occitan, Romanian and others. Spanish and French are not descended from Cicero's prose. They are descended from everyday speech, most of it the speech of people who never read him.

The written standard did not die with the empire. **Late Latin** (third to sixth century) is the language of Jerome's Bible translation, the Vulgate, and of Augustine. **Medieval Latin** was the working language of the church, the law, the universities and science across Europe for a thousand years. Nobody learnt it as a baby, and everybody educated wrote it. Newton published his physics in Latin in 1687. Linnaeus named the plants and animals in it.

So Latin is "dead" only in a narrow sense: it has had no native speakers for well over a thousand years. It has never stopped being read, and the amount written in it after the Romans is far larger than what the Romans left.

## Which Latin this guide teaches

This guide teaches Classical Latin, with the prose of Cicero and Caesar as the norm. There are three reasons.

First, every grammar and dictionary describes this variety, so every statement here can be checked.

Second, it is the hub. Early Latin, poetry, the Vulgate and medieval documents all differ from it in ways that are easy to list once you know the classical form. The reverse route is much harder.

Third, medieval and later writers were themselves taught from the classical authors. They were aiming at this target, even when they missed.

The other varieties are not ignored. Part 7 has lessons on Early and colloquial Latin, on poetry, on Late and Medieval Latin, and on reading inscriptions and manuscripts. The readings in part 8 run from graffiti on the walls of Pompeii to two clauses of Magna Carta.

## What kind of language it is

Three facts shape everything that follows.

Latin words change their endings. A noun has up to twelve forms, a verb well over a hundred. The ending tells you what the word is doing in the sentence. The next lesson is about this, because it is the idea the whole grammar hangs on.

Latin has no word for "the" or "a". The single word *rēx* means "king", "a king" or "the king". Context decides, and you will be surprised how rarely you miss the articles.

Latin distinguishes long vowels from short ones, and builds its word stress and its poetry on that distinction. The Romans did not usually mark length in writing. Modern textbooks, this one included, mark a long vowel with a bar called a macron: *ā ē ī ō ū*. Part 1 explains why it matters.

## What you already know

If you read English you already own a large Latin vocabulary. English borrowed Latin words directly (*animal*, *exit*, *via*, *data*) and borrowed thousands more through French. If you know Spanish or French you have a second advantage: most of their core words and much of their verb system are Latin, worn down by fifteen centuries of use. This guide points out those links as they come up. They are shortcuts for memory. They are not a method: Latin grammar has to be learnt on its own terms, and it starts on the next page.
