## The route

The guide has nine parts after this one. Read them in order. Each lesson uses only what earlier lessons have taught, so skipping ahead will show you forms nobody has explained yet.

Part 1 covers sounds and spelling. Part 2 gets you to short, complete sentences. Parts 3 and 4 are the core: nouns with their cases, then verbs with their tenses. Parts 5 and 6 build longer sentences. Part 7 covers the special topics, including poetry, inscriptions and medieval Latin. Part 8 is real texts, read line by line. Part 9 is reference: tables, a glossary and the book list.

Each lesson has the same shape. First the idea and the reason for it. Then how the form is built, step by step. Then examples. Then the traps. A table comes only after the pattern has been explained, as a place to look things up. The lesson ends with exercises and worked answers. Do the exercises before reading the answers. Building a sentence yourself is the test of whether you understood it.

## How to read an example

Every example has the same layers. Here is one. It is a preview: its forms are taught in part 2.

:::example
orig: Nautae puellās vident.
morphemes: naut-ae puell-ās vide-nt
gloss: sailor-NOM.PL girl-ACC.PL see-3PL
literal: Sailors girls see.
translation: The sailors see the girls.
note: Preview, to show the layers of an example.
source: constructed
confidence: constructed
preview: NOM, ACC, PL, 3
:::

The first line is the Latin as you would meet it in a book.

The second line cuts each word at the joins. A hyphen separates the stem from the ending.

The third line is the **gloss**. It labels each piece, word under word and piece under piece. Meanings are in lower case: *sailor*, *see*. Grammatical labels are in capitals: NOM, PL. A full stop joins labels that share a single piece: the ending *-ae* means nominative and plural at once, so it is glossed NOM.PL. Tap any label in the app to see what it stands for. Each label is explained in plain words in the lesson that introduces it, and all of them are listed in the glossary in part 9.

A literal translation follows where it helps to show the Latin order. Then comes natural English, and a note saying what the example is there to show.

## Spelling in this guide

Long vowels carry a macron: *ā ē ī ō ū*. The Romans did not write these marks, and most printed editions of Latin authors leave them out. They are here because the length of a vowel can change the meaning of a word and decides where the stress falls. By part 8 you will be reading texts as they are normally printed, without them.

The letter *v* is used for the consonant and *u* for the vowel. The letter *i* does duty as both vowel and consonant, and *j* is not used. Part 1 explains these choices and shows you the other conventions you will meet.

When a real Latin sentence is quoted, its words are those of the edition cited. Its spelling is adjusted to match the rest of the guide.

## Real sentences and made-up ones

Latin has no living native speakers to ask. Everything known about it comes from texts. This guide therefore tells you where each example comes from. In the app, each example and table has a small coloured dot. Tap it to see the label and the source.

- **attested**: a real sentence from a real text, with author, work and passage. Translations are always this guide's own.
- **constructed**: a sentence built for this guide from rules and words that have each been checked. In early lessons most examples are constructed, because real Latin sentences use too much grammar at once. As the guide goes on, attested examples take over.
- **verified**: a form or table checked against a named source.
- **uncertain**: the sources disagree, or the form is a scholarly reconstruction. The lesson says so in words as well.
- **unverified**: written but not yet checked. You should not see this label in a finished guide.

## Where the facts come from

The grammar follows two standard reference works that are free to read online: Allen and Greenough's *New Latin Grammar* (1903) and Bennett's *New Latin Grammar* (1918). Individual word forms and vowel lengths were checked against the Latin entries of Wiktionary, which follow the standard dictionaries. Meanings follow Lewis and Short's *Latin Dictionary* (1879).

Pronunciation follows W. Sidney Allen's *Vox Latina* (1978), the standard account of how classical Latin sounded and of the evidence for it.

The reasons behind the forms, the "why" of each lesson, come from historical grammar, chiefly Michael Weiss's *Outline of the Historical and Comparative Grammar of Latin* (2020). Where historians of the language disagree, or do not know, the lesson says so.

The choice of which words to teach follows the Dickinson College *Latin Core Vocabulary*, a list of the thousand words that occur most often in Latin texts. A reader who knows that list recognises most of the words on any page.

The annotated bibliography in part 9 gives full details and says what each book is good for.

## How much to memorise

Less than a school course would ask. The aim of this guide is that you understand why each form is what it is, so that you can rebuild it. Tables are there to be consulted.

Some things must still become automatic through reading: the endings of nouns and verbs, and the commonest few hundred words. The exercises and the readings are how that happens. Do them, and come back to earlier lessons when something later depends on them.
