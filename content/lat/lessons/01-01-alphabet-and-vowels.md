## The idea

Latin spelling is close to a recording. Each letter stands for one sound, and nothing is silent.

The alphabet of Cicero's day had twenty-three letters: A B C D E F G H I K L M N O P Q R S T V X Y Z. It is the English alphabet without J, U and W. This lesson covers the vowels. The consonants follow in the next lesson, and until then you can read them as in English.

There are five vowel letters: *a e i o u*. (A sixth, *y*, occurs only in words borrowed from Greek and sounds like French *u*.) Each of the five comes in two versions, short and long. A long vowel is the same kind of sound held for about twice the time. This guide marks it with a macron: *ā ē ī ō ū*.

Length is the one thing Latin spelling did not record, and it matters for three reasons.

First, it separates words. *Malum* is "an evil" and *mālum* is "an apple". *Liber* is "book" and *līber* is "free".

Second, it separates forms of the same word. *Rosa* and *rosā* are two different forms of "rose", and *venit* and *vēnit* are "comes" and "came". Part 2 onwards teaches these forms. Without length you could not tell them apart.

Third, length decides where the stress of a word falls, and Latin poetry is built from patterns of long and short. Lessons 3 and 4 of this part show how.

No recordings exist, so how is any of this known? The evidence is of four kinds. Roman grammarians described their own speech. Poetry only scans if each vowel has the right length. Stonecutters sometimes marked a long vowel with a stroke above it. And the Romance languages treat originally long and short vowels differently. The result is called the **restored pronunciation**. It is a reconstruction, and a well-supported one.

## How it is built

To say a Latin vowel, take three decisions in order.

1. Which of the five is it? The basic values are those of Spanish or Japanese *a e i o u*: pure, steady sounds. Do not let them glide, as English does in "day" and "go".
2. Is there a macron? If so, hold the vowel for two beats. If not, one beat. Japanese does the same: *obasan* is "aunt" and *obāsan* is "grandmother".
3. Adjust the quality slightly. A long vowel was probably said with the mouth a little more closed, a short one a little more open. Short *i* is the vowel of English "sit" and long *ī* that of "machine". Short *u* is "put", long *ū* is "rule". Short *e* is "pet", and long *ē* is French *é*, held. Short *o* is British "pot", and long *ō* is French *eau*, held. For *a* only the length changes.

Step 3 is the least certain. The length difference is beyond doubt. The small differences of quality are the usual reconstruction, drawn mainly from what the Romance languages later did.

Sounds are written here in the International Phonetic Alphabet, in square brackets. Each symbol is one sound. The mark [ː] means "long". The mark [ˈ] stands before the stressed syllable.

## Watch out

**Length is not stress.** English stretches stressed vowels and squeezes the others, so English speakers hear "long" and "stressed" as one thing. In Latin they are independent. *Bene* has a stressed short vowel. *Nēmō* has an unstressed long one.

**No weak vowels.** English turns unstressed vowels into a blur, as in the last syllable of "sofa". Latin never does. The final *a* of *lūna* is a clear [a].

**A macron is not optional decoration.** Most printed Latin has none, and you will read without them in part 8. That works only because by then you will know the words. Learn each word with its lengths, as you would learn a Japanese word with its long vowels.

## Connections

Spanish and French lost vowel length, but it left tracks. A stressed Latin short *e* or *o* usually became a double vowel in Spanish, and a long one did not: *bene* gives *bien*, but *sōl* gives *sol*. Japanese keeps a length contrast of the Latin kind, and so does Arabic (*kataba* "he wrote", *kātaba* "he corresponded with").

## Exercises

1. Write the sounds of *māter* and *pater* in brackets and say what differs.
2. Which word has an unstressed long vowel: *lūna*, *nēmō* or *bene*? Use the stress marks in the examples.
3. A book prints "liber" with no macron. Which two words could it be, and how do they differ in sound?
4. An English speaker reads *sine* to rhyme with "fine". Name the two mistakes.
5. The Romans did not write macrons. Give two kinds of evidence that tell us *māter* has a long a.
6. Spanish *bien* comes from Latin *bene*. What does the Spanish *ie* tell you about the first Latin *e*?

## Worked answers

1. [ˈmaːtɛr] and [ˈpatɛr]. The first vowel is long in *māter* and short in *pater*.
2. *Nēmō*. In [ˈneːmoː] the stress mark stands before the first syllable, so the long ō of the second syllable is unstressed. In *lūna* the long vowel is the stressed one, and *bene* has no long vowel.
3. *Liber* [ˈlɪbɛr], "book", or *līber* [ˈliːbɛr], "free". The first has short i as in "sit". The second has long ī as in "machine". Only the context tells you which the book means.
4. Latin *i* is never the glide of English "fine": here it is short [ɪ]. And the final *e* is not silent: it is a full [ɛ], a second syllable. The word is [ˈsɪnɛ].
5. Any two of these. Verse: lines containing *māter* scan only if its first vowel is long. Inscriptions: stonecutters sometimes marked long vowels with a stroke. Grammarians: Romans described which vowels were long. Romance: the daughter languages treat long and short vowels differently.
6. It was short. Stressed short *e* became *ie* in Spanish, and long *ē* stayed *e*. So Spanish confirms what the missing macron on *bene* tells you.
