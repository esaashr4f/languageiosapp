## The idea

A **syllable** is one pulse of a word: a vowel or diphthong, with the consonants that lean on it. *Lūna* has two, *lū* and *na*.

Latin measures its syllables by time. Some take one beat to say and some take two. A one-beat syllable is called **light**. A two-beat syllable is called **heavy**.

This matters because the two big rhythmic systems of Latin count syllable weight. The stress rule of the next lesson asks one question: is a certain syllable heavy? And Latin verse is built from patterns of heavy and light syllables, where English verse uses stressed and unstressed ones.

Weight is not the same thing as vowel length, though length is one source of it. The clock starts at the vowel and runs to the end of the syllable. So there are two ways to fill two beats:

- a long vowel or a diphthong fills them alone;
- a short vowel fills one, and a consonant that closes the syllable fills the other.

A syllable that ends in a short vowel has one beat and is light. Consonants before the vowel never count: the clock has not started.

Japanese counts the same way. The *n* that closes *hon* is a beat, and so is the doubled consonant in *kitte*. A Latin heavy syllable is a two-beat syllable in that sense.

So you need to know where each syllable ends.

## How it is built

This guide shows syllable breaks with a raised dot: *lū·na*.

1. Count the vowels and diphthongs. That is the number of syllables. Two vowels side by side that are not *ae*, *au* or *oe* are two syllables: *de·us*. The first of them is normally short.
2. A single consonant between vowels goes with the vowel after it: *fē·mi·na*.
3. When two consonants stand together, split them. The first stays behind and closes its syllable: *tem·pus*, *mit·tō*. With three, at least the first stays behind.
4. One kind of pair is not split. A **stop** is a consonant that blocks the breath completely: *p t c b d g*. A stop followed by *l* or *r* is said as one tight unit, the kind that can begin a word, so both consonants go to the next syllable: *pa·tri·a*.
5. Count letters by sound. *X* is two consonants, [ks], so it splits: *saxum* is *sak·sum*. *Qu* is one consonant. *Ch*, *ph*, *th* are one each. *H* counts as nothing. Consonant *i* between vowels is double: *maior* is *maj·jor*.
6. Now weigh each syllable. If it has a long vowel or diphthong, it is heavy. If it ends in a consonant, it is heavy. Otherwise it is light.

Older grammars call a heavy syllable "long", and say it is long "by nature" (long vowel) or "by position" (closed by a consonant). This guide keeps "long" for vowels and "heavy" for syllables, so that the two are never confused.

## Watch out

**A heavy syllable does not make its vowel long.** In *tem·pus* the *e* stays short. The weight comes from the *m*. A macron tells you about the vowel only.

**Poets may split a stop from *l* or *r*.** In prose, and so for the stress rule, *patria* is *pa·tri·a* with a light first syllable. In verse a poet may choose *pat·ri·a* and get a heavy one. Part 7 returns to this.

**The last syllable of a word is a special case.** A final consonant closes its syllable only if a consonant or a pause follows. Before a vowel it slides across to the next word. The marks above treat each word as standing alone. The stress rule never looks at the last syllable, so this matters only in verse.

## Connections

Japanese calls these beats morae: *hon* has two, *kitte* three, *obāsan* five. A haiku counts them. Arabic verse, like Latin verse, is built on light and heavy syllables.

## Exercises

1. Divide *corpus* into syllables and mark each heavy or light.
2. Divide *deus* and *aetās*. Why does one have a diphthong and the other not?
3. The *a* of *saxum* is short. Why is its first syllable heavy?
4. Divide *annus* and *anus*. What does the difference do to the weight of the first syllable?
5. Divide *pulcher* and *lingua*. Which letters must you count by sound and not by spelling?
6. *Tenebrae* has the consonants *b* and *r* together. Explain why the syllable before them is light in prose.
7. Divide *imperātor* and say, for each heavy syllable, where its second beat comes from.

## Worked answers

1. *cor·pus*: heavy, heavy. Two consonants stand together, so they split. The *r* closes the first syllable. The *s* closes the second when the word stands alone.
2. *de·us* and *ae·tās*. The pair *ae* is one of the three diphthongs, so it is one syllable. The pair *eu* in *deus* is not, so the vowels belong to separate syllables. *De* is light, and the *e* is short before another vowel. *Ae* and *tās* are both heavy.
3. The letter *x* is two sounds, [ks]. They split: *sak·sum*. The [k] closes the first syllable and gives it its second beat.
4. *an·nus* and *a·nus*. The double *n* splits, so the first syllable of *annus* is closed and heavy. In *anus* the single *n* goes with the next vowel, and the first syllable is light.
5. *pul·cher*: heavy, heavy. The *ch* is one consonant, so the split is between *l* and *ch*. *lin·gua*: heavy, light. The *gu* is one consonant, [ɡʷ], and the *u* is not a vowel, so the word has two syllables.
6. *B* is a stop and *r* follows it. Such a pair goes whole to the next syllable: *te·ne·brae*. Nothing closes *ne*, and its vowel is short, so it is light.
7. *im·pe·rā·tor*. *Im*: the *m* closes it. *Rā*: the long vowel fills both beats. *Tor*: the *r* closes it when the word stands alone. *Pe* ends in a short vowel and is light.
