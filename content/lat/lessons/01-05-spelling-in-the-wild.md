## The idea

**Orthography** means the rules for spelling a language. Latin has had several, and how a word looks depends on who wrote it down, and when.

The Romans of Cicero's day wrote only what we call capitals. Small letters grew out of quick handwriting and came into general use only at the end of the eighth century AD. Word spaces and punctuation came later too.

The Roman alphabet also had one letter where we now have two. **I** wrote both the vowel [i] and the consonant [j]. **V** wrote both the vowel [u] and the consonant [w]. Consonant *i* and *v* are close to the vowels said quickly before another vowel, so one letter each served. *J* and *U* are later shapes of *I* and *V*. Early modern printers used them to tell the two sounds apart.

The Romans did not mark vowel length. Stonecutters sometimes put a stroke called an **apex** over a long vowel, or wrote a long *ī* as a taller *I*. Neither habit became general.

So every modern edition makes choices, and editors differ.

## How it is built

To turn any spelling into this guide's, take four steps.

1. Convert the capitals. Write the word in lower case. Keep a capital only for a proper name, or for the first word of a sentence.
2. Sort out *V*. Before a vowel at the start of a syllable it is the consonant: write *v*. Elsewhere it is the vowel: write *u*. *Qu* and *gu* keep *u*. So VIVVS is *vīvus*. Some editions print *u* for both sounds (*uir*, *uenit*). Treat it the same way.
3. Sort out *I* and *J*. This guide writes *i* for both. If a book prints *j*, as in *Julius* or *jam*, change it to *i*. The rule for which *i* is a consonant is the one from lesson 2.
4. Add the macrons from a dictionary. The page cannot give them, and a guessed macron can give a wrong stress.

This guide's choices, and why: lower case, for ease of reading; *v* and *u* kept apart, because they are different sounds; one *i*, as in Wiktionary, the *Oxford Latin Dictionary* and most current textbooks; macrons on every long vowel, because length is part of the word.

Older books choose differently. Lewis and Short's dictionary prints *j* (*jam*, *Juppiter*). Many scholarly editions, including most Oxford Classical Texts, print *u* for both sounds. Almost all real editions leave out macrons.

Inscriptions and manuscripts add their own habits. At this stage you need four.

- **Abbreviations.** C. is the first name *Gāius* and CN. is *Gnaeus*: the letter C once stood for the sound [ɡ], and the abbreviations kept the old value. COS is *cōnsul*.
- **Older spellings.** In older Latin, *o* was written instead of *u* after *v*, and the habit was not entirely given up until the middle of the first century AD: SERVOS for *servus*, VOLTVS for *vultus*.
- **Changes in later speech.** In the Empire *ae* came to be said as a long [ɛː], so later scribes mixed it up with *e*. *H* stopped being pronounced, so they dropped it or added it in the wrong place.
- **Dots between words.** Many inscriptions separate words with a raised dot, not a space.

## Watch out

**The letter *j* is not a Roman sound.** It is a printer's letter for the [j] of "yes". Do not say *Iūlius* or *iam* with English [dʒ] or French [ʒ].

**Capitals in this guide are not all Roman.** A capital on *Rōma* or *Rōmānus* is a modern convention. Roman texts were all capitals, so none of them showed anything.

**Older spellings are not mistakes.** SERVOS was correct when it was cut.

## Connections

French and Spanish got *j* from the same Latin consonant *i*, after its sound had changed: *iam* became French *jà* (now in *déjà*) and *Iūlius* gave Spanish *Julio*. The Spanish *j* now sounds [x], a further change.

## Exercises

1. Write IVLIVS in this guide's spelling and say how you decided each *I* and *V*.
2. Write VIVVS in this guide's spelling. The vowel *i* is long, as the dictionary shows.
3. Rewrite *Juppiter* (Lewis and Short) and *uenit* (an Oxford text) in this guide's spelling.
4. An inscription reads SERVOS. What does a modern edition print, and why is the stone not wrong?
5. Suetonius (*Divus Iulius* 37.2) says that a placard in Caesar's triumph read VENI : VIDI : VICI, "I came, I saw, I conquered". Write the three words in this guide's spelling. Use the principal parts of *veniō*, *videō* and *vincō*.
6. Divide your answer to exercise 5 into syllables and place the stress in each word.
7. Why does this guide write *iam* and not *jam*?
8. A text reads C. IVLIVS CAESAR COS. Expand and rewrite it in this guide's spelling.

## Worked answers

1. *Iūlius*, three syllables: *Iū·li·us*. The first I is before a vowel at the start of the word: consonant [j]. The first V is before a consonant: vowel *u*. The second I follows a consonant, so it is a vowel. The last V is before *s*: vowel. The macron comes from the dictionary.
2. *Vīvus*. The first and second V each stand before a vowel at the start of a syllable: consonant *v*. The third is before *s*: vowel *u*.
3. *Iuppiter* and *venit*. Change *j* to *i*, and the consonant *u* before a vowel to *v*. Whether the word is *venit* "comes" or *vēnit* "came" only the context tells you.
4. *Servus*. SERVOS was a correct spelling until about the middle of the first century AD. Editions normally use the later one.
5. *Vēnī, vīdī, vīcī*: the third principal part of each verb. Every vowel is long, and the capitals give no hint of it.
6. *VĒ·nī*, *VĪ·dī*, *VĪ·cī*. Two syllables each, so each is stressed on the first.
7. A Roman wrote IAM. The *j* is a later printers' letter. This guide follows Wiktionary and the *Oxford Latin Dictionary* in using one *i*.
8. *Gāius Iūlius Caesar cōnsul*: "Gaius Julius Caesar, consul". C. keeps the old value of C as [ɡ]. COS leaves out the weak *n*.
