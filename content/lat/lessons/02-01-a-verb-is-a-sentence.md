## The idea

In English, "laughs" is not a sentence. You have to say who laughs. In Latin, *rīdet* is a complete sentence: "he laughs", "she laughs" or "it laughs". The verb carries its doer inside it.

It does this with its ending. Latin sorts every doer by two questions.

The first question is **person**: how the doer stands to the speaker. First person is the speaker ("I", "we"). Second person is the one spoken to ("you"). Third person is anyone or anything else ("he", "she", "it", "they").

The second question is **number**: one doer or more than one. One is **singular**, more than one is **plural**.

Three persons times two numbers gives six combinations, and the verb has an ending for each: *-ō*, *-s*, *-t*, *-mus*, *-tis*, *-nt*. Because the ending already names the doer, Latin leaves out words like "I" and "you" unless it wants to stress them (ag1903 §295). Japanese and Korean also leave the doer unsaid, but there the listener works it out from context. In Latin the ending states it.

All the forms in this lesson are in the **present tense**. A **tense** is a set of verb forms that places the action in time. The present covers what English splits three ways: *amō* is "I love", "I am loving" and "I do love" (ag1903 §160).

## How it is built

Verbs fall into a few families that build their forms alike. Each family is called a **conjugation**. This part uses the first two. The first conjugation has a long *ā* before its endings, as in *amāre* "to love". The second has a long *ē*, as in *vidēre* "to see". The third and fourth conjugations come in part 4.

To build a present form, take four steps.

1. Find the stem. Take the dictionary's second form, the infinitive, and remove *-re*: *amā-re* gives *amā-*, *vidē-re* gives *vidē-*.
2. Decide the person and number of the doer, and pick the ending.
3. Join stem and ending.
4. Check the length of the stem vowel. It stays long except in three places, each covered by a general rule of Latin sound (ag1903 §§10, 604):
   - before another vowel it is short: *vide-ō*;
   - before *nt* it is short: *ama-nt*, *vide-nt*;
   - before a single final consonant it is short: *ama-t*, *vide-t*.

The first person singular of the first conjugation needs one more step. The stem vowel and the ending have merged: the form is *amō*, not *amaō*. Allen and Greenough explain that these verb stems were built with an old suffix whose vowels later contracted (ag1903 §174). Wiktionary, following de Vaan's etymological dictionary, gives the earlier form as perhaps *amāō*. In the second conjugation the *ē* only shortened before the *ō* and stayed: *videō*. Why one conjugation contracted and the other did not is not explained here.

The endings are inherited, not Latin inventions. The *-ō* continues the first-person ending that the parent language, Indo-European, used in verbs with a linking vowel before the endings (Wiktionary). Allen and Greenough set *-nt* beside the Doric Greek *-nti* of the same person, and trace *-mus* to an older *-mos* (ag1903 §174). They suggest, as a likelihood only, that the endings were once pronouns (ag1903 §163).

## Where it applies

The person endings belong to every finite verb, that is every verb form that has a person. The present tense has a wider range than the English simple present.

- **Something going on now.** *Rīdent*, "they are laughing" (ag1903 §465).
- **A habit, or something always true.** *Saepe errās*, "you often get it wrong". Allen and Greenough quote Cicero's "the gods do not bother with trifles", in the present (ag1903 §465).
- **Emphasis.** English "I do love" is plain *amō*. Latin has no helping verb for this.
- **Something begun earlier and still going on.** With a phrase meaning "for a long time", Latin uses the present where English says "have been": "I have long been urging you" is a present in Cicero (ag1903 §466). Part 4 returns to this.
- **Something attempted but not achieved.** The present can mean "tries to": Vergil's "he tries to grasp him" is a present (ag1903 §467).
- **The near future, in everyday speech and poetry** (ag1903 §468), as English "we leave tomorrow".
- **Past events told vividly.** Narrators switch to the present to put the reader inside the action (ag1903 §469). You will meet this "historic present" constantly in the readings of part 8.
- **What an author says in a book that survives:** "Epicurus says" is a present in Latin as in English (ag1903 §465).

The endings themselves reach further than the present. The other tenses of part 4 use the same *-s*, *-t*, *-mus*, *-tis*, *-nt*, with a marker between stem and ending. Only the perfect has its own set (ag1903 §163).

## Watch out

**Do not keep the stem vowel in "I".** *\*Amaō* is not a Latin form. The vowel has merged with the ending: *amō*. The second conjugation keeps its vowel short: *videō*, not *\*vidēō*.

**Do not lengthen the vowel before *-t* or *-nt*.** *\*Amāt* and *\*amānt* break the rules of Latin sound: the vowel is short before a single final consonant and before *nt*. Say *amat*, *amant*. Before *-s*, *-mus* and *-tis* it stays long: *amās*, not *\*amas*.

**Do not translate English "am" in "I am working".** *\*Sum labōrō* puts "I am" (lesson 5 of this part) in front of "I work". Latin builds no present from "be": "I am working" is *labōrō*.

**Do not borrow the Spanish endings.** Spanish *amáis* tempts you to write *\*amāis*. The Latin second person plural keeps its *t*: *amātis*. Spanish *ama* has lost a *t* that Latin always pronounces: *amat*, not *\*ama*.

**The dictionary form is "I".** Latin dictionaries list a verb under its first person singular: *amō*, *videō*. When a word list says *amō* "love", the form itself means "I love".

**Third person has no gender.** *Amat* is "he loves", "she loves" or "it loves". Context decides. Latin's words for "he" and "she" come in part 3.

**Length tells forms apart.** The stress rule from part 1 depends on it: *a·MĀ·mus* but *A·mant*.

## Connections

Spanish kept all six endings and, like Latin, rarely needs a pronoun: *amo, amas, ama, amamos, amáis, aman*. French spelling keeps traces of them, but in speech *aime, aimes, aime, aiment* sound alike, and French says *je*, *tu*, *il* every time.

## Exercises

1. Build "we stand" from *stō, stāre*. Show each step.
2. Build "they are afraid" from *timeō, timēre*, and say why the *e* is short.
3. Take apart *properātis*: stem, ending, person, number, meaning.
4. Why is "I love" *amō* and not *amaō*?
5. *Amās* has a long *ā* and *amat* a short *a*. Explain the difference.
6. Place the stress in *amāmus*, *amant*, *videō* and *vidēmus*.
7. Translate *Ibi iacent et rīdent*, then change both verbs to the first person singular.
8. Say "you (one person) are well" and "you (more than one) are well" with *valeō*.

## Worked answers

1. The infinitive *stāre* minus *-re* gives the stem *stā-*. "We" is first person plural, ending *-mus*. Join them: *stāmus*. The vowel is followed by *m*, not by a vowel, *nt* or a single final consonant, so it stays long.
2. The stem is *timē-*. "They" is third person plural, ending *-nt*. A vowel before *nt* is short, so the form is *timent*.
3. *Properā-tis*. The stem *properā-* means "hurry". The ending *-tis* is second person plural. *Properātis*: "you (more than one) are hurrying".
4. The stem vowel *ā* and the ending *-ō* contracted into a single *ō*. Wiktionary gives the older form as perhaps *amāō*.
5. The ending *-s* leaves the vowel long. In *amat* the vowel stands before a single final consonant, *t*, and Latin shortens a vowel there.
6. *a·MĀ·mus*: the penult has a long vowel and is heavy. *A·mant*: two syllables, so the first is stressed. *VI·de·ō*: the penult *de* is light, because its vowel is short before another vowel, so the stress goes back to *vi*. *vi·DĒ·mus*: the penult has a long vowel.
7. "They are lying there and laughing." *Iace-nt* and *rīde-nt* are both third person plural. The first person singular forms are *iaceō* and *rīdeō*: *Ibi iaceō et rīdeō*, "I am lying there and laughing".
8. The stem is *valē-*. Singular: *valēs*. Plural: *valētis*. The vowel stays long before both endings.
