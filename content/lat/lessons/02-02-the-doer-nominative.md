## The idea

*Rīdet* says that one person other than you and me is laughing. It does not say who. To name the doer, Latin adds a noun: *Puella rīdet*, "the girl laughs".

The word that names the doer is the **subject**. The verb already holds a subject in its ending. The noun spells out who that subject is. So the two must fit: a singular noun goes with a singular ending, a plural noun with a plural ending. A noun is always third person, because it names someone other than the speaker and the listener. This fit is called **agreement** in number and person (ag1903 §316).

The noun also needs an ending, to show that it is the doer and not, say, the thing acted on. A form of a noun that shows its job in the sentence is called a **case**. The case of the subject is the **nominative**. It is also the form a dictionary lists.

Nouns, like verbs, fall into families with shared endings. A noun family is called a **declension**. Part 2 uses the first two. Part 3 surveys all five.

- The **first declension** has nouns like *puella* "girl". Its nominative ends in *-a* for one and *-ae* for more than one.
- The **second declension** has nouns like *servus* "slave". Its nominative ends in *-us* for one and *-ī* for more than one.

Where do these endings come from? The first declension's stem ends in a long *ā*, which is shortened in the nominative singular (ag1903 §40). The *-us* of the second declension was earlier *-os*: you met the old spelling SERVOS in the last lesson of part 1 (ag1903 §46). Wiktionary traces it to an Indo-European *-os*, which is also the source of Greek *-ος*. The history of the two plural endings is more tangled and is not covered here.

## How it is built

Say "the sailors are working".

1. Find the verb: *labōrō*, stem *labōrā-*.
2. Find the noun: *nauta* "sailor", first declension.
3. More than one sailor: nominative plural, *nautae*.
4. The subject is plural and third person, so the verb takes *-nt*: *labōrant*.
5. Put them together: *Nautae labōrant*.

Two singular subjects joined by *et* count as more than one, so the verb is plural: *Puer et puella rīdent* (ag1903 §317).

Some second-declension nouns end in *-er* or *-ir* in the nominative singular and add no ending at all: *puer* "boy", *vir* "man", *magister* "teacher" (ag1903 §§45, 47). Their plurals are regular: *puerī*, *virī*. In *magister* the *e* belongs only to the nominative singular, so the plural is *magistrī*. The dictionary's second form shows you which kind you have: *puer, puerī* keeps the *e*, *magister, magistrī* drops it.

## Where it applies

- **The subject of a verb in any person and tense** (ag1903 §339). The nominative noun may stand before the verb or after it: *Puella rīdet*, *Hīc stant sociī*.
- **Several subjects together.** Two or more singular subjects joined by *et* normally take a plural verb (ag1903 §317). When each subject is thought of separately, the verb may agree with the nearest one alone (ag1903 §317).
- **A group named by a singular noun.** *Turba*, "crowd", normally takes a singular verb. Authors sometimes use a plural when they think of the individuals (ag1903 §317).
- **What the subject is said to be.** After "be", the second noun is nominative too: *Nauta sum*, "I am a sailor". Lesson 5 of this part.
- **Exclamations.** A noun called out on its own is nominative (ag1903 §339). Lesson 7 of this part has a real example from the Gospel of John.
- **Naming.** Dictionaries and word lists give nouns in the nominative singular.
- **Calling someone.** The form used to address a person, the vocative, is the same as the nominative for almost every noun. The exception is second-declension nouns in *-us*, which address with *-e*: *serve*, "slave!" (ag1903 §§38, 45). Part 3 covers it.

## Watch out

**Do not mix numbers.** *\*Puella rīdent* pairs a singular noun with a plural ending. Make them match: *Puella rīdet* or *Puellae rīdent*.

**Do not give masculine *-a* nouns the second-declension plural.** A sailor is a man, so *\*nautī* can look right, especially if you think of Spanish *-o* for men. The declension sets the endings, not the sex: *Nautae labōrant*.

**Do not add *-us* to *puer* or keep the *e* of *magister*.** *\*Puerus* and *\*magisterī* are not Latin. The entries *puer, puerī* and *magister, magistrī* tell you: *puer*, *puerī*; *magister*, *magistrī*.

**Do not use the Spanish plural ending for a subject.** Spanish *las niñas* tempts you to write *\*Puellās rīdent*. In Latin *-ās* is the object form, taught in the next lesson. The subject plural is *-ae*: *Puellae rīdent*.

**Do not take the first noun as the subject.** That habit comes from English. In *Gaudet marītus* the subject is last. Read the endings.

**Do not look for "the".** Latin has no article, so *nauta* is "sailor", "a sailor" or "the sailor".

**Vowels before a vowel are short.** *Fīlius* and *fīlia* have a short *i* in the second syllable, before *u* and *a*. So *FĪ·li·us* is stressed on the first syllable.

## Connections

Korean and Japanese mark the subject with a tag after the noun: Korean 이/가, Japanese が. The Latin nominative does the same job, but the tag is fused into the end of the noun and changes with number. Arabic marks the subject of a verb with the ending *-u*, which is also called the nominative.

## Exercises

1. Say "the girl is crying" and "the girls are crying".
2. Say "the son and the husband are working".
3. Take apart *Lēgātī manent* word by word.
4. *Undae* and *ventī* are both plural subjects. Write a sentence with each, using *taceō* and *properō*.
5. A student writes *Puer gaudent*. What is wrong, and what are the two ways to fix it?
6. Why is the plural of *magister* *magistrī* and not *magisterī*?
7. Translate *Umbra iacet, sed nauta semper labōrat*.

## Worked answers

1. *Puella* is nominative singular. The verb *fleō* has the stem *flē-*, shortened before the final *t*: *Puella flet*. For more than one: *Puellae flent*, with the stem vowel short before *nt*.
2. Two singular subjects joined by *et* take a plural verb: *Fīlius et marītus labōrant*.
3. *Lēgāt-ī*: envoy, nominative plural. *Mane-nt*: stay, third person plural. "The envoys are staying." The verb is plural because the subject is.
4. *Undae tacent*: "the waves are silent". *Ventī properant*: "the winds hurry". Both verbs end in *-nt* to agree with a plural subject.
5. *Puer* is singular and *gaudent* is plural, so they do not agree. Either make the verb singular, *Puer gaudet*, or the noun plural, *Puerī gaudent*.
6. The dictionary entry *magister, magistrī* shows that the *e* appears only in the nominative singular. The stem is *magistr-*, and the plural ending *-ī* is added to it.
7. *Umbr-a iace-t*: "the shadow lies still". *Sed*: "but". *Naut-a semper labōra-t*: "the sailor always works". So: "The shadow lies still, but the sailor is always working."
