## The idea

Some actions stop with the doer. *Puella rīdet*: the girl laughs, and that is the whole event. Others reach something else. In "the sailor calls the girl", the calling lands on the girl. The noun that names what an action lands on is the **object**.

Latin marks the object with its own case, the **accusative**. So a sentence with a subject and an object carries two labelled nouns: one in the nominative, one in the accusative. Once both are labelled, their order no longer has to say who did what. That is the freedom you saw in part 0.

The accusative endings are the most regular in the language. In every masculine or feminine noun of every declension, the accusative singular ends in *-m* and the accusative plural ends in *-s* (ag1903 §38). For the first two declensions:

- first declension: *-am* for one, *-ās* for more than one: *puellam*, *puellās*;
- second declension: *-um* for one, *-ōs* for more than one: *servum*, *servōs*.

The *-m* is old. Wiktionary traces *-um* to an earlier *-om*, and Allen and Greenough note that *-om* was still written after *u* and *v* to the end of the Republic (ag1903 §46). The *-am* likewise goes back to an Indo-European ending in *-m*. Why the plural vowels are long is not explained here.

Recall from part 1 that final *m* was weakly said, with the vowel before it nasalised. *Puellam* ended in something like the French *-an* of *Jean*, not a full English *m*.

## How it is built

Say "the wind moves the waves".

1. Find the verb: *moveō*, stem *movē-*.
2. Find the doer: *ventus*, one wind. Nominative singular: *ventus*.
3. Find the object: *unda*, more than one wave. First declension accusative plural: *undās*.
4. The subject is singular, so the verb is third person singular: *movet*. The verb agrees with the subject only, never with the object.
5. Put the words in order. The neutral order of prose is doer, object, verb: *Ventus undās movet*.

Reading runs the other way. Do not start from the first word. Go through the sentence and label each word by its ending.

1. Which word has a person ending? That is the verb, and the ending tells you the number of the subject.
2. Which noun is nominative, and agrees with the verb? That is the subject.
3. Which noun is accusative? That is the object.

If the verb is first or second person, there is no nominative noun: the doer is "I", "you" or "we", and any noun in the sentence is the object. *Servōs laudō* is "I praise the slaves".

Nouns in *-er* add the accusative endings to the stem the plural shows you: *puer, puerī* gives *puerum*; *ager, agrī* "field" gives *agrum*.

## Watch out

**First declension plurals look alike to a beginner.** *Puellae* is nominative and *puellās* accusative. Check the last letter: the accusative ends in *-s*, like every accusative plural in this lesson.

**The verb agrees only with its subject.** In *Fīliae pecūniam cūrant* the money is singular, and the verb is plural anyway. If a plural verb has two nouns beside it, the plural one in the nominative is its subject.

**English verbs and Latin verbs do not always match.** "Wait for", "look at" and "be afraid of" take a preposition in English. *Exspectō*, *spectō* and *timeō* take a plain accusative. Learn each verb with what it takes.

**Not every *-um* is an accusative.** The next lesson introduces nouns whose nominative also ends in *-um*. For now, every *-um* you meet is the accusative of an *-us* or *-er* noun.

## Connections

Japanese marks the object with を and Korean with 을/를, as Latin marks it with *-m*. Arabic gives the object the ending *-a*, its accusative. Spanish lost its noun cases, but it still flags a human object with *a*: *veo a la niña*, "I see the girl".

## Exercises

1. Say "the girl calls the sailor", then put the object first.
2. Say "we are waiting for the envoys".
3. Take apart *Mūrum servī spectant* and translate it.
4. Translate *Puerōs terrent ventī*.
5. Which word is the subject in *Gladiōs habent sociī*, and how do you know?
6. A student translates *Nautās puella iuvat* as "the sailors help the girl". Correct him.
7. Say "I am looking after the table and the money".
8. Give the accusative singular and plural of *ager* and *porta*.

## Worked answers

1. The doer is one girl: *puella*. The object is one sailor: accusative *nautam*. The verb is third person singular: *vocat*. *Puella nautam vocat*. Object first: *Nautam puella vocat*.
2. "We" is in the verb: *exspectāmus*. The envoys are the object, plural: *lēgātōs*. *Lēgātōs exspectāmus*.
3. *Mūr-um*: wall, accusative singular. *Serv-ī*: slaves, nominative plural. *Specta-nt*: look at, third person plural, agreeing with *servī*. "The slaves are looking at the wall."
4. *Puer-ōs* is accusative plural, the object. *Terre-nt* is third person plural. *Vent-ī* is nominative plural, the subject. "The winds frighten the boys."
5. *Sociī*. It is nominative plural, and the verb *habent* is third person plural. *Gladiōs* ends in *-ōs*, so it is accusative: "The allies have swords."
6. *Nautās* is accusative plural, so the sailors are the object. *Puella* is nominative singular and agrees with the singular verb *iuvat*. "The girl helps the sailors."
7. The subject is "I": *cūrō*. Both objects are accusative singular, joined by *et*: *Mēnsam et pecūniam cūrō*.
8. *Ager*: *agrum*, *agrōs*, on the stem *agr-*. *Porta*: *portam*, *portās*.
