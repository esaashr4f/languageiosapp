## The idea

Spanish and French sort every noun into two classes, masculine and feminine. Latin sorts them into three: **masculine**, **feminine** and **neuter**. These classes are called **genders**. The word "neuter" is Latin for "neither": *ne* "not" plus *uter* "either of two" (Wiktionary).

Gender is a property of the noun itself. You cannot hear it in most noun endings. You see it in the words that must match the noun, chiefly adjectives (lesson 7 of this part). It still matters now, for one reason: the neuter has its own nominative and accusative.

Allen and Greenough separate two sources of gender (ag1903 §30).

- **Natural gender**: nouns for male beings are masculine and nouns for female beings are feminine. *Pater* is masculine, *māter* feminine. Latin often makes a pair from one stem: *deus, dea* "god, goddess"; *dominus, domina* "master, mistress"; *fīlius, fīlia*.
- **Grammatical gender**: everything else gets a gender too, with no sex behind it. *Silva* "forest" is feminine and *mūrus* "wall" is masculine.

For the first two declensions, grammatical gender follows the declension and the ending (ag1903 §§42, 48):

- first declension nouns are feminine, except words for men or for roles usually filled by men: *nauta*, *poēta* are masculine;
- second declension nouns in *-us*, *-er* or *-ir* are masculine, with a few feminine exceptions such as *humus* "ground";
- second declension nouns in *-um* are neuter.

## How it is built

The neuter differs from the other two genders in one rule: its nominative and accusative are always the same, and in the plural both end in short *-a* (ag1903 §38). *Bellum* "war" is the subject in "war frightens" and the object in "they prepare war". The plural *bella* is both too.

The likeliest reason is age. Wiktionary traces the *-um* of *bellum* to an Indo-European ending *-om* that was already both the accusative of masculine nouns and the nominative and accusative of neuter ones. So *servum* and *bellum* share the ending because one ancestor served both. Why the parent language left its neuters with no separate subject form is not explained here.

To predict the two cases of a second-declension noun, read its dictionary entry.

1. *Bellum, bellī*, n. The nominative ends in *-um* and the entry says neuter.
2. Nominative and accusative singular: *bellum*.
3. Nominative and accusative plural: drop *-um*, add *-a*: *bella*.

When both nouns in a sentence are neuter, the endings cannot tell you which is the doer. Look first at the verb. If one noun is singular and the other plural, the verb's number picks out the subject. If both have the same number, the meaning and the neutral order, subject first, decide.

In this lesson the gloss adds each noun's gender to its ending label: `sailor-NOM.SG.M`. Later lessons show gender only where an ending reveals it.

## Where it applies

- **People and gods: natural gender.** Men and male gods are masculine, women and goddesses feminine: *deus*, *dea* (ag1903 §30).
- **Roles usually filled by men** are masculine in any declension: *nauta*, *poēta* (ag1903 §30).
- **Everything else, by declension and ending.** First declension feminine; second declension *-us*, *-er*, *-ir* masculine; *-um* neuter (ag1903 §§42, 48).
- **Cities, countries and trees are feminine** whatever their ending (ag1903 §§32, 48). *Rōma* is feminine. So are Greek town names in *-us*, such as *Corinthus*.
- **Words that do not decline are neuter**, and so is a word quoted for its own sake (ag1903 §33). *Nihil* "nothing" is neuter.
- **Nouns with no singular keep a gender too:** *arma* is neuter, *tenebrae* and *kalendae* feminine.
- **Where gender shows.** In the noun itself, only through the neuter's nominative and accusative. In other words, through agreement: adjectives (lesson 7 of this part), then pronouns and participles in parts 3 and 4.

## Watch out

**Do not treat an *-a* noun as feminine by its sound.** Spanish and French train you to hear *-a* as feminine. *Nauta* and *poēta* are masculine. The difference will show when you add an adjective in lesson 7.

**Do not give neuters the masculine plural.** *\*Templī manent* and *\*Bellōs parant* use *servus* endings. The neuter plural is *-a* in both cases: *Templa manent*, *Bella parant*.

**Do not read a neuter plural as a feminine singular.** *\*Dōna manet* takes *dōna* as one thing. It is plural: *Dōna manent*.

**Do not expect Spanish or French gender to match.** *Templum* gives Spanish *templo* and French *temple*, both masculine, because neither language has neuter nouns. Learn the Latin gender with the Latin word. The dictionary entry gives it: f., m. or n.

**Do not assume *-us* means masculine.** *Humus* "ground" is feminine. Its endings are those of *servus*.

***Arma* has no singular.** *Arma, armōrum*, n. "weapons" is used only in the plural. Its nominative and accusative are both *arma*.

## Connections

Greek, Sanskrit and German also have three genders, inherited from the same parent. Arabic has two, like Spanish. Japanese and Korean have none: their nouns need no class, because nothing in the sentence has to match them.

## Exercises

1. Give the gender and the nominative and accusative plural of *signum*, *mūrus* and *silva*.
2. Say "they are preparing weapons".
3. In *Templa auxilium exspectant*, which word is the subject? Explain.
4. Is *nauta* masculine or feminine, and how do you know without a dictionary?
5. Translate *Dea praemia et dōna habet*.
6. Why are *bellum* the subject and *bellum* the object spelled the same?
7. Explain how a reader decides who beats whom in *Ferrum aurum superat*.

## Worked answers

1. *Signum* is neuter: *signa*, *signa*. *Mūrus* is masculine: *mūrī*, *mūrōs*. *Silva* is feminine: *silvae*, *silvās*.
2. "They" is in the verb: *parant*. *Arma* is neuter plural, and its accusative is *arma*: *Arma parant*.
3. *Templa*. The verb *exspectant* is plural. *Auxilium* is singular, so it cannot be the subject. "The temples are waiting for help."
4. Masculine. Sailors were men, and Latin treats words for roles usually filled by men as masculine, whatever their declension.
5. *De-a* is nominative singular feminine and agrees with *habe-t*. *Praemi-a* and *dōn-a* are neuter plural, and the singular verb rules them out as subject. "The goddess has rewards and gifts."
6. They are neuter, and a neuter's nominative and accusative are always the same. Wiktionary traces the ending to an Indo-European *-om* that already did both jobs for neuters.
7. Both nouns are neuter singular, so neither the endings nor the verb decide. The reader falls back on meaning and on the neutral order, which puts the subject first: "Iron beats gold."
