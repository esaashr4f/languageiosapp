## The idea

The verb "be" does two jobs, in Latin as in English.

The first job is to link. *Marītus nauta est*: "the husband is a sailor". Nothing is done to anything. The sentence says that the subject and the second noun are the same person. A noun used this way, saying what the subject is, is called a **predicate noun** (ag1903 §283).

Because no action lands on it, the predicate noun is not an object, and it does not take the accusative. It takes the same case as the subject: the nominative (ag1903 §284). That is why the glossary describes the nominative as the case of the doer and also of what a thing is said to be.

The second job is to state that something exists. *Sunt deī*: "there are gods", or "gods exist". Here *sum* needs nothing after it (ag1903 §284). In this use the verb usually comes first, before its subject (ag1903 §598).

The verb is *sum*, *esse*, *fuī*. It is the commonest verb in Latin, and the most irregular.

## How it is built

Most verbs have a stem and a linking vowel before the ending: *amā-mus*, *vidē-tis*. *Sum* has neither. Its endings go straight onto a short root. Allen and Greenough call such verbs athematic, and note that very few survive in Latin (ag1903 §174).

The root has two shapes, *es-* and *s-*. Allen and Greenough set Latin beside Sanskrit to show the pattern (ag1903 §170). Sanskrit has *as-mi* "I am" and *as-ti* "is" with the full root, but *s-mas* "we are" and *s-anti* "they are" with the short one. Latin keeps the full root in *es*, *est*, *estis*, and the short root in *sumus*, *sunt*.

*Sum* itself has the short root too, where Sanskrit has the full one. Wiktionary notes that early Latin also had *esum*, and that the short forms probably began as unstressed forms leaning on a neighbouring word.

The first person *sum* ends in *-m*, not *-ō*. This is the old ending of the athematic verbs, which Sanskrit *as-mi* shows in full. The *-ō* of *amō* belongs to the other main type (Wiktionary). The *u* in *sum*, *sumus*, *sunt* is not explained here.

Because the root and ending are fused, the gloss does not cut these forms. *Est* is glossed `be.3SG` as one piece.

To build "we are allies":

1. "We" is first person plural: *sumus*.
2. "Allies" says what we are, so it is a predicate noun, nominative plural: *sociī*.
3. The neutral order puts the verb last: *Sociī sumus*.

## Where it applies

- **Linking two nouns**: *Marītus nauta est*.
- **Linking a noun and a description**: "the girl is happy". This needs adjectives, taught in lesson 7 of this part.
- **Stating that something exists**, usually with the verb first: *Sunt deī* (ag1903 §§284, 598).
- **Saying where something is**, with a place word: *Hīc sunt nautae*. Phrases like "in the forest" need a preposition and a case from part 3.
- **Saying who owns something.** Latin often says "there is a book to me" for "I have a book". The case for "to me" is taught in part 3.
- **As a helper in other verb forms.** Some passive tenses are built from a participle and *sum*, as Wiktionary's tables of *amō* show. Part 4.
- **Left out.** Latin sometimes drops *est* altogether, especially in exclamations and short sayings. Lesson 7 of this part has a sentence with no verb at all.

## Watch out

**Do not put the second noun in the accusative.** *\*Marītus nautam est* treats the sailor as acted on. *Sum* is an equals sign: both sides are nominative. *Marītus nauta est*.

**Do not build the present with "be".** English "I am working" tempts you to write *\*Sum labōrō*. Latin uses the plain verb: *Labōrō*.

**Do not leave the verb singular because it comes first.** *\*Est nautae* has a plural subject. The verb agrees wherever it stands: *Sunt nautae*.

**Do not use *stō* for Spanish *estar*.** Spanish splits "be" into *ser* and *estar*. Latin has one verb. *\*Hīc stō* for "I am here" says "I stand here". Say *Hīc sum*.

**Do not use "have" for "there is".** French *il y a* and Spanish *hay* tempt you to write *\*Habet fābulam* for "there is a story". That means "he has a story". Say *Est fābula*.

**Do not make "I am" like *amō*.** *\*Esō* and *\*sō* are not Latin. *Sum* keeps the old ending *-m*.

**Which nominative is the subject?** In *Marītus nauta est*, either noun could in principle be the subject. Usually the subject is the one already known, and it tends to come first. Context settles the rest.

**Short *e*, short stress.** The *e* of *es*, *est*, *estis* is short. *Estis* is *ES·tis* and *sumus* is *SU·mus*: two syllables, so the first is stressed.

## Connections

Spanish *es* and *son*, French *est* and *sont*, continue *est* and *sunt*. Japanese です and Korean 이다 also link two nouns, and like *sum* they come at the end of a neutral sentence. Arabic usually needs no verb at all for "X is Y" in the present.

## Exercises

1. Say "I am a teacher" and "we are teachers".
2. Say "you (one person) are a friend".
3. Take apart *Barbarī sumus* and translate it.
4. Why is the second noun in *Vīta fābula est* not *fābulam*?
5. Translate *Sunt īnsulae* and *Īnsulae sunt*, and say how they differ.
6. Sanskrit has *as-mi* and *s-anti*. Which Latin forms continue each shape of the root?
7. Say "the island is a province".
8. Translate *Somnus quoque morbus est*.

## Worked answers

1. "I" is in the verb: *sum*. *Magister* says what I am, so it is nominative: *Magister sum*. For "we": *Magistrī sumus*, with both words plural.
2. *Amīcus es*. The second person singular is *es*, and *amīcus* is a nominative singular predicate noun.
3. *Barbar-ī*: foreigner, nominative plural, a predicate noun. *Sumus*: be, first person plural. "We are foreigners."
4. *Est* links two names for the same thing. Nothing is acted on, so there is no object, and the predicate noun takes the subject's case, the nominative.
5. *Sunt īnsulae* puts the verb first, so it states existence: "there are islands". *Īnsulae sunt* is more likely "they are islands", with *īnsulae* as predicate noun and the subject understood. Context can override this, but the order points that way.
6. The full root *es-* of *as-mi* survives in *es*, *est* and *estis*. The short root *s-* of *s-anti* survives in *sumus* and *sunt*, and also in *sum*.
7. *Īnsula prōvincia est*. Both nouns are nominative singular, subject first.
8. *Somn-us*: sleep, nominative singular, subject. *Quoque*: also, stressing *somnus*. *Morb-us*: illness, predicate noun. *Est*: is. "Sleep, too, is an illness."
