## The idea

To deny a statement, Latin puts *nōn* "not" in front of it: *Labōrō*, "I am working"; *Nōn labōrō*, "I am not working". There is no helping verb like English "do". **Negation** means turning a statement into its denial.

Where does *nōn* come from? Wiktionary calls its origin uncertain. One proposal derives it from an older *noenum*, "not one".

To ask a question that expects "yes" or "no", Latin does not change the word order (ag1903 §331). It adds a small word. The commonest is *-ne*, which is never said alone: it is attached to the end of another word. A word attached like this is called an **enclitic**. You met one in part 1: *-que* "and". In glosses the join is shown with `=`, and *-ne* is glossed Q, the **question particle**.

*-ne* goes on the word the question is about, the emphatic word (ag1903 §332). That word is often put first. *Rīdēsne?* asks "are you laughing?". *Nautane labōrat?* asks "is it the sailor who is working?".

Two further words let the speaker hint at the answer he expects (ag1903 §332):

- *nōnne* (*nōn* plus *-ne*) expects "yes": "they are glad, aren't they?";
- *num* expects "no": "they aren't fighting, are they?".

There is a curious history here. Wiktionary, following de Vaan, derives *-ne* probably from an Indo-European *né* "not". Allen and Greenough note that *-ne* alone sometimes has the force of *nōnne*, and think this was its original meaning (ag1903 §332). A question once asked "not so?", like English "isn't it?" or French *non?*, and the negative sense faded. *Num* is unrelated: Wiktionary connects it with *nunc* "now".

## How it is built

To deny "the sailor is laughing":

1. Build the statement: *Nauta rīdet*.
2. The denial covers the whole statement, so *nōn* goes before the verb: *Nauta nōn rīdet* (ag1903 §599).

To deny one word only, put *nōn* before that word: *Nōn nauta sed poēta rīdet*, "not the sailor but the poet is laughing". For strong emphasis, *nōn* opens the sentence (ag1903 §599).

To ask "is the sailor laughing?":

1. Decide which word the question is about. If it is the action, that is the verb.
2. Put that word first and attach *-ne*: *Rīdetne nauta?*
3. To expect "yes", begin with *nōnne* instead: *Nōnne nauta rīdet?* To expect "no", begin with *num*: *Num nauta rīdet?*

By the traditional rule from part 1, *-ne* pulls the stress onto the syllable before it: *rī·DĒS·ne*, *rī·DET·ne*.

Latin has no single word for "yes" or "no" (ag1903 §336). To say yes, repeat the verb: *Valetne? Valet.* "Is he well? Yes." To say no, repeat it with *nōn*: *Nōn valet*. Short answers also exist (ag1903 §336). For "yes": *ita* "so", *certē* "certainly", *vērō* "truly", *ita vērō*; Wiktionary adds *sīc* "thus". For "no": *nōn*, *minimē* "not at all".

## Where it applies

- **Denying a whole statement**: *nōn* before the verb (ag1903 §599). This works with *sum* too: *Nōn sum*.
- **Denying one word**: *nōn* before that word, often answered by *sed* "but": *Nōn nauta sed poēta*.
- **Emphatic denial**: *nōn* first in the sentence (ag1903 §599).
- **"And not"**: *neque*, also written *nec*, not *et nōn* (ag1903 §328). "And never" is *nec umquam*, with a word for "ever" taught later.
- **Words with "not" built in**: *numquam* "never", *nēmō* "no one", *nihil* "nothing". Latin uses these instead of *nōn* plus a positive word (ag1903 §328).
- **Two negatives make a positive** (ag1903 §326). *Nōn numquam* is "sometimes". *Nēmō nōn rīdet* is "everyone laughs".
- **"Say that ... not"**: Latin prefers *negō* "deny" (ag1903 §328). Part 6 shows it with a whole clause.
- **Yes-or-no questions**: *-ne* on the word in question (ag1903 §332), often put first.
- **Questions with no particle**: sometimes a question is marked only by the voice, or in writing by context. Allen and Greenough note that such a sentence may be a question or an ironic statement (ag1903 §332).
- **Expecting an answer**: *nōnne* for yes, *num* for no (ag1903 §332).
- **"Whether"**: in reported questions *num* and *-ne* mean "whether" (ag1903 §332). Part 6.
- **Commands**: "don't!" does not use *nōn*. It uses *nē* and a different verb form, taught in part 4.

## Watch out

**Do not put *nōn* after the verb.** French *je ne travaille pas* puts *pas* after the verb, which may tempt *\*Labōrō nōn*. *Nōn* goes before what it denies: *Nōn labōrō*.

**Do not double the negative as Spanish and French do.** Spanish *no veo nada* and French *je ne vois rien* use two negatives for one "no". In Latin two negatives cancel (ag1903 §326). *\*Nōn nihil videō* means "I see something". For "I see nothing" say *Nihil videō*.

**Do not write *et nōn* for "and not".** *\*Sociī sumus et nōn pugnāmus* is not normal Latin. Use *neque*: *Sociī sumus neque pugnāmus*.

**Do not let *-ne* stand alone.** *\*Ne rīdēs?* is not a question. *-ne* must hang on a word: *Rīdēsne?*

**Do not use *num* for "don't they?".** *\*Num puerī gaudent?* expects "no". For "the boys are glad, aren't they?" use *Nōnne puerī gaudent?*

**Do not answer "yes" with *sī*.** Spanish *sí* is "yes", but Latin *sī* means "if". Answer with the verb, or with *ita* or *certē*: *Rīdēsne? Rīdeō.*

**Do not turn a statement into a question by moving words.** *Rīdet nauta* is still a statement (ag1903 §331). Add *-ne*: *Rīdetne nauta?*

## Connections

Japanese ends a question with か and Korean with a question ending such as -까, where Latin adds *-ne*. Arabic opens one with *hal*. Japanese, like Latin, often answers "yes" by repeating the verb.

## Exercises

1. Deny *Puerī rīdent*.
2. Ask "are the allies fighting?" with *-ne* on the verb.
3. Ask the same, expecting "yes", and then expecting "no".
4. Translate *Nōnne magister es?* and give a "yes" answer in Latin.
5. Explain the difference between *Nōn poēta labōrat* and *Poēta nōn labōrat*.
6. Say "the boy is not crying and is not afraid", using *neque*.
7. What does *Nēmō nōn peccat* mean, and why?
8. Correct *\*Ne vidēs puellam?*

## Worked answers

1. The denial covers the whole statement, so *nōn* goes before the verb: *Puerī nōn rīdent*.
2. The question is about the action, so the verb comes first with *-ne*: *Pugnantne sociī?*
3. Expecting yes: *Nōnne sociī pugnant?* Expecting no: *Num sociī pugnant?*
4. "You are a teacher, aren't you?" *Nōnne* expects yes. Latin answers by repeating the verb: *Sum*, "I am". *Ita* or *certē* would also do.
5. In *Nōn poēta labōrat*, *nōn* stands before *poēta* and denies that word: "it is not the poet who is working". In *Poēta nōn labōrat* it stands before the verb and denies the action: "the poet is not working".
6. *Puer nōn flet neque timet*. The first denial is *nōn*. The second, joined by "and", is *neque*.
7. "Everyone does wrong." *Nēmō* "no one" and *nōn* cancel out: "no one does not do wrong".
8. *-ne* cannot stand alone, so attach it to the verb, put first: *Vidēsne puellam?* "Do you see the girl?"
