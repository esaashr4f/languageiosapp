## The idea

In English, order carries the grammar: "the girl sees the sailor" and "the sailor sees the girl" are different events. In Latin the endings carry the grammar, so order is free to do another job. It shows what matters most.

Allen and Greenough put the principle in one sentence: the word most prominent in the speaker's mind comes first, and the rest follow in order of prominence (ag1903 §597). Moving a word forward is the Latin equivalent of stressing it with the voice in English: "it's the *sailor* she sees".

That only works if there is a baseline to move away from. For the formal prose of Cicero and Caesar, the baseline is subject first and verb last, with everything else between (ag1903 §596). Allen and Greenough explain the first half: the subject is what the sentence is about. They describe the verb-last habit as a special Latin tendency, which writers also vary on purpose to avoid monotony (ag1903 §596).

Treat this baseline as a default, not a law. Modern studies of Latin word order, such as Pinkster's *Oxford Latin Syntax* and Devine and Stephens's *Latin Word Order*, find that the figures vary greatly by author and by kind of text. Verse, letters and comedy all depart from Caesar.

## How it is built

To choose an order, start from the neutral one and ask what you want to stress.

1. Write the neutral order: subject, object, verb. *Puella nautam videt*.
2. To stress the object, move it to the front: *Nautam puella videt*, "it's the sailor the girl sees".
3. To stress the action itself, move the verb to the front: *Videt puella nautam*, "the girl does see the sailor" (ag1903 §598).
4. To state that something exists, put *est* or *sunt* first (ag1903 §598): *Est fābula*.

Inside a phrase the same principle applies. The word that decides the meaning comes first (ag1903 §598). In *perīculum magnum* the danger is the point; in *magnum perīculum* its size is. Words of quantity such as *multī* usually come first anyway (ag1903 §598).

Some small words have fixed places, whatever the emphasis (ag1903 §599).

- *Autem* "however", *enim* "for", *vērō* "indeed" and *quoque* "too" are never first. They usually stand second.
- *Itaque* "and so" comes first.
- *Nōn* stands before the word it denies (lesson 6 of this part).
- *-que* "and" hangs on the second of the two things it joins: *puerī puellaeque*, "boys and girls". Wiktionary traces it to an Indo-European *-kʷe*, the source also of Greek *τε*.

To read an author's order, run the steps backwards. Find the neutral order the sentence would have had. Then ask which word has been moved, and where. The move is the author's emphasis.

## Where it applies

- **Neutral formal prose**: subject first, verb last (ag1903 §596). Expect it in Caesar and much of Cicero, less in letters, comedy and verse.
- **Bringing a word forward to stress it or to contrast it** (ag1903 §597): *Nautam puella videt*.
- **The verb first** when the action, or the truth of the whole statement, is the point (ag1903 §598): *Videt puella nautam*, *Sum pius Aenēās*.
- **Existence**: *est*, *sunt* first (ag1903 §598).
- **Inside a phrase**: the deciding word first, so an emphatic adjective goes before its noun (ag1903 §598).
- **Quantity words** such as *multī* before their noun (ag1903 §598).
- **Fixed phrases** whose order never changes: *populus Rōmānus* (ag1903 §598).
- **Persons**: Romans liked to put a name or a pronoun in an emphatic place (ag1903 §598).
- **Parallel and crossed pairs.** Two contrasting pairs may come in the same order, or in mirror order, *a b b a*, called **chiasmus** (ag1903 §598).
- **Splitting a noun from its adjective**, especially in verse, so that one pair wraps round another (ag1903 §598): Martial's line.
- **Questions**: the word carrying *-ne* comes first (lesson 6 of this part).
- **Words with fixed places**: *autem*, *enim*, *vērō*, *quoque* second; *itaque* first; *nōn* before what it denies; *-que* on the second item (ag1903 §599).

## Watch out

**Do not put *autem* or *enim* first.** English "however" can open a sentence. *\*Autem nauta labōrat* cannot. Put it second: *Nauta autem labōrat*.

**Do not attach *-que* to the first item.** *\*Puerīque puellae rīdent* is wrong for "boys and girls". It goes on the second: *Puerī puellaeque rīdent*, or use *et*: *Puerī et puellae rīdent*.

**Do not read emphasis into the last word.** English often stresses the end of a sentence. Latin does not put the important word last for emphasis (ag1903 §597). Its emphasis is at the front.

**Do not pair an adjective with the nearest noun.** In Martial's line *nostrōs* stands next to *mea*, but it cannot go with it: *nostrōs* is accusative plural masculine and *mea* nominative singular feminine. Match by endings: *nostrōs libellōs*, *mea Rōma*.

**Do not take the first noun as the subject.** That English habit turns *Nautam puella videt* into "the sailor sees the girl". The *-am* says the sailor is seen.

**Do not "correct" a verb-first sentence to verb-last.** *Videt puella nautam* is not careless. It has a different emphasis from *Puella nautam videt*, and a translation should show it.

## Connections

Japanese and Korean share the neutral verb-last order of Latin prose and also mark roles with tags, so they too can move an object to the front for emphasis. Spanish keeps more freedom than English: *a la niña la ve el marinero* fronts the object as Latin *Puellam nauta videt* does.

## Exercises

1. Rewrite *Servus dominum timet* three ways: neutral, object stressed, verb stressed. Translate each.
2. Put *enim* into *Nautae labōrant* correctly.
3. Join *fīlius* and *fīlia* with *-que* as the subject of *gaudent*.
4. What does the order of *Multōs libellōs habet poēta* stress?
5. In *Magnum perīculum timēmus*, why does the adjective come first? Give the neutral alternative.
6. In Martial's line, which adjective goes with which noun, and how do you know?
7. Correct *\*Itaque autem nautae properant* so that both small words are in place.
8. Translate *Est puella fōrmōsa* and say why *est* comes first.

## Worked answers

1. Neutral: *Servus dominum timet*, "the slave fears his master". Object first: *Dominum servus timet*, "it's his master the slave fears". Verb first: *Timet servus dominum*, "the slave does fear his master".
2. *Enim* is never first, so it stands second: *Nautae enim labōrant*, "for the sailors are working".
3. *-que* hangs on the second item: *Fīlius fīliaque gaudent*. The verb is plural, for two subjects.
4. The object *multōs libellōs* comes first, and the subject *poēta* is held to the end. The stress is on how many books he has. *Multōs* would come before its noun anyway, as a word of quantity.
5. The deciding word in the phrase comes first. Here the size of the danger is the point. The neutral phrase, with the danger as the point, is *perīculum magnum*: *Perīculum magnum timēmus*.
6. *Nostrōs* goes with *libellōs*, both accusative plural masculine. *Mea* goes with *Rōma*, both nominative singular feminine. Position does not decide it; the endings do.
7. *Itaque* comes first and *autem* second, and two such words in one clause would clash in meaning anyway. Keep one: *Itaque nautae properant* or *Nautae autem properant*.
8. "There is a beautiful girl." *Est* of existence regularly stands first (ag1903 §598).
