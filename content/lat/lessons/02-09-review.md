## Before you start

This review covers part 2: the six person endings of the present tense (lesson 1), the nominative subject (lesson 2), the accusative object (lesson 3), the three genders and the neuter's shared nominative and accusative (lesson 4), *sum* (lesson 5), *nōn* and the question words *-ne*, *nōnne* and *num* (lesson 6), adjectives that agree (lesson 7), and word order with the small connecting words (lesson 8). It also uses the sounds and stress of part 1.

Everything stays inside the first two declensions and the first two conjugations, as part 2 did. Work each exercise before reading its answer. Every answer names the lesson to reread if you went wrong.

## Passage

**A. The poet and the sailor.** A constructed passage. It uses only words and grammar from parts 1 and 2.

1. Gāius et Iūlius amīcī sunt. Gāius poēta est, Iūlius nauta.
2. Nauta ventōs undāsque nōn timet; poēta autem perīcula timet.
3. Iūlius pecūniam aurumque amat et semper labōrat.
4. Gāius libellōs amat, et saepe sedet et cōgitat; itaque pecūniam nōn habet.
5. Num poēta miser est? Minimē. Laetus est, nam multōs amīcōs habet.
6. Iūlius rīdet et Gāium rogat: "Nōnne aurum amās?"
7. Gāius negat: "Amīcitia vērum aurum est."

**B. Three famous lines.** Attested; wording from The Latin Library, in this guide's spelling. Each is short, and each uses only part 2 grammar, apart from the new words glossed here.

1. *Arma virumque canō.* Vergil, Aeneid 1.1, the first words of the poem. New word: *canō* "I sing (of)". It is a verb of the third conjugation (part 4), but its "I" form ends in *-ō* like the verbs you know.
2. *Fortīs fortūna adiuvat.* Terence, Phormio 203, a proverb a slave quotes to his young master. New words: *fortīs* "brave people", an accusative plural of an adjective you will meet in part 5 (also spelled *fortēs*); *adiuvat* "helps", from *adiuvō*, which is *ad* "to" plus *iuvō*.
3. *Ego sum pāstor bonus.* Vulgate, John 10:11. New words: *ego* "I" (part 3) and *pāstor* "shepherd", a nominative singular.

## Exercises

1. Translate sentences 1 and 2 of passage A.
2. Translate sentences 3 and 4.
3. Translate sentence 5.
4. Translate sentences 6 and 7.
5. Translate the three lines of passage B.
6. In sentence 1, why is it *amīcī sunt* and not *\*amīcōs sunt*? And why does the second half need no verb?
7. In sentence 2, explain *undāsque*: what is *-que* doing, and why is it on that word?
8. In sentence 5, what answer does *Num* expect, and what case and gender is *Laetus*, and why?
9. In sentence 7, both nouns are nominative. Which is the subject, which is the predicate, and what does *vērum* agree with?
10. In passage B, line 2, which word is the subject? Explain from the endings, then say why the order is not the neutral one.
11. In passage B, line 1, explain the forms *arma* and *virumque*.
12. Say "the sailors are not afraid of the wind, are they?"
13. Say "the good master praises the tired slaves".
14. Say "is the war long?", asking about the length.
15. Say "we are Roman allies and we do not fear dangers", using *neque*.
16. Say "the temples are old and beautiful".
17. Say "the poet is a sailor" and "the poet loves the sailor", and explain the difference in case.
18. Correct *\*Puella laetus est* ("the girl is happy").
19. Correct *\*Nautae labōrat* ("the sailors are working").
20. Correct *\*Bellum magnus timent* ("they fear a great war").
21. Correct *\*Poēta bonam est* ("the poet is good").
22. Correct *\*Amaō aurum* ("I love gold").
23. Correct *\*Sum labōrō* ("I am working").
24. Correct *\*Autem nauta labōrat* ("the sailor, however, is working") and *\*Rīdentne nōn puerī?* ("aren't the boys laughing?").

## Worked answers

1. "Gaius and Julius are friends. Gaius is a poet, Julius a sailor. The sailor does not fear the winds and the waves; the poet, however, fears dangers." Two singular subjects joined by *et* take a plural verb, *sunt* (lesson 2). *Amīcī* is a predicate noun in the nominative (lesson 5). *Perīcula* is a neuter plural, accusative here as the object of *timet* (lesson 4).
2. "Julius loves money and gold and is always working. Gaius loves little books, and often sits and thinks; and so he has no money." Every verb is third person singular, agreeing with a singular subject (lessons 1 and 2). *Libellōs* is the accusative plural of a second-declension noun (lesson 3). *Itaque* stands first in its clause (lesson 8). *Nōn* stands before the verb it denies (lesson 6).
3. "The poet isn't wretched, is he? Not at all. He is happy, for he has many friends." *Num* expects "no", and *minimē* gives that answer (lesson 6). *Laetus* is a predicate adjective in the nominative masculine singular (lesson 7). *Nam* gives the reason and stands first (lesson 8). *Multōs* agrees with *amīcōs*: accusative, masculine, plural (lesson 7).
4. "Julius laughs and asks Gaius: 'You love gold, don't you?' Gaius says no: 'Friendship is true gold.'" *Gāium* is the object of *rogat*, the person asked (lesson 3). *Nōnne* expects the answer "yes" (lesson 6); Gaius surprises him. *Negat* means "says no", "denies" (lesson 6).
5. "I sing of arms and the man." "Fortune helps the brave." "I am the good shepherd." In the first, *canō* is first person singular, so the doer is "I" and needs no separate word (lesson 1). In the second, *fortūna* is the subject (see answer 10). In the third, *ego* is stated for emphasis; *pāstor bonus* is a predicate noun with its adjective, both nominative (lessons 5 and 7).
6. After *sum* the noun says what the subject is, not what is acted on, so it takes the nominative (lesson 5). The second half, *Iūlius nauta*, borrows *est* from the first half; Latin, like English "Julius a sailor", can leave a repeated verb unsaid.
7. *-que* means "and" and is attached to the second of the two things it joins: *ventōs undāsque*, "winds and waves" (lesson 8). It is an enclitic, so it is written joined to its word and pulls the stress onto the syllable before it: *un·DĀS·que* (part 1, and lesson 6 of this part).
8. *Num* expects "no" (lesson 6). *Laetus* is nominative masculine singular: it describes Gaius, a man, and it follows *est*, so it is a predicate adjective in the subject's case (lessons 5 and 7).
9. After *est* both nouns take the nominative, so the endings cannot decide (lesson 5). The neutral order puts the subject first, and the sense agrees: the sentence is about friendship, and says what it is. So *amīcitia* is the subject and *aurum* the predicate noun (lesson 8). *Vērum* is neuter nominative singular, agreeing with *aurum, aurī*, a neuter noun (lessons 4 and 7); it cannot go with the feminine *amīcitia*.
10. *Fortūna* ends in *-a*, a first-declension nominative singular. *Fortīs* ends in *-s* after a long vowel, and a masculine or feminine accusative plural always ends in *-s* (lesson 3). The verb *adiuvat* is singular, so its subject must be singular: *fortūna* (lesson 2). The neutral order would be *Fortūna fortīs adiuvat*. Putting *fortīs* first stresses it: it is the brave, and not others, whom Fortune helps (lesson 8).
11. *Arma, armōrum* is a neuter plural noun (lesson 4 of this part, which listed it as plural only). Its nominative and accusative are the same; here it is the object of "I sing", so accusative. *Virum* is the accusative singular of *vir*, the second object; *-que* joins it to *arma* (lessons 3 and 8).
12. *Num nautae ventum timent?* "Afraid of" is the plain object of *timeō* (lesson 3). *Num* expects "no" (lesson 6). The plural subject takes *-nt* (lesson 2).
13. *Dominus bonus servōs fessōs laudat.* *Bonus* agrees with *dominus*: nominative masculine singular. *Fessōs* agrees with *servōs*: accusative masculine plural (lesson 7). Neutral order: subject, object, verb (lesson 8).
14. *Longumne bellum est?* The question is about the length, so *-ne* goes on *longum* and that word comes first (lesson 6). *Longum* is neuter to agree with *bellum* (lessons 4 and 7). *Bellumne longum est?* would ask "is it the war that is long?".
15. *Sociī Rōmānī sumus neque perīcula timēmus.* *Sociī* and *Rōmānī* are nominative after *sumus* (lessons 5 and 7). *Neque* means "and not" and replaces *et nōn* (lesson 6).
16. *Templa antīqua et pulchra sunt.* *Templum* is neuter, so its plural is *templa*, and both adjectives take the neuter plural *-a* (lessons 4 and 7). *Pulcher* drops its *e* outside the masculine nominative singular: *pulchra* (lesson 7). The verb is plural.
17. *Poēta nauta est*: after *sum*, *nauta* names what the poet is, so it is nominative (lesson 5). *Poēta nautam amat*: *amō* takes an object, so *nautam* is accusative (lesson 3). The second sentence has two people; the first has one.
18. *Puella laeta est.* The adjective takes the noun's gender: *puella* is feminine (lesson 7).
19. *Nautae labōrant.* A plural subject takes the plural ending *-nt* (lesson 2). The vowel is short before *nt* (lesson 1).
20. *Bellum magnum timent.* *Bellum* is neuter, so *magnus* must be neuter: *magnum*, which is also the accusative the object needs (lessons 4 and 7).
21. *Poēta bonus est.* Two errors. After *est* the adjective is nominative, not accusative (lesson 5). And *poēta* is masculine, though it ends in *-a*, so the adjective is masculine (lesson 7).
22. *Amō aurum.* The stem vowel *ā* and the ending *-ō* have merged (lesson 1). The order is free; *Aurum amō* is the neutral one (lesson 8).
23. *Labōrō.* Latin builds its present without a word for "am"; the one verb form covers "I work", "I am working" and "I do work" (lesson 1).
24. *Nauta autem labōrat*: *autem* is never first in its clause (lesson 8). *Nōnne puerī rīdent?*: *nōnne* is *nōn* with *-ne* already attached, and it opens the question; a question expecting "yes" does not put *-ne* on the verb and *nōn* after it (lesson 6).
