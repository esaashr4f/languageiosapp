## The idea

Part 2 used two cases. The nominative named the doer and the accusative named the thing acted on. A Latin noun has four more. Each case is a form of the noun that does one family of jobs in the sentence (ag1903 §35).

- **Nominative**: the subject, and what the subject is said to be. *Rēx rīdet*, "the king laughs".
- **Vocative**: the form used to call someone. "Slave, come here!" Lesson 6 of this part.
- **Accusative**: the thing acted on; also the goal of a movement, and the case after many prepositions. Lesson 5 and lesson 13 of this part.
- **Genitive**: one noun hanging on another, "of" or "-'s": "the king's daughter". Lesson 2.
- **Dative**: the person concerned, "to" or "for": "he gives a gift to the king". Lesson 3.
- **Ablative**: where something comes from, what it is done with, where and when it happens: "from", "with", "by", "in". Lesson 4.

Allen and Greenough add that a few words keep traces of a seventh case, the **locative**, meaning "at a place": *Rōmae*, "at Rome" (ag1903 §35). Lesson 13 of this part covers it.

The ablative does three jobs because it is three old cases run together. Allen and Greenough explain that an older "from" case, a "with" case and an "in" case drifted close in meaning, and that sound changes then made their endings largely the same (ag1903 §398). Latin kept one set of forms for all three.

The word "case" is itself Latin. *Cāsus* means "a fall". Wiktionary explains that its grammatical sense was borrowed from the Greek grammarians' word for a case, *ptōsis*, which also means "a fall".

Six cases times two numbers gives up to twelve forms for every noun. You already know that nouns come in families called declensions. Latin has five. They are told apart in two ways: by the sound the stem ends in, and by the ending of the genitive singular (ag1903 §37).

Why five patterns and not one? Allen and Greenough explain that the case endings were once added in much the same way to every stem, and that the stems ended in different sounds: *ā*, *o*, *u*, *ē*, *i* or a consonant (ag1903 §36). Over centuries the last sound of the stem and the first sound of the ending ran together. What survives looks like five different sets of endings. Underneath, it is one system applied to five kinds of stem. That is why the same ideas keep returning: the accusative singular of every masculine and feminine noun ends in *-m*, in all five declensions (ag1903 §38).

## How it is built

A Latin dictionary gives every noun in the same three pieces: the nominative singular, the genitive singular (often shortened to its ending), and the gender.

- *puella, -ae* f.
- *servus, -ī* m.
- *rēx, rēgis* m.
- *manus, -ūs* f.
- *rēs, reī* f.

The nominative is listed because it is the naming form. The genitive is listed because the nominative often hides the noun's real shape. *Rēx* gives no hint that every other form starts *rēg-*. The genitive *rēgis* shows it.

To read an entry, take four steps.

1. Read the nominative. This is the form for the subject.
2. Read the genitive ending and find the declension: *-ae* first, *-ī* second, *-is* third, *-ūs* fourth, *-eī* or *-ēī* fifth (ag1903 §37).
3. Find the stem: take the genitive and remove its ending (ag1903 §37 a). *Rēg-is* gives *rēg-*. (For nouns like *puella* and *servus*, Allen and Greenough count the vowel as part of the stem, *puellā-*, *servo-*; this guide cuts before it, which is what you need for adding endings.) *Nōmin-is* gives *nōmin-*. Every form except the nominative singular is built on this stem.
4. Note the gender. It decides the adjective, and it tells you whether the accusative is the same as the nominative.

Now build the accusative singular.

1. Decide the gender. A neuter's accusative is the same as its nominative (part 2, lesson 4): *nōmen*.
2. For a masculine or feminine, add the declension's accusative ending to the stem. Each ends in *-m* (ag1903 §38): first *-am*, second *-um*, third *-em*, fourth *-um*, fifth *-em*.
3. Shorten a long vowel before the final *-m*. A Latin vowel is short before final *-m*, and in words of more than one syllable before most other single final consonants except *s* (ag1903 §604 j). So *rēs* has *rem*, with a short *e*, and *diēs* has *diem* (ag1903 §96).

Take *lēx, lēgis* f., "law". Third declension, from *-is*. The stem is *lēg-*. Feminine, so the accusative is *lēg-* plus *-em*: *lēgem*.

## Where it applies

The dictionary entry and the stem rule work for every noun in the language. Some entries look different, and each difference tells you something.

- **All five declensions** are read the same way: *fāma, -ae* f.; *modus, -ī* m.; *genus, generis* n.; *cāsus, -ūs* m.; *diēs, diēī* m. (ag1903 §37).
- **Third-declension nouns** always print the full genitive, because the stem cannot be guessed from the nominative: *nōmen, nōminis*; *genus, generis*; *vōx, vōcis*. Lesson 7 of this part explains why their nominatives look so different.
- **Plural-only nouns** are listed in the nominative plural, with a plural genitive: *arma, armōrum* n.; *tenebrae, -ārum* f. (part 2, lesson 4).
- **Nouns that do not decline** are marked "indeclinable": *nihil* "nothing", and *fās*, *nefās* "right" and "wrong". They are used only as nominative and accusative (ag1903 §103).
- **Nouns that change meaning or gender in the plural.** *Littera* is a letter of the alphabet; the plural *litterae* is a letter you send, or literature. *Locus* "place" has two plurals: *loca*, neuter, "places", and *locī*, "passages in a book" (ag1903 §§106-107).
- **Adjectives** are listed by their nominatives, *bonus, -a, -um*, because their genitive is predictable. Some adjectives of another pattern give a genitive instead (part 5).
- **The stem rule applies to every case but the nominative singular.** The cases of lessons 2 to 5 of this part are all built on the stem you find here.

The cases themselves cover every noun's job in a sentence. Lessons 2 to 6 of this part take them one at a time. Lesson 13 shows how the accusative, the ablative and the locative say "where to", "where from" and "where".

## Watch out

**Do not build on the nominative.** *\*Rēxem* adds the ending to the wrong form. The genitive gives the stem: *rēg-is*, so *rēgem*. In the same way *nōmen, nōminis* gives *nōmin-* for every form except the nominative and accusative singular.

**Do not take every *-us* noun as second declension.** *Servus, -ī*, *manus, -ūs* and *genus, generis* all end in *-us*. Only the genitive tells them apart. *\*Manī* and *\*genī* are not Latin. Check the second form of the entry every time.

**Do not take the declension as the gender.** *Manus* is feminine though it ends in *-us* (ag1903 §90). *Diēs* is masculine though the fifth declension is otherwise feminine (ag1903 §97). Gender and declension are listed separately because they are separate facts.

**Do not give a neuter an accusative in *-m*.** *\*Nōminem* treats *nōmen* as masculine or feminine. A neuter's accusative is its nominative: *Nōmen mūtat*.

**Do not keep the long vowel before *-m*.** *\*Rēm* and *\*diēm* break the rule that a vowel is short before final *-m* (ag1903 §§96, 604 j). Write *rem*, *diem*.

**Do not read the case off one ending.** *-ae* is the genitive and the dative singular and the nominative plural of the first declension. *Fīliae* alone could be any of the three. The sentence decides. The next lessons show how.

**Do not mix up the two terms.** A case is one job, such as the accusative. A declension is one family of nouns, such as the third. Every declension has every case.

**Do not take a Spanish or French noun for the Latin nominative.** Most Romance nouns continue the Latin accusative, not the nominative. Spanish *rey* comes from *rēgem*, and French *rien* "nothing" from *rem*, "a thing" (Wiktionary). The Latin subject is still *rēx*, *rēs*.

## Connections

Arabic nouns have three cases, marked *-u*, *-a* and *-i*; Latin's six cover the same ground and more. Japanese and Korean put the job in a separate particle after the noun: Japanese の, に and で do much of the work of the Latin genitive, dative and ablative. Spanish and French lost the cases, and mostly kept the accusative form of each noun: Spanish *nombre*, French *nom* from *nōmen*.

## Exercises

1. Read the entry *vōx, vōcis* f.: give the declension, the stem and the accusative singular.
2. Do the same for *modus, -ī* m. and *fāma, -ae* f.
3. Why does a dictionary give *genus, generis* in full but *servus, -ī* with only the ending?
4. Say "they announce the law".
5. Say "we change the name" and explain why the noun has no *-m*.
6. A student writes *\*manī* as the genitive of *manus*. What does the entry *manus, -ūs* f. tell him?
7. Give the accusative singular of *rēs* and *diēs*, and explain the vowel.
8. Which jobs does the ablative do, and why does one case do so many?

## Worked answers

1. The genitive ends in *-is*, so *vōx* is third declension. The stem is *vōc-*. It is feminine, so the accusative adds *-em*: *vōcem*.
2. *Modus, -ī*: second declension, stem *mod-*, masculine, accusative *modum*. *Fāma, -ae*: first declension, stem *fām-*, feminine, accusative *fāmam*.
3. The stem of *servus* is plain from the nominative, so the ending is enough. The stem of *genus* is *gener-*, which the nominative does not show. Third-declension entries give the whole genitive for that reason.
4. "They announce" is *nūntiant*. *Lēx, lēgis* f. has the stem *lēg-*, accusative *lēgem*: *Lēgem nūntiant*.
5. *Nōmen mūtāmus*. *Nōmen* is neuter, and a neuter's accusative is the same as its nominative.
6. The genitive is *manūs*, so the noun is fourth declension, not second. The entry also says it is feminine. There is no form *\*manī*.
7. *Rem* and *diem*. The stem vowel *ē* is shortened before the final *-m*, because a Latin vowel is always short before final *-m*.
8. "From" (separation and source), "with" or "by" (tool and company), and "in" or "at" (place and time). Allen and Greenough explain that three old cases with these meanings merged, partly because their meanings overlapped and partly because sound change made their endings alike.
