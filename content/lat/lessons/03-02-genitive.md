## The idea

Every case so far has linked a noun to the verb. The nominative names who acts, the accusative what is acted on. The **genitive** links a noun to another noun. In "the king's daughter", "the walls of the town", "a man of great courage", the second noun does not take part in the action. It narrows down the first: which daughter, which walls, what kind of man.

Allen and Greenough put the genitive's job in one sentence: it expresses the relation of one noun to another (ag1903 §341). They add that it is sometimes called the adjective case. That name explains almost everything the genitive does. *Fīlia rēgis*, "the king's daughter", says what *fīlia mea* "my daughter" says: it picks out one daughter by her connection to someone. A genitive does for a noun what an adjective does.

English has two ways to say this: "the king's daughter" and "the daughter of the king". Latin has one, the genitive ending. It is translated "of" or "-'s", but it covers more relations than either. *Amor patriae* is "love of the homeland", where the homeland is what is loved, not who loves.

You already know one genitive form for every noun: the dictionary's second form is the genitive singular. *Rēx, rēgis*: *rēgis* means "of the king".

## How it is built

The endings for the first two declensions:

- first declension: *-ae* for one, *-ārum* for more than one: *puellae* "of the girl", *puellārum* "of the girls";
- second declension: *-ī* for one, *-ōrum* for more than one: *servī* "of the slave", *servōrum* "of the slaves". Neuters are the same: *bellī*, *bellōrum*.

Nouns in *-er* add these to the stem the dictionary shows: *puerī*, *puerōrum*; *agrī*, *agrōrum*. The third declension's genitive singular is *-is*, as in *rēgis*. Its plural comes in lesson 7 of this part.

The genitive plural of every declension ends in *-um* (ag1903 §38): *-ārum*, *-ōrum*, and later *-um* and *-uum*. Allen and Greenough record two older endings that explain the singular. The first declension's *-ae* was once *-āī*, said as two syllables; poets still use it now and then, as in *aulāī* "of the hall" (ag1903 §43). An even older genitive in *-ās* survives in one phrase, *pater familiās*, "father of the household" (ag1903 §43).

Nouns in *-ius* and *-ium* had a single *-ī* in the genitive until the time of Augustus: *fīlī* "of the son", *ōtī* "of leisure" (ag1903 §49). Later writers use *fīliī*. Both are correct; Cicero has the short form.

To build "the walls of the towns":

1. The head noun is *mūrus*, "wall". Its case depends on its job in the sentence: here, the object, so *mūrōs*.
2. The towns say which walls. That is the genitive. *Oppidum* is second declension, plural: *oppidōrum*.
3. Put them together: *mūrōs oppidōrum*. The genitive usually follows its noun, but may come first for emphasis (lesson 8 of part 2).

The genitive never agrees with the noun it hangs on. *Mūrōs* is accusative plural masculine, *oppidōrum* genitive plural neuter, and each keeps its own form.

## Where it applies

- **Possession and authorship**: *servus dominī*, "the master's slave"; *librī Cicerōnis*, "Cicero's books" (ag1903 §343).
- **The one who has a quality or does an act**: *fāma Caesaris*, "Caesar's reputation"; *mors Ariovistī*, "the death of Ariovistus" (ag1903 §343). Grammars call this the subjective genitive.
- **The object of a feeling or an action**, after nouns like *amor*, *timor*, *odium*, *studium*: *amor patriae* "love for the homeland", *timor deōrum* "fear of the gods" (ag1903 §348). This is the objective genitive.
- **The whole that a part is taken from** (ag1903 §346): after nouns such as *pars*: *pars nautārum*; after neuter words of quantity: *multum labōris* "a lot of toil", *nihil novī* "nothing new"; after the adverbs *satis* "enough", *parum* "too little" and *nimis* "too much": *satis pecūniae*, *parum ōtī*.
- **Quality**, with an adjective: *vir magnī animī* (ag1903 §345). Prose more often uses the ablative for this (lesson 4 of this part).
- **Material**: *talentum aurī*, "a talent of gold" (ag1903 §344).
- **In the predicate, after *sum***: *Ager rēgis est*, "the field is the king's" (ag1903 §343).
- **After adjectives of desire, knowledge, memory, fullness and sharing**: *glōriae cupidus*, *plēnus dōnōrum* "full of gifts", *iūris perītus* "skilled in law" (ag1903 §349).
- **"For the sake of"**: the genitive before *causā*: *glōriae causā*, "for glory's sake". The core vocabulary lists it this way; *causā* itself is a form of *causa* taught in lesson 4.
- **After verbs of remembering, forgetting and accusing** (ag1903 §§350-352). Part 5, lesson 9.
- **Value**: *magnī* "of great worth" (ag1903 §417). Part 5.

## Watch out

**Do not use a preposition for "of".** Spanish *la hija del rey* and French *la fille du roi* tempt you to look for a word for *de*. *\*Fīlia dē rēge* means "the daughter about the king": Latin *dē* means "down from" or "about" (lesson 4 of this part). Use the genitive: *fīlia rēgis*.

**Do not choose *-ārum* or *-ōrum* by gender.** *\*Nautōrum* gives a masculine noun the second-declension ending. The declension decides: *nautārum*.

**Do not read every *-ī* as a plural.** *Servī* is the nominative plural and also the genitive singular. *Servī labōrant* is "the slaves work"; *Fīlius servī labōrat* is "the slave's son works". The verb's number and the noun in front of it decide.

**Do not use a genitive of quality without an adjective.** *\*Vir animī* does not mean "a man of courage" (ag1903 §345). Add the adjective, *vir magnī animī*, or use an adjective alone.

**Do not put the name of a city in the genitive.** English "the city of Rome" is *urbs Rōma*, two nominatives side by side. *\*Urbs Rōmae* is not the regular classical form: a genitive in place of apposition is rare (ag1903 §343 d).

**Do not use the genitive of "I" for "my".** For "my book" Latin uses the adjective *meus*: *liber meus*, not a genitive of the word for "I" (ag1903 §343). Lesson 10 of this part explains.

**Do not make the genitive agree.** *\*Mūrōs oppidōs* copies the case of the head noun. The genitive keeps its own case: *mūrōs oppidōrum*.

**Do not assume "of" means possession.** *Amor patris* can be a father's love for his child, or someone's love for his father (ag1903 §348). Only context decides. In the other direction, English "of" for material, "made of gold", is usually *ex aurō* in Latin, with a preposition taught in lesson 4.

## Connections

Japanese の and Korean 의 join a noun to the noun after it, much as the Latin genitive does. Arabic shows the same relation with two nouns side by side and the second in its *-i* case, the genitive. Spanish *de* and French *de* took over the genitive's job when the cases were lost.

## Exercises

1. Say "the sailor's daughter" and "the sailors' daughters", as subjects.
2. Say "we are looking at the gates of the town".
3. Take apart *Pars frūmentī manet* and translate it.
4. Translate *Uxor lēgātī glōriae cupida est*.
5. Say "the slave has too little leisure".
6. Why is *\*nautōrum* wrong, and what is right?
7. What two meanings can *amor patris* have?
8. Translate *Fīliī rēgis rīdent* and *Fīlius rēgis rīdet*, and explain the forms of *fīliī* and *fīlius*.

## Worked answers

1. The head noun is the subject, so nominative: *fīlia*, *fīliae*. The sailors are genitive: *nautae* singular, *nautārum* plural. *Fīlia nautae*; *fīliae nautārum*.
2. "We look at" is *spectāmus*. The gates are the object: *portās*. The town says which gates: *oppidī*. *Portās oppidī spectāmus*.
3. *Pars*: part, nominative singular, the subject. *Frūment-ī*: grain, genitive singular, the whole. *Mane-t*: stays, singular to agree with *pars*. "Part of the grain remains."
4. *Uxor*: wife, nominative singular. *Lēgāt-ī*: of the envoy. *Glōri-ae*: of glory, depending on *cupida*. *Cupid-a*: feminine nominative singular, agreeing with *uxor*. "The envoy's wife is eager for glory."
5. *Parum* takes the genitive of what there is too little of: *parum ōtī*. The verb is *habet*: *Servus parum ōtī habet*. *Ōtiī* is also correct.
6. *Nauta* is first declension, and the first declension's genitive plural is *-ārum*, whatever the gender: *nautārum*.
7. A father's love (the father loves) or love for a father (the father is loved). The genitive does not say which.
8. *Fīliī* is nominative plural and agrees with the plural *rīdent*: "the king's sons are laughing". *Fīlius* is nominative singular: "the king's son is laughing". In both, *rēgis* is the genitive singular of *rēx*.
