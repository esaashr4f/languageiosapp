## The idea

In "the sailor gives the girl a rose", two people are involved besides the giver. The rose is handed over: it is the object, in the accusative. The girl is not handed over. She receives, she is the one the giving is for. Latin puts her in a third case, the **dative**.

Allen and Greenough describe the difference this way. The accusative names what an action directly affects. The dative names someone who shares in the action or receives it consciously (ag1903 §360). In *Dat puellae rosam* the girl takes the rose; the rose has no say in it. That is why the dative is mostly used of persons, or of things thought of as persons.

So the dative answers the question "to whom?" or "for whom?". It is translated "to" or "for", and sometimes by nothing at all, as in English "give the girl a rose". The glossary's short name for it is "the person concerned". The person may gain or lose, be told, be obeyed or be harmed; in each case the action happens with that person in view.

Allen and Greenough also note a second family of uses: the dative of purpose, "that for which something serves" (ag1903 §360). *Salūtī est*, "he is for safety", means "he is a saviour". This lesson shows both.

## How it is built

The endings:

- first declension: *-ae* for one, *-īs* for more than one: *puellae*, *puellīs*;
- second declension: *-ō* for one, *-īs* for more than one: *servō*, *servīs*; neuters the same: *bellō*, *bellīs*;
- third declension singular: *-ī* on the stem: *rēgī*, *frātrī*, *sorōrī*. The plural comes in lesson 7 of this part.

Two facts save memory. The dative and ablative plural are always the same (ag1903 §38), so *-īs* will serve again in the next lesson. And the first declension's *-ae* is now three cases at once: genitive singular, dative singular and nominative plural. Allen and Greenough record that the dative, like the genitive, was once *-āī*, here pronounced as a diphthong (ag1903 §43). Both became *-ae*.

One small exception. *Dea* "goddess" and *fīlia* "daughter" take *-ābus* in the dative and ablative plural, *deābus*, *fīliābus*, so as not to be confused with *deīs*, *fīliīs* from *deus* and *fīlius* (ag1903 §43).

To build "the sailor gives the girl a rose" with words you know, say "the teacher tells the boys a story".

1. The verb: *narrō* "tell", third person singular: *narrat*.
2. The subject: *magister*, nominative.
3. What is told is the object: *fābula*, accusative singular: *fābulam*.
4. Who is told is the receiver: *puer*, dative plural: *puerīs*.
5. Put the subject first and the verb last; the two other nouns go between, in either order: *Magister puerīs fābulam narrat*.

The new verb *dō* "give" has one irregularity. Its stem vowel is short everywhere except in *dās*: *damus*, *datis*, *dant*, not *\*dāmus* (Wiktionary).

Some verbs take the dative where English has a plain object: "obey", "please", "harm", "persuade", "favour". Allen and Greenough explain that these verbs kept an older meaning that did not take an object (ag1903 §367). Their examples: *suādeō*, the simple verb inside *persuādeō*, is literally "make something pleasant to someone", and *serviō* "serve" is "be a slave to someone". The person is not acted on but concerned, so the dative is logical.

## Where it applies

- **The receiver, with verbs that also take an object**: giving, telling, showing, owing, offering. *Rēgī dōnum dat*; *Puerīs fābulam narrat*; *Frātrī pecūniam dēbeō* (ag1903 §362). Also *nūntiō*, *praebeō*.
- **The person addressed or affected, with verbs that take no object**: *Magister puerīs respondet*, "the teacher answers the boys" (ag1903 §366).
- **Verbs of favouring, helping, pleasing, obeying, persuading, commanding, serving and their opposites** take the dative alone (ag1903 §367): *placeō*, *noceō*, *pāreō*, *persuādeō*, *faveō*, *studeō* "be keen on", *imperō*. *Rēx sociīs imperat*, "the king gives orders to the allies".
- **Compounds of *sum***: *adsum* "be present, stand by", *dēsum* "be lacking", *prōsum* "be of use" (ag1903 §§370, 373). *Fortūna nautīs adest*, "fortune stands by the sailors". *Prōsum* puts a *d* before a vowel: *prōdest*, *prōdestis*.
- **Possession, with *sum***: *Sorōrī meae multī amīcī sunt* (ag1903 §373). Vergil's shepherd says *est mihi namque domī pater*, "for I have a father at home" (Eclogues 3.33). Allen and Greenough contrast it with the genitive: *Liber est meus* stresses whose the book is, *Est mihi liber* that I have one.
- **A name, with *nōmen est***: *Puerō nōmen est Mārcus*, "the boy's name is Marcus". The name itself is usually nominative in Cicero; Livy and Sallust often put it in the dative too (ag1903 §373).
- **Advantage and disadvantage**: who an action is done for, when the sentence is complete without it: *tibi arās* (ag1903 §376).
- **The person judging**: "in someone's eyes" (ag1903 §378). *Rēx deīs cārus est*, "the king is dear to the gods", shades into "the king is dear in the gods' eyes".
- **Purpose, often with a second dative of the person**: *Lēgātus sociīs salūtī est* (ag1903 §382).
- **After adjectives of pleasing, fitness, nearness, likeness and friendship**: *grātus*, *aptus*, *cārus*, *amīcus* "friendly", *inimīcus* "hostile" (ag1903 §384).
- **Later uses**: taking something away from a person (ag1903 §381), the dative of the agent with the gerundive (part 7), and the poetic dative of the direction of motion (ag1903 §360).

## Watch out

**Do not put the receiver in the accusative because English has no "to".** "He gives the king a gift" has two plain objects in English. *\*Rēgem dōnum dat* has two accusatives and means nothing. The receiver is dative: *Rēgī dōnum dat*.

**Do not translate Spanish *a* or French *à* with *ad*.** *\*Ad rēgem dōnum dat* uses the preposition of motion towards a place (lesson 5 of this part). For a person receiving, use the dative alone. *Ad* with the accusative is right only when real movement is the point, as with sending a letter to someone far away (ag1903 §363).

**Do not give a dative verb an accusative.** *\*Servus dominum pāret* and *\*Ventus nautās nocet* treat "obey" and "harm" as in English. These verbs take the dative: *dominō pāret*, *nautīs nocet*.

**Do not give the dative to every verb of helping.** *Iuvō* "help" takes the accusative (ag1903 §367): *\*Nautīs iuvat* is wrong; *Nautās iuvat*. Each verb has to be learned with its case.

**Do not make the owner the subject of *sum*.** *\*Soror mea multī amīcī sunt* has a subject that does not agree with the verb. With *sum*, the thing owned is the subject and the owner is dative: *Sorōrī meae multī amīcī sunt*. If you want "have" as a verb, use *habeō*: *Soror mea multōs amīcōs habet*.

**Do not read *-īs* as one case.** *Puellīs* and *servīs* are dative or ablative plural. Here, the verb and sense decide.

**Do not read *-ae* as one case either.** *Fīliae fābulam narrat* is "he tells his daughter a story", with *fīliae* dative singular; *Fīliae rīdent* is "the daughters laugh". The verb's number is the first clue.

**Do not lengthen the *a* of *dō*.** *\*Dāmus*, *\*dātis*: the forms are *damus*, *datis*, *dant*. Only *dās* has a long vowel.

**Do not use the dative for a place you go to.** "He hurries to Rome" is not *\*Rōmae properat*. Places take the accusative, with or without a preposition (lessons 5 and 13 of this part).

## Connections

Japanese に and Korean 에게 mark the receiver, as the dative does: Japanese 子どもに本をあげる, "give a child a book". Spanish keeps a dative in its pronouns: *le doy un regalo*, "I give him a gift", where *le* continues the Latin dative *illī* (Wiktionary). Korean, like Latin, says "to me there is" for "I have": 나에게 책이 있다.

## Exercises

1. Say "the poet tells the girls a story".
2. Say "the allies obey the king", and explain the case of "king".
3. Take apart *Fīliae meae pecūniam dō* and translate it.
4. Say "my brother has a beautiful field", with *sum*. Then say it with *habeō*.
5. A student writes *\*Puerōs nocet*. Correct it and explain.
6. Why is *Nautās iuvat* right but *\*Nautīs iuvat* wrong?
7. Translate *Fortūna bonīs adest*.
8. Translate *Fābula nautīs nōn placet* in natural English.

## Worked answers

1. "Tells" is *narrat*, with *poēta* as subject. The story is the object: *fābulam*. The girls receive it: dative plural *puellīs*. *Poēta puellīs fābulam narrat*.
2. *Sociī rēgī pārent*. *Pāreō* takes the dative: the king is the one obeyed, concerned in the action, not acted on. *Rēx, rēgis* gives the stem *rēg-* and the dative *rēgī*.
3. *Fīli-ae me-ae*: to my daughter, dative singular, with *meae* agreeing. *Pecūni-am*: money, the object. *D-ō*: I give. "I am giving my daughter money." *Fīliae* could also be genitive or nominative plural, but the singular verb, with "I" as subject, and the sense rule those out.
4. With *sum*: *Frātrī meō ager pulcher est*. The field is the subject; the brother, the owner, is dative. With *habeō*: *Frāter meus agrum pulchrum habet*.
5. *Noceō* takes the dative of the one harmed: *Puerīs nocet*, "he harms the boys".
6. *Iuvō* is one of the verbs of helping that take the accusative (ag1903 §367). The dative belongs to verbs like *faveō* and *adsum*.
7. *Fortūn-a* is the subject; *bon-īs* is a dative plural adjective used as a noun, "good people"; *adest* is "stands by". "Fortune stands by the good."
8. "The sailors do not like the story." The Latin says the story is not pleasing to the sailors; English makes the sailors the subject.
