## The idea

The last lesson introduced the prepositions that take the ablative. This lesson adds the larger group that takes the accusative, and the two prepositions, *in* and *sub*, that take either.

Why does Latin need prepositions at all, when it has cases? Allen and Greenough answer that it once hardly did. In the earliest stages, they say, the case endings alone gave the sense. As the endings lost force, speakers added adverbs to make the meaning precise, and these adverbs became prepositions (ag1903 §219). Many are still case forms themselves: *suprā* "above" is an old ablative, *circum* "around" an old accusative (ag1903 §219). Most of them still work as adverbs too: *post* "behind, after" also means "afterwards"; *prope* "near" also means "nearby".

So a preposition does not replace the case. It sharpens it. Each preposition is fixed to one case, and the choice is not random. The ablative ones are the "from" words (*ā*, *ē*, *dē*), the "with" words (*cum*, *sine*) and a few of position (*prō*). The accusative is the case of the goal, the point a movement aims at. *Ad* "to, towards" takes it for that reason, and most prepositions of direction and position follow.

The clearest proof is *in* and *sub*. With the accusative they mean motion: *in silvam* "into the forest", *sub arborem* "to the foot of the tree". With the ablative they mean rest: *in silvā* "in the forest", *sub arbore* "under the tree" (ag1903 §220). The case alone tells you whether anything moves.

## How it is built

To say "the sailors are hurrying to the gate":

1. The verb: *properant*.
2. The gate is the goal of the movement. "To, towards" is *ad*.
3. *Ad* takes the accusative: *portam*.
4. *Nautae ad portam properant*.

To choose between the two cases after *in*:

1. Ask whether the sentence describes movement into the place, or being in it.
2. Movement into: accusative. *In silvam ambulat*, "he walks into the forest".
3. Being in, or doing something inside: ablative. *In silvā ambulat*, "he walks in the forest". The walking happens inside; nobody enters.

*Sub* works the same way. "Under" with movement, accusative; "under" at rest, ablative.

A preposition normally stands just before its noun. When an adjective goes with the noun, the preposition often stands between them: *magnā cum cūrā*, "with great care"; *multās per gentēs*, "through many nations" (ag1903 §599).

The accusative has one more use that belongs here. A few verbs of teaching, asking and hiding take two accusatives: one for the person, one for the thing (ag1903 §396). *Doceō* "teach", *rogō* "ask" and *cēlō* "hide" are the commonest. Allen and Greenough explain why: each verb has two senses at once. *Doceō* means both "show a thing" and "instruct a person"; *cēlō* both "hide a thing" and "keep a person in the dark" (ag1903 §396). Each sense brings its own object.

## Where it applies

**Prepositions with the accusative** (ag1903 §§220-221). Each is shown with its main senses.

- *ad*: to, towards; near; with persons, "to": *ad portam*, *ad rēgem*.
- *ante*: in front of; before (time): *ante templum*, *ante bellum*.
- *post*: behind; after (time): *post mūrum*, *post bellum*.
- *per*: through, across: *per agrōs*.
- *inter*: between, among; during: *inter arma*, *inter sociōs*.
- *apud*: at the house of, in the presence of, among: *apud rēgem*.
- *circum*: around: *circum oppidum*.
- *trāns*: across, on the other side of: *trāns rīpam*.
- *contrā*: against; opposite: *contrā barbarōs pugnant*.
- *ob* and *propter*: on account of, because of: *ob culpam*, *propter perīculum*. *Ob* also means "facing, in front of".
- *prope*: near: *prope castra*.
- *praeter*: past, along; besides, except: *praeter rīpam*, *praeter Caesarem* "except Caesar".
- *suprā*: above, beyond: *suprā caelum*.

**Prepositions with either case** (ag1903 §220):

- *in*: accusative "into, onto, against"; ablative "in, on".
- *sub*: accusative "to the foot of, under" (with movement); ablative "under" (at rest).

**Prepositions with the ablative**, from lesson 4: *ā/ab*, *ē/ex*, *dē*, *cum*, *sine*, *prō*.

**The same words as adverbs.** *Ante*, *post*, *prope*, *circum*, *contrā* and *suprā* can stand without a noun: *Post labōrāmus*, "afterwards we work" (ag1903 §219). The case tells you which: with a noun in the accusative, a preposition; with no noun, an adverb.

**Two accusatives with verbs of teaching, asking and hiding**: *doceō*, *rogō*, *cēlō*, and a few others (ag1903 §396). *Magistrum sententiam rogō*, "I ask the teacher his opinion".

**Other places the accusative goes.** With verbs of naming and making, a second accusative says what the object is called or made (ag1903 §393): part 4 shows it with *appellō* "call" and *creō* "elect". The goal of motion with names of towns, and the extent of time and space, need no preposition at all: lesson 13 of this part.

## Watch out

**Do not give a preposition the wrong case.** Spanish *con*, *a* and *en* take any noun unchanged, so the Latin case is easy to forget. *\*Ad portā*, *\*cum sociōs*: *ad* takes the accusative, *cum* the ablative. *Ad portam*, *cum sociīs*.

**Do not use *in* with the ablative for "into".** Spanish *en* and English "in" cover both. *\*In silvā properant* says they hurry about inside the forest. To go in, use the accusative: *In silvam properant*.

**Do not use *in* with the accusative for "in".** *\*In silvam iacent* makes lying down a journey. Rest takes the ablative: *In silvā iacent*.

**Do not use *per* for "because of".** Spanish *por* means both "through" and "because of". Latin splits them: *per* is "through"; "because of" is *propter* or *ob*, or the plain ablative of cause. *\*Per perīculum manent* is wrong; *Propter perīculum manent*.

**Do not give the person a dative after *doceō*.** Spanish *enseñar algo a alguien* and French *apprendre quelque chose à quelqu'un* put the person in a dative. *\*Puerīs litterās docet* is wrong. Both are accusative: *Puerōs litterās docet*. The same with *rogō*: *Magistrum sententiam rogō*, not *\*Magistrō*.

**Do not use *ad* for a receiver.** "He gives a gift to the king" is *Rēgī dōnum dat* (lesson 3 of this part). *Ad* means movement towards.

**Do not read *apud* as "in".** *\*Apud silvam habitat* does not mean "he lives in the forest". *Apud* is used with persons, or with a place in the sense "at, near". Say *In silvā habitat*.

**Do not add a preposition to the name of a town you go to.** "They hurry to Rome" is *Rōmam properant*, with no *ad* in the usual classical style (ag1903 §427). Lesson 13 of this part explains.

## Connections

Japanese and Korean have postpositions, which come after the noun: 森へ, 숲으로 "to the forest". Latin prepositions come before it, but do the same work. Spanish *en* covers both *in silvā* and *in silvam*; German, like Latin, keeps them apart by case: *im Wald* "in the forest", *in den Wald* "into the forest".

## Exercises

1. Say "the boys walk through the forest".
2. Say "the girls hurry into the temple", then "the girls are in the temple".
3. Take apart *Post bellum sociī trāns rīpam habitant* and translate it.
4. Translate *Nautae circum īnsulam nāvigant*.
5. Say "the teacher teaches the girls wisdom".
6. Correct *\*In castra manent* and *\*Ad rēgī properat*.
7. In *Sub arborem properat* and *Sub arbore sedet*, explain the two cases.
8. Is *contrā* a preposition or an adverb in *Contrā barbarōs pugnant*? And in *Magister rogat; puer contrā tacet*?

## Worked answers

1. "Through" is *per* with the accusative: *per silvam*. *Puerī per silvam ambulant*.
2. Into: *in* with the accusative: *Puellae in templum properant*. In: *in* with the ablative: *Puellae in templō sunt*.
3. *Post bell-um*: after the war, accusative. *Soci-ī*: the allies, subject. *Trāns rīp-am*: across the river bank, accusative. *Habita-nt*: they live. "After the war the allies live on the other side of the river."
4. *Circum īnsul-am*: around the island, accusative. "The sailors sail around the island."
5. *Doceō* takes two accusatives: the girls, *puellās*, and wisdom, *sapientiam*. *Magister puellās sapientiam docet*.
6. Staying is rest, not motion: *In castrīs manent*, with the ablative plural. *Ad* takes the accusative: *Ad rēgem properat*.
7. With movement towards the place under the tree, *sub* takes the accusative: "he hurries under the tree". At rest, the ablative: "he sits under the tree".
8. In the first, *contrā* has a noun in the accusative, *barbarōs*: a preposition, "they fight against the barbarians". In the second it has no noun: an adverb, "the boy, in reply, is silent".
