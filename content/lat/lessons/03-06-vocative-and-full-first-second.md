## The idea

Every case so far has given a noun a part in the sentence. The **vocative** does not. It is the form you use to call or address someone: "Marcus, are you working?" (ag1903 §340). The person called stands outside the sentence and listens to it.

For almost every noun the vocative is the same as the nominative (ag1903 §38). *Puella* calls a girl, *puellae* calls several, *rēx* calls a king. So you have been able to address people since part 2 without knowing it.

The exception is the commonest type of name. Second-declension nouns in *-us* have a vocative in short *-e*: *servus*, *serve*; *Mārcus*, *Mārce* (ag1903 §38). Why? Allen and Greenough explain that the second declension's stem vowel *o* had a variant *e*, an alternation inherited from the parent language, and that this variant survives in the vocative (ag1903 §45). The vocative *-e* is not an ending added to the stem. It is a form of the stem itself, with no ending added (ag1903 §338).

Nouns in *-ius*, which include many Roman family names, contract further. *Fīlius* becomes *fīlī*, *Lūcius* *Lūcī*, *Vergilius* *Vergilī*, keeping the stress of the nominative: *Ver·GI·lī* (ag1903 §49). The possessive *meus* has a vocative of its own, *mī*: *mī fīlī*, "my son" (ag1903 §145).

This lesson also completes the first two declensions. You now know all six cases for each, so the full tables come at the end, with the few special forms each declension has.

## How it is built

To call someone, take three steps.

1. Find the nominative and the declension from the dictionary entry.
2. If the noun is second declension in *-us*, replace *-us* with *-e*: *amīcus*, *amīce*. If it ends in *-ius*, replace *-ius* with *-ī*: *Lūcius*, *Lūcī*. Otherwise, the vocative is the nominative: *puella*, *puer*, *magister*, *nauta*.
3. An adjective with the noun agrees with it in the vocative: *serve bone*, "good slave"; *mī fīlī*.

The rest of the sentence speaks to that person, so its verb is second person: *Mārce, labōrās*, "Marcus, you are working".

The vocative is often preceded by *ō*, "O": *ō dea*. Wiktionary notes that this use of *ō* copies Greek, and that older Latin called people with the bare vocative alone.

For the full first and second declensions, recall what each lesson of this part added:

- nominative and accusative from part 2;
- genitive *-ae, -ārum* and *-ī, -ōrum* (lesson 2);
- dative *-ae, -īs* and *-ō, -īs* (lesson 3);
- ablative *-ā, -īs* and *-ō, -īs* (lesson 4);
- vocative, the same as the nominative except *-e* and *-ī* in the second-declension singular (this lesson).

Nouns in *-er* follow their genitive. *Puer, puerī* keeps the *e* throughout; *ager, agrī* drops it everywhere except the nominative and vocative singular (ag1903 §47). Both have the vocative *puer*, *ager*: there is no *-us* to replace. *Vir, virī* "man" goes like *puer*.

## Where it applies

- **Calling or addressing a person**: *Mārce*, *amīce*, *puellae* (ag1903 §340).
- **Addressing gods**: *dea*, *ō dea*. *Deus* has no vocative in classical Latin; Allen and Greenough report that it is said to have been *dee*, and the Vulgate uses *deus* (ag1903 §49). The Vulgate renders the cry from the cross as *Deus meus, Deus meus* (Mark 15:34).
- **Addressing things, places and abstractions as if they were people**: *Ō fortūna*, *Rōma*.
- **With an adjective, which agrees**: *serve bone*, *mī fīlī*. Poets sometimes put in the vocative an adjective that describes the person spoken to, where prose would use the nominative (ag1903 §340).
- **Proper names in *-ius*** contract to *-ī*: *Gāī*, *Lūcī*, *Vergilī* (ag1903 §49). Adjectives in *-ius* do not: they take *-ie* (ag1903 §49).
- **With *ō***: in solemn address, appeals and emotion, *ō dea*.
- **In letters and farewells**: the vocative goes with the command forms of part 4, as in *valē, amīce*, "goodbye, my friend".

The completed first and second declensions apply to every noun whose genitive ends in *-ae* or *-ī*, and to every adjective like *bonus*, *miser* and *pulcher*. The special forms:

- *dea* and *fīlia* have *-ābus* in the dative and ablative plural (ag1903 §43);
- the genitive plural *-um* for *-ōrum* in a few words, especially in verse: *deum* "of the gods", *virum* "of men", and words of money and measure such as *nummum* "of coins" (ag1903 §49);
- the genitive singular *-ī* for *-iī* in *-ius* and *-ium* nouns before Augustus: *fīlī*, *ōtī* (ag1903 §49);
- *deus* has three plural forms in the nominative, *dī*, *diī* and *deī*, and *dīs*, *diīs*, *deīs* in the dative and ablative (Wiktionary);
- a locative in *-ae* and *-ī* for names of towns: *Rōmae*, *Corinthī* (ag1903 §§43, 49), lesson 13 of this part;
- Greek nouns keep some Greek endings: *Aenēās*, which you met in part 2 (ag1903 §§44, 52).

## Watch out

**Do not call a second-declension *-us* noun by its nominative.** Spanish says *Marco, ¿trabajas?* with no change. *\*Mārcus, labōrās?* is wrong in classical Latin. Say *Mārce, labōrās?* The nominative for address does occur alongside an imperative (ag1903 §340), but the regular form is the vocative.

**Do not add *-e* to nouns without *-us*.** *\*Naute*, *\*puere*, *\*magistre*: *nauta* is first declension, and *puer* and *magister* have no *-us* to replace. Say *nauta*, *puer*, *magister*. (*Puere* is found in early Latin, as if from a nominative *puerus*: ag1903 §50.)

**Do not give *-ius* nouns *-ie*.** *\*Lūcie* is not the classical form. Names and *fīlius* contract: *Lūcī*, *fīlī*. Only adjectives in *-ius* have *-ie*.

**Do not use *meus* when calling someone.** The regular vocative masculine is *mī*: *mī fīlī*, *mī amīce* (ag1903 §145). The feminine is unchanged: *mea fīlia*.

**Do not make the verb third person.** *\*Mārce, labōrat* speaks to Marcus but says "he works". When you address someone, the verb is second person: *Mārce, labōrās*.

**Do not put the predicate in the vocative.** *\*Ō fortūna, saeve es*: the description after *es* is a predicate adjective, nominative and feminine to match *fortūna*: *saeva es*. Prose keeps the predicate in the nominative even when the subject is the person addressed.

**Do not read *ō* as always a vocative marker.** *Ō* also introduces exclamations about something, in the accusative: *ō fortūnātam rem pūblicam!*, "O fortunate state!" (ag1903 §397). Check the ending.

**Do not mistake a vocative plural for a subject.** *Amīcī, sociī estis*: both words end in *-ī*. The commas, the second-person verb and the sense show that *amīcī* addresses and *sociī* is the predicate.

## Connections

Korean has a true vocative ending: 민수야 "Minsu!", with -아/-야 on the name, used to friends and juniors. Arabic calls with the particle *yā*, which, like Latin *ō*, comes before the name. Spanish and French lost the vocative ending; they mark address by intonation and commas alone.

## Exercises

1. Call *Quīntus*, *Titus* and *Lūcius*.
2. Say "friend, you are sick" to a man, and "friend, you are sick" to a woman (*amīca*).
3. Take apart *Mī Mārce, vīcīnus fīlium tuum salūtat* and translate it.
4. Give the vocative singular of *nauta*, *ager*, *dominus*, *fīlius* and *magister*.
5. Correct *\*Mārcus, cibum parat?* so that it asks Marcus whether he is preparing food.
6. Give the genitive and ablative plural of *dea* and *deus*.
7. Decline *liber, librī* "book" in the singular, all six cases.
8. In *Puerī, puerī labōrant*, how can a reader tell which word is vocative? Translate.

## Worked answers

1. All three are second declension in *-us* or *-ius*. *Quīnte*, *Tite*: replace *-us* with *-e*. *Lūcī*: *-ius* contracts to *-ī*.
2. To a man: *Amīce, aeger es*. The vocative is *amīce*; the predicate adjective is nominative masculine, *aeger*. To a woman: *Amīca, aegra es*. The first declension's vocative is *amīca*; the adjective is feminine, *aegra*.
3. *Mī Mārc-e*: my Marcus, vocative. *Vīcīn-us*: the neighbour, nominative, subject. *Fīli-um tu-um*: your son, object. *Salūta-t*: greets. "My dear Marcus, the neighbour is greeting your son."
4. *Nauta* (first declension); *ager* (no *-us*); *domine*; *fīlī*; *magister*.
5. Address Marcus with the vocative, make the verb second person, and add *-ne*: *Mārce, cibumne parās?* or *Mārce, parāsne cibum?*
6. *Dea*: genitive plural *deārum*, ablative plural *deābus*. *Deus*: genitive plural *deōrum* (or *deum* in verse), ablative plural *dīs*, *diīs* or *deīs*.
7. *Liber* drops its *e*, like *ager*: nominative *liber*, vocative *liber*, accusative *librum*, genitive *librī*, dative *librō*, ablative *librō*.
8. The first *puerī* is followed by a comma and the verb is third person, so the speaker is talking to some boys about other boys. "Boys, the boys are working." With a second-person verb the speaker would be telling the boys about themselves: *Puerī, labōrātis*, "boys, you are working".
