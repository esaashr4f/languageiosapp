## The idea

The third declension is the largest family of Latin nouns, and the one where the dictionary entry matters most. You have met many of its members already: *rēx, rēgis*; *pater, patris*; *homō, hominis*; *corpus, corporis*; *nōmen, nōminis*. In each, the nominative looks unlike the genitive.

The reason lies in the stem. In the first two declensions the stem ends in a vowel, *ā* or *o*. In this half of the third declension it ends in a consonant: *rēg-*, *patr-*, *homin-*, *corpor-*. The endings are added straight onto that consonant (ag1903 §54).

In every case but one, the ending begins with a vowel: *rēg-is*, *rēg-ī*, *rēg-em*, *rēg-e*. A vowel after the consonant leaves it alone, so the stem shows plainly. The nominative singular is the exception. There the stem stands at the end of the word, either bare or with *-s* added (ag1903 §§56, 61). Consonants at the end of a word, or crowded against *s*, were exposed to sound changes, and the nominative was reshaped by them. Allen and Greenough put it plainly: the nominative is always derived from the stem, by a few regular modifications (ag1903 §55).

So the nominative is the odd one out, and the genitive is the honest form. That is why the dictionary prints the full genitive for every third-declension noun, and why you build every other case on it.

This lesson covers the consonant stems. The next covers the i-stems, which share almost all the same endings but differ in four.

## How it is built

The endings, added to the stem:

- singular: accusative *-em*, genitive *-is*, dative *-ī*, ablative *-e*;
- plural, masculine and feminine: nominative, vocative and accusative *-ēs*; genitive *-um*; dative and ablative *-ibus*;
- neuters: nominative, vocative and accusative singular are the bare nominative; nominative, vocative and accusative plural *-a* (ag1903 §38). The other cases are as for masculines and feminines.

The vocative is the nominative, as in nearly all nouns.

The nominative singular is made from the stem in one of these ways (ag1903 §§55-56, 61, 63):

1. **Stop plus *s*.** Masculines and feminines whose stem ends in a stop add *-s*. *C* or *g* with *s* is written *x*: *rēg-s* is *rēx*, *duc-s* is *dux*, *pāc-s* is *pāx*. A *t* or *d* drops before the *s*: *mīlit-s* is *mīles*, *custōd-s* is *custōs*, *ped-s* is *pēs*, *virtūt-s* is *virtūs*.
2. **No *-s* after *l*, *r* or *n*.** Stems ending in these add nothing: *labor*, *soror*, *honor*, *cōnsul*. Stems in *-ōn-* also drop the *n*: *legiōn-* gives *legiō*, *sermōn-* gives *sermō*. Stems in *-din-* and *-gin-* drop the *n* and end in *-ō*: *virgin-* gives *virgō*, *ōrdin-* gives *ōrdō* (ag1903 §61). *Homō, hominis* belongs here too. Stems in *-tr-* show *-ter*: *patr-* gives *pater*, *frātr-* *frāter*.
3. **Neuters show the bare stem**, with two adjustments. Stems in *-in-* have *-en*: *nōmin-* gives *nōmen*, *flūmin-* *flūmen*, *carmin-* *carmen* (ag1903 §63). Most stems in *-er-* and *-or-* have *-us*: *gener-* gives *genus*, *corpor-* *corpus*, *oper-* *opus*.

That last group has the most interesting history. Allen and Greenough explain that these were once *s*-stems (ag1903 §63). An old *s* between two vowels became *r* in Latin, a change called **rhotacism**: the turning of *s* into *r* (ag1903 §15). So *genus* kept its *s* at the end of the word, where no vowel followed, while the genitive *\*genes-is* became *generis*. Wiktionary reconstructs the older form as *\*genesis* on the same grounds. The same change gives *corpus, corporis*, *opus, operis*, *tempus, temporis*. In *honor* the analogy ran the other way: the older nominative was *honōs*, rebuilt on *honōris* (ag1903 §15).

The vowel often differs too: *mīles* but *mīlit-*, *nōmen* but *nōmin-*. Allen and Greenough put this down partly to sound laws acting on weakly stressed syllables, partly to analogy (ag1903 §61).

To decline *mīles, mīlitis* m. "soldier":

1. The genitive *mīlit-is* gives the stem *mīlit-*.
2. Masculine, so the plural nominative and accusative take *-ēs*.
3. Singular: *mīles*, *mīles*, *mīlitem*, *mīlitis*, *mīlitī*, *mīlite*. Plural: *mīlitēs*, *mīlitēs*, *mīlitēs*, *mīlitum*, *mīlitibus*, *mīlitibus*.

## Where it applies

- **Every noun whose genitive singular ends in *-is*** is third declension (lesson 1 of this part). The consonant stems are those whose genitive plural is *-um*: *rēgum*, *mīlitum*, *nōminum*. The dictionary does not always print the genitive plural, so the next lesson gives the signs that a noun is an i-stem instead.
- **Masculine and feminine consonant stems** of every kind: in stops (*rēx*, *dux*, *mīles*, *pēs*, *virtūs*, *cīvitās*), in liquids (*pater*, *soror*, *labor*, *cōnsul*) and in nasals (*homō*, *legiō*, *virgō*).
- **Neuter consonant stems**: in *-men* (*nōmen*, *flūmen*, *carmen*), in *-us* with *-eris* or *-oris* (*genus*, *opus*, *scelus*, *vulnus*; *corpus*, *tempus*, *lītus*), and a few others: *caput, capitis*; *iter, itineris*, irregular.
- **Third-declension adjectives** use the same endings, with a few i-stem forms (part 5).
- **Words for people by their role** are often third declension: *dux*, *comes*, *custōs*, *eques*, *mīles*, *imperātor*. Many of them are masculine or feminine according to the person meant: Wiktionary gives *dux*, *comes* and *custōs* as either.
- **Gender**, where meaning does not decide, follows the ending of the nominative in most nouns (ag1903 §§84-87):
  - masculine: *-or*, *-ōs*, *-er*, *-es* with genitive *-itis*: *labor*, *honor*, *mōs*, *pater*, *mīles*;
  - feminine: *-ō* (especially *-iō*, *-dō*, *-gō*), *-ās*, *-ūs*, *-x*: *legiō*, *ōrātiō*, *virgō*, *cīvitās*, *virtūs*, *lēx*, *pāx*;
  - neuter: *-men* and *-us*: *nōmen*, *corpus*; also *caput*.
  - Common exceptions: *arbor* f.; *sermō*, *ōrdō* m.; *pēs* m. Allen and Greenough advise learning each gender from the dictionary (ag1903 §84).

## Watch out

**Do not build on the nominative.** *\*Mīlesem*, *\*mīlesis*, *\*virgōnem* add endings to the wrong form. The genitive gives the stem: *mīlitem*, *mīlitis*, *virginem*.

**Do not give third-declension nouns second-declension plurals.** *\*Mīlitī pugnant* and *\*ducōs laudant* borrow from *servus*. The plural is *-ēs*: *Mīlitēs pugnant*, *ducēs laudant*.

**Do not give neuters *-ēs*.** *\*Corporēs*, *\*nōminēs*: neuter plurals end in *-a* in the nominative and accusative: *corpora*, *nōmina*.

**Do not take every *-us* as masculine.** *\*Corpus magnus* follows the *-us* of *servus*. *Corpus* is a neuter of the third declension: *corpus magnum*. The genitive *corporis* gives it away.

**Do not read *-ēs* as one case.** *Mīlitēs ducēs laudant* has two plural nouns in *-ēs* and a plural verb. Either could be the subject. Latin relies on context and on the neutral order, subject first: "the soldiers praise the leaders". A writer who wants no doubt rephrases.

**Do not confuse the dative *-ī* with the second-declension genitive.** *Rēgī* is "to the king"; *servī* is "of the slave". Check the declension first.

**Do not give the ablative a long vowel.** *\*Rēgō* and *\*rēgē* copy the first two declensions. The third-declension ablative singular is short *-e*: *rēge*.

**Do not add *-i-* to the genitive plural of consonant stems.** *\*Mīlitium*, *\*rēgium*: the genitive plural is *-um*: *mīlitum*, *rēgum*. The *-ium* belongs to the i-stems of the next lesson. Some nouns in *-tās* have both: *cīvitātum* and *cīvitātium* (Wiktionary).

**Do not expect Spanish or French gender to match.** *Honor* and *labor* are masculine in Latin and Spanish, but Spanish *la flor* and French *la fleur* are feminine while Latin *flōs* is masculine (ag1903 §85). Check the Latin.

## Connections

Spanish nouns continue the Latin accusative, so they keep the stem the nominative hid: *ciudad* from *cīvitātem*, *rey* from *rēgem* (Wiktionary). French *-té* likewise continues the accusative *-tātem* (Wiktionary), so Spanish *-dad* and French *-té* both answer to Latin *-tās*, *-tātis*. Korean has its own sound changes at the edge of a word: the final consonant of a noun is pronounced differently alone than before a vowel, much as *rēx* hides the *g* that *rēgis* shows.

## Exercises

1. From *dux, ducis* m., give the accusative singular and the nominative plural.
2. Decline *pēs, pedis* m. in the plural.
3. Take apart *Custōdēs ante portam cum mīlitibus stant* and translate it.
4. Say "the leader praises the soldiers' courage".
5. Why is the genitive of *genus* *generis* and not *\*genesis*?
6. Correct *\*Flūminēs magnī sunt*.
7. Translate *Ōrātiō ducis legiōnibus placet*.
8. Sort by likely gender, then check with the vocabulary: *ōrdō*, *legiō*, *labor*, *caput*, *lūx*.

## Worked answers

1. The stem is *duc-*. Accusative singular *ducem*; nominative plural *ducēs*.
2. Stem *ped-*, masculine: *pedēs*, *pedēs*, *pedēs*, *pedum*, *pedibus*, *pedibus*.
3. *Custōd-ēs*: guards, nominative plural, subject. *Ante port-am*: in front of the gate. *Cum mīlit-ibus*: with the soldiers, ablative. *Sta-nt*: they stand. "The guards are standing in front of the gate with the soldiers."
4. *Dux* is the subject. "Courage" is the object: *virtūtem*. "Of the soldiers": genitive plural *mīlitum*. *Dux virtūtem mīlitum laudat*.
5. The stem was once *genes-*. In the nominative the *s* ended the word and stayed. In the genitive it stood between two vowels, and Latin turned *s* into *r* there: *generis*.
6. *Flūmen* is neuter, so its plural is *flūmina*, and the adjective is neuter plural: *Flūmina magna sunt*.
7. *Ōrātiō*: the speech, subject. *Duc-is*: of the leader. *Legiōn-ibus*: to the legions, dative with *placet*. "The legions like the general's speech."
8. By ending: *ōrdō* and *legiō* in *-ō*, feminine; *labor* in *-or*, masculine; *caput* in *-ut*, neuter; *lūx* in *-x*, feminine. The vocabulary confirms all but *ōrdō*, which is an exception: masculine.
