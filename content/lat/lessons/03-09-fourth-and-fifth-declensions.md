## The idea

The last two declensions are small, but their nouns are common. *Manus* "hand", *senātus* "senate", *exercitus* "army", *domus* "house" belong to the fourth; *rēs* "thing" and *diēs* "day", two of the most frequent nouns in Latin, to the fifth.

Each is defined by the vowel its stem ends in, as lesson 1 of this part said (ag1903 §37).

- The **fourth declension** has stems in *u*: *manu-*, *senātu-*. Masculines and feminines add *-s* in the nominative: *manus*. Neuters show the bare stem with a long *ū*: *cornū* "horn" (ag1903 §88).
- The **fifth declension** has stems in *ē*: *rē-*, *diē-*. The nominative adds *-s*: *rēs*, *diēs*. The *ē* appears in every case (ag1903 §95).

Seen this way, the two declensions are no harder than the second. *Servus* is an *o*-stem with *-s*; *manus* is a *u*-stem with *-s*; *rēs* an *ē*-stem with *-s*. The endings follow the same principles you already know: accusative singular in *-m*, accusative plural in *-s*, genitive plural in *-um*, dative and ablative plural alike (ag1903 §38).

The fourth declension's endings mostly keep the *u* visible: genitive *manūs*, dative *manuī*, accusative *manum*, ablative *manū*. The long *ū* of the genitive comes from contraction: Allen and Greenough record old genitives in *-uis* and *-uos*, such as *senātuis* (ag1903 §92). Before *-bus* the *u* usually weakened to *i*: *manibus* (ag1903 §88).

Many fourth-declension nouns are action nouns made from verbs with the suffix *-tus* or *-sus* (ag1903 §94). *Cāsus* "fall" comes from *cadō* "fall", *cursus* "running, course" from *currō* "run", *sēnsus* "feeling" from *sentiō* "feel". That is why so many of them name an act or a process.

## How it is built

**Fourth declension.** The endings, added to the stem without its *u*:

- singular: *-us*, *-us*, *-um*, *-ūs*, *-uī*, *-ū*;
- plural: *-ūs*, *-ūs*, *-ūs*, *-uum*, *-ibus*, *-ibus*;
- neuters: singular *-ū* in nominative, vocative, accusative, dative and ablative, genitive *-ūs*; plural *-ua*, *-uum*, *-ibus* (Wiktionary, cornū).

Most fourth-declension nouns in *-us* are masculine. The feminines are few: *manus*, *domus*, *anus* "old woman", and a handful of others. The only neuters in common use are *cornū* and *genū* "knee" (ag1903 §§90-91).

**Fifth declension.** The endings, added to the stem without its *ē*:

- singular: *-ēs*, *-ēs*, *-em*, *-ēī* or *-eī*, *-ēī* or *-eī*, *-ē*;
- plural: *-ēs*, *-ēs*, *-ēs*, *-ērum*, *-ēbus*, *-ēbus*.

The *ē* is shortened in two places. Before *-m*, as always: *rem*, *diem* (ag1903 §96). And in the genitive and dative singular after a consonant: *reī*, *speī*, *fideī*, but *diēī*, *aciēī* after a vowel (ag1903 §96). Allen and Greenough note that early Latin still had the long vowel in *rēī* and the rest.

All fifth-declension nouns are feminine except *diēs*, usually masculine, and *merīdiēs* "midday", masculine (ag1903 §97). Only *rēs* and *diēs* have a full plural. The others have none, or only the nominative and accusative plural: *spēs*, *aciēs*, *faciēs*, *speciēs* (ag1903 §98).

To decline *exercitus, -ūs* m. "army":

1. The genitive *exercitūs* shows the fourth declension. The stem is *exercitu-*.
2. Singular: *exercitus*, *exercitus*, *exercitum*, *exercitūs*, *exercituī*, *exercitū*.
3. Plural: *exercitūs*, *exercitūs*, *exercitūs*, *exercituum*, *exercitibus*, *exercitibus*.

Notice that *exercitūs* is four forms: genitive singular and nominative, vocative and accusative plural. Only the vowel length and the sentence tell them apart.

## Where it applies

**The fourth declension**

- **Masculine nouns in *-us*, genitive *-ūs***: *senātus*, *exercitus*, *metus*, *cursus*, *impetus*, *ūsus*, *portus*, *gradus*, *spīritus*, *sēnsus*, *flūctus*, *currus*, *vultus*.
- **A few feminines**: *manus*, *domus*, *anus* (ag1903 §90).
- **The neuters *cornū* and *genū*** (ag1903 §91).
- **Action nouns from verbs**, in *-tus* and *-sus* (ag1903 §94). Their accusative and ablative forms also make the supine of part 7.
- **Defective nouns**: some occur in only one or two cases. Allen and Greenough list *impetus* among nouns found only in the nominative, accusative and ablative singular (ag1903 §103), though Wiktionary's table gives the full set. *Iussū* "by order" exists only in the ablative (ag1903 §94).
- **Mixed forms.** *Domus* takes second-declension forms beside fourth: ablative *domō*, accusative plural *domōs*; and the locative *domī* "at home" (ag1903 §93), lesson 13 of this part. *Senātus* sometimes has a genitive *senātī* (ag1903 §92).
- **Dative and ablative plural in *-ubus*** in *lacus*, *portus* (beside *-ibus*) and a few others (ag1903 §92).

**The fifth declension**

- ***Rēs* and *diēs***, the only nouns declined throughout (ag1903 §98).
- **Feminine abstract nouns with no plural, or a partial one**: *spēs*, *fidēs*, *aciēs*, *faciēs* "face, appearance", *speciēs* "appearance" (ag1903 §98).
- ***Rēs* in set phrases**, where it takes its meaning from an adjective: *rēs pūblica* "the state", *rēs mīlitāris* "warfare", *rēs gestae* "deeds" (part 4).
- **Time words**: *diēs*, *merīdiēs*, and the adverb *hodiē* "today", which Allen and Greenough explain as an old locative of *diēs* (ag1903 §98).
- **Gender of *diēs*.** Masculine in general; feminine for a fixed date, and for time in general: *longa diēs* "a long time" (ag1903 §97).
- **Overlap with other declensions.** Some nouns have forms of both the first and fifth: *māteria* and *māteriēs* "material" (ag1903 §98).

## Watch out

**Do not decline fourth-declension nouns like *servus*.** *\*Exercitī*, *\*exercitō*, *\*exercitōs*: the genitive *exercitūs* in the entry shows the fourth declension. *Exercitūs*, *exercituī*, *exercitū*, *exercitūs*.

**Do not read *-ūs* as one case.** *Exercitūs* can be genitive singular or nominative or accusative plural. In a text without macrons, *exercitus* (nominative singular) looks the same too. Check the verb's number and the noun the form depends on.

**Do not make *manus* and *domus* masculine.** *\*Manus dexter*, *\*domus magnus* follow the *-us* ending. Both are feminine: *manus dextra*, *domus magna*.

**Do not lengthen the *e* of *rem*, *spem*, *fidem*.** *\*Rēm*, *\*spēm*: a vowel is short before final *-m* (ag1903 §96). The same goes for *reī*, *speī*, *fideī* in the genitive and dative.

**Do not make *rēs* masculine because *diēs* is.** *\*Rēs pūblicus* is wrong. *Rēs* is feminine: *rēs pūblica*.

**Do not form plurals that do not exist.** *\*Spērum*, *\*fidērum*: most fifth-declension nouns have no genitive plural in classical Latin (ag1903 §98). Say *multae spēs* only in the nominative and accusative; otherwise rephrase.

**Do not translate *rēs* as "thing" every time.** *Rēs* takes its sense from context: "matter", "affair", "property", "event", "fact". *Rēs pūblica* is "the state", not "the public thing". The Romance words for "thing", Spanish *cosa* and French *chose*, come from a different Latin word, *causa* (Wiktionary).

**Do not confuse *cornū* with the second-declension neuters.** *\*Cornum*, *\*corna*: the forms are *cornū*, plural *cornua*.

## Connections

Spanish *mano* and French *main* are still feminine, a direct inheritance from *manus*. Spanish *día* is masculine like *diēs*, though it ends in *-a*. French *rien* "nothing" is the accusative *rem* (lesson 1 of this part), while the everyday word for "thing", Spanish *cosa*, French *chose*, comes from *causa* (Wiktionary).

## Exercises

1. Decline *portus, -ūs* m. in the singular.
2. Give the genitive singular and the nominative plural of *rēs* and *diēs*.
3. Take apart *Impetus equitum hostēs terret* and translate it.
4. Translate *Cursus flūminis longus est*.
5. Say "the consul saves the state".
6. Correct *\*Manus meus fessus est*.
7. In *Metus exercitūs magnus est* and *Exercitūs ad portum properant*, why is *exercitūs* singular in one and plural in the other?
8. Say "the soldiers have hope", using *spēs* and *habeō*.

## Worked answers

1. Stem *portu-*: *portus*, *portus*, *portum*, *portūs*, *portuī*, *portū*.
2. *Rēs*: genitive *reī*, plural *rēs*. *Diēs*: genitive *diēī*, plural *diēs*. The *e* of *reī* is short after a consonant; *diēī* keeps the long *ē* after a vowel.
3. *Impet-us*: the attack, nominative singular, subject. *Equit-um*: of the cavalry, genitive plural. *Host-ēs*: the enemy, accusative plural. *Terre-t*: frightens, singular to agree with *impetus*. "The cavalry's attack frightens the enemy."
4. *Curs-us*: the course, nominative. *Flūmin-is*: of the river. "The river's course is long."
5. *Cōnsul* is the subject; *rēs pūblica* is the object, both words in the accusative: *Cōnsul rem pūblicam servat*.
6. *Manus* is feminine, so both adjectives are feminine: *Manus mea fessa est*, "my hand is tired".
7. In the first, *exercitūs* follows *metus*, and the verb *est* is singular: *exercitūs* is the genitive singular, "of the army". In the second, the verb *properant* is plural and *exercitūs* is its subject: nominative plural.
8. *Mīlitēs spem habent*. *Spem* is the accusative singular, with a short *e*.
