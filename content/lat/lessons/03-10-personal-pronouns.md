## The idea

A **pronoun** is a word that stands in for a noun: "I", "you", "him", "ourselves". Latin has personal pronouns for the first and second persons only: *ego* "I", *tū* "you" (one person), *nōs* "we", *vōs* "you" (more than one) (ag1903 §142). For "he", "she" and "they" it borrows a pointing word, taught in the next lesson.

You have used these pronouns without seeing them. *Labōrō* already says "I work": the verb ending names the subject (part 2, lesson 1). So the nominatives *ego*, *tū*, *nōs*, *vōs* are not needed as subjects. Latin uses them only to stress or contrast the person: *Ego labōrō, tū rīdēs*, "*I* am working; *you* are laughing" (ag1903 §295).

The other cases are a different matter. A verb ending can only name the subject. To say "he calls *me*", "I give *you* money", "*with us*", Latin needs the pronoun in the accusative, dative or ablative. Those are the forms you will meet most.

They do not look like the nominative. "I" is *ego*, but "me" is *mē*. Wiktionary explains that this split is inherited: Indo-European already used one root for "I" as subject and another for "me" in the other cases. English shows the same split in "I" and "me".

The second part of this lesson is the **reflexive**: a pronoun that refers back to the subject of its own sentence, like English "myself" or "themselves" (ag1903 §144). In the first and second persons Latin needs no special form: *mē videō* is "I see myself", because "me" can only be the speaker. In the third person it does need one. *Videt eum* would be "he sees him", someone else. So Latin has a third-person reflexive, *sē*, "himself, herself, itself, themselves", and its adjective *suus*, "his own, her own, their own" (ag1903 §§144, 299).

## How it is built

The pronouns decline. Their cases follow the order you know; they have no vocative of their own except *tū* and *vōs*, used to call someone.

- *ego* "I": accusative *mē*, genitive *meī*, dative *mihi*, ablative *mē*.
- *tū* "you": accusative *tē*, genitive *tuī*, dative *tibi*, ablative *tē*.
- *nōs* "we": accusative *nōs*, genitive *nostrum* or *nostrī*, dative and ablative *nōbīs*.
- *vōs* "you": accusative *vōs*, genitive *vestrum* or *vestrī*, dative and ablative *vōbīs*.
- *sē* "himself, herself, itself, themselves": no nominative; accusative *sē*, genitive *suī*, dative *sibi*, ablative *sē*. The same forms serve for singular and plural (ag1903 §144).

The final vowel of *ego*, *mihi*, *tibi* and *sibi* is printed short here; poets treat it as either long or short (ag1903 §604).

Three details:

1. **The genitives are not for possession.** *Meī*, *tuī*, *nostrī* and the rest are really genitives of the possessive adjectives (ag1903 §143). They are used for the object of a feeling: *amor tuī*, "love for you". The forms *nostrum* and *vestrum* are used for "of us" as a group: *pars nostrum*, "some of us" (ag1903 §295). For "my", "your", "our", use the adjectives *meus*, *tuus*, *noster*, *vester* and, for the reflexive, *suus* (ag1903 §302).
2. ***Cum* follows the pronoun** and is written joined to it: *mēcum* "with me", *tēcum*, *nōbīscum*, *vōbīscum*, *sēcum* (ag1903 §§143-144).
3. ***Suus* agrees with the thing owned**, like *meus* (part 2, lesson 7). Only its reference goes back to the subject: *Fīlia patrem suum amat*, "the daughter loves her father", with *suum* masculine to match *patrem*.

To say "the boy washes himself":

1. The subject is *puer*, third person.
2. The object is the subject himself. Third person, so the reflexive: *sē*.
3. *Puer sē lavat*. Compare *Puer eum lavat*, "the boy washes him", another person; *eum* is from the next lesson.

In glosses, the pronouns are glossed by person, number and case: *mē* is `1SG.ACC`. The reflexive is `REFL`, with its case. *Suus* is glossed `REFL.own`, with its ending.

## Where it applies

**Personal pronouns**

- **As subject, for emphasis or contrast only**: *Ego labōrō, tū rīdēs* (ag1903 §295). In Jesus' words *Ego sum via et vēritās et vīta*, "I am the way and the truth and the life" (Vulgate, John 14:6), *ego* is emphatic.
- **As object, receiver, or after prepositions**: *mē vocat*, *tibi dō*, *ā mē*, *ad nōs*.
- **With *cum***, joined after: *mēcum*, *tēcum*, *nōbīscum*, *vōbīscum* (ag1903 §143).
- **"Of us", "of you" as a group**: *nostrum*, *vestrum*: *pars nostrum*, *nēmō vestrum* "none of you" (ag1903 §295).
- **The object of a feeling**: *meī*, *tuī*, *nostrī*, *vestrī*: *amor tuī*, "love for you"; *memor nostrī*, "mindful of us" (ag1903 §143).
- **"We" for "I"**: a writer may say *nōs* of himself, as English authors write "we". *Vōs* is never used for one person (ag1903 §143).
- **Emphatic forms** with *-met*: *egomet* "I myself", *vōsmet*; and doubled *mēmē*, *tētē* (ag1903 §143).

**The reflexive**

- **Referring to the subject of its own sentence**, in any case but the nominative: *sē lavat*, *sibi parant*, *sēcum* "with himself" (ag1903 §§144, 299).
- **The possessive *suus*** for "his own, her own, its own, their own", when the owner is the subject: *uxōrem suam amat* (ag1903 §299). Used as a noun, *suī* means "his men, their people": *suōs vocat*, "he calls his men" (ag1903 §302).
- **In longer sentences**, *sē* and *suus* can refer to the subject of the main verb from inside a reported statement or request (ag1903 §300). Part 6 shows this.
- **The first and second persons** use the ordinary pronouns and possessives as reflexives: *mē laudō*, *tē lavās*, *nōs exercēmus* "we train ourselves" (ag1903 §144).

## Watch out

**Do not add a subject pronoun to every verb.** English and French need *I*, *je*. *\*Ego labōrō* in a plain statement sounds emphatic, "*I* am working". Say *Labōrō*, as Spanish says *trabajo*.

**Do not use the genitive of a pronoun for "my".** *\*Liber meī* is not "my book" (ag1903 §302). Use the adjective: *liber meus*. The same for *\*domus nostrī*: *domus nostra*.

**Do not mix the two genitive plurals.** *\*Pars nostrī* for "some of us" uses the form for feelings. A part of a group takes *nostrum*: *pars nostrum*. *Amor nostrī*, "love for us", takes *nostrī*.

**Do not put *cum* first with these pronouns.** *\*Cum mē ambulat* is not classical. Say *Mēcum ambulat* (ag1903 §143).

**Do not use *vōs* to address one person politely.** French *vous* and Spanish *vosotros* or *usted* habits tempt *\*Vōs, magister, bene docētis* to a single teacher. Latin has no polite plural. One person is *tū*: *Tū, magister, bene docēs*.

**Do not use *sē* or *suus* for someone other than the subject.** *\*Marītus uxōrem suam amat* is right only if it is his own wife. To say "the husband loves his (another man's) wife", Latin uses a genitive pronoun, *eius*, taught in the next lesson: *Marītus uxōrem eius amat*.

**Do not make *suus* agree with the owner.** *\*Puella frātrem suam amat* copies the girl's gender. *Suus*, like *meus*, agrees with the thing owned: *frātrem suum*.

**Do not use *sē* as a subject.** *\*Sē labōrat* for "he himself works" has no meaning: *sē* has no nominative. For "he himself", Latin uses *ipse* (lesson 12 of this part).

**Do not use *sē* in the first or second person.** *\*Sē laudō* means nothing. "I praise myself" is *Mē laudō*.

## Connections

Spanish and French continue these forms: Spanish *me*, *te*, *se*, *nos*; French *me*, *te*, *se*, *nous*, *vous*. Spanish *conmigo* comes from *cum* plus *mēcum* (Wiktionary), so the preposition appears twice. Japanese and Korean, like Latin, leave out "I" and "you" unless they are needed, and Japanese 自分 (jibun) is a reflexive that, like *sē*, points back to the subject.

## Exercises

1. Say "the teacher is praising us".
2. Say "I am walking with you" (to one person), then to several people.
3. Take apart *Fīliī sē exercent* and translate it.
4. Translate *Nēmō nostrum tē accūsat*.
5. Say "the soldier shows the leader his (own) wound".
6. Correct *\*Liber meī novus est*.
7. In *Caesar Dumnorīgem ad sē vocat*, who is *sē*, and why can it not be Dumnorix?
8. Correct *\*Vōbīs fābulam narrō, nōn sibi*, meant as "I am telling the story to you, not to myself".

## Worked answers

1. The subject is *magister*. "Us" is the object: *nōs*. *Magister nōs laudat*.
2. *Tēcum ambulō*. To several: *Vōbīscum ambulō*. *Cum* is attached after the ablative.
3. *Fīli-ī*: the sons, subject. *Sē*: themselves, reflexive accusative, referring back to the sons. *Exerce-nt*: they train. "The sons are training themselves."
4. *Nēmō nostrum*: none of us, with the group genitive *nostrum*. *Tē*: you, object. *Accūsa-t*: accuses. "None of us is accusing you."
5. *Mīles* is the subject. The leader receives: dative *ducī*. The wound is the object: *vulnus*, neuter. "His own" refers to the subject: *suum*, neuter accusative singular to agree with *vulnus*. *Mīles ducī vulnus suum mōnstrat*.
6. Possession takes the adjective, not the genitive of *ego*: *Liber meus novus est*.
7. *Sē* is Caesar. The reflexive always points back to the subject of its own sentence, and Dumnorix is the object. For any other person Latin uses a form of *is*, from the next lesson.
8. The subject is "I", first person, and *sibi* is the third-person reflexive. In the first person the ordinary pronoun is the reflexive: *Vōbīs fābulam narrō, nōn mihi*.
