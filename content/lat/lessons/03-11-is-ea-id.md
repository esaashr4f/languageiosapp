## The idea

The last lesson gave you "I", "you", "we" and the reflexive "self". It left a gap: Latin has no personal pronoun for "he", "she", "it" and "they" (ag1903 §295). It fills the gap with a pointing word, *is, ea, id*.

A pointing word, or **demonstrative**, picks out a particular person or thing: "this", "that" (ag1903 §146). *Is* is the weakest of them. Allen and Greenough say it does not point at anything in view; it refers to someone just mentioned, or about to be explained (ag1903 §297). That is exactly the job of English "he" or "she" in a story: *Mārcum videō; eum vocō*, "I see Marcus; I call him". So *is* serves as the third-person pronoun.

It does a second job as an adjective, beside a noun: *ea fābula*, "that story", the one just mentioned. English uses "that" or "this" for it, or often just "the".

Because *is* is a demonstrative and not a personal pronoun, it behaves like an adjective. It has three genders and agrees with the noun it refers to. *Ea* is "she" for a woman, but also "it" for any feminine noun: *silvam videō; ea magna est*, "I see the forest; it is big". Latin "it" can be *is*, *ea* or *id*, depending on the noun's gender, just as Spanish says *lo* or *la*. Its case, like any noun's, comes from its job in its own sentence.

The genitive of *is* gives Latin its non-reflexive "his", "her" and "their": *eius*, "of him, of her", and *eōrum*, *eārum*, "of them". Allen and Greenough set the two side by side (ag1903 §296). *Caesar et exercitus eius*: "Caesar and his army". But *Caesar exercitum suum laudat*: "Caesar praises his own army", where the owner is the subject.

## How it is built

*Is* follows the first and second declensions in most forms, with two endings of its own in the singular: a genitive in *-ius* and a dative in *-ī*, the same for all three genders. These two belong to what grammars call the pronominal declension, shared with *ille*, *ipse* and a small group of adjectives in part 5 (ag1903 §§113, 146).

- Singular: *is*, *ea*, *id*; accusative *eum*, *eam*, *id*; genitive *eius* for all genders; dative *eī* for all genders; ablative *eō*, *eā*, *eō*.
- Plural: *eī* or *iī*, *eae*, *ea*; accusative *eōs*, *eās*, *ea*; genitive *eōrum*, *eārum*, *eōrum*; dative and ablative *eīs* or *iīs* (Wiktionary gives also *īs*).

*Eius* is pronounced with a doubled consonantal *i*, [ˈɛjjʊs], like *maior* in part 1, so its first syllable is heavy.

To replace a noun with *is*, take three steps.

1. Find the noun it stands for, and take that noun's gender and number. "The girls": feminine plural.
2. Decide the job of the pronoun in its own sentence. "I call them": object, so accusative.
3. Combine: feminine plural accusative, *eās*. *Puellae rīdent; eās vocō*, "the girls are laughing; I call them".

To say "his" or "her":

1. Ask whether the owner is the subject of the sentence.
2. If yes, use *suus*, agreeing with the thing owned (last lesson).
3. If no, use the genitive *eius* (one owner) or *eōrum*, *eārum* (several). It does not change to match the thing owned: *fīlius eius*, *fīlia eius*, *fīliī eius*.

## Where it applies

- **"Him", "her", "it", "them" in any case but the nominative**: *eum vocō*, *eī dō*, *cum eīs*. This is the commonest use (ag1903 §296).
- **"He", "she", "it", "they" as subject**, only when the subject needs to be named: to switch the reader's attention to someone just mentioned, or for contrast. Allen and Greenough quote Cicero: *vēnit mihi obviam tuus puer; is mihi litterās reddidit*, "your boy met me; he handed me a letter" (ag1903 §297).
- **"That", "the said", as an adjective**: *ea fābula*, *eō diē* "on that day" (lesson 13 of this part).
- **Non-reflexive possession**: *eius*, *eōrum*, *eārum*, when the owner is not the subject (ag1903 §296).
- **The whole of which a part is taken**: *nēmō eōrum*, *pars eārum*.
- **A whole idea**: neuter *id*, *ea*: *Id nōn putō* (ag1903 §297).
- **Pointing forward to a clause**: *is quī* "the man who", *id quod* "the thing that" (ag1903 §297). Part 6.
- **Adding a detail with emphasis**: *et is quidem*, "and he at that", *vincula, et ea sempiterna*, "chains, and those everlasting" (ag1903 §298).
- **A fixed phrase**: *eius modī*, "of that kind, such" (ag1903 §146).
- ***Īdem*, "the same"**, is *is* with the particle *-dem* (ag1903 §146). Next lesson.

## Watch out

**Do not add *is* as a subject every time.** *\*Is labōrat* for a plain "he is working" is emphatic: "*he* is working". The verb ending already says "he". Say *Labōrat*, as Spanish says *trabaja*.

**Do not use *eius* when the owner is the subject.** *\*Caesar exercitum eius laudat* means another man's army. For his own, *suum*.

**Do not use *suus* when the owner is not the subject.** *\*Caesar suam dextram prēndit* would mean Caesar takes his own right hand. For Diviciacus's hand: *eius dextram*.

**Do not make *eius* agree.** *Eius* is a genitive, not an adjective. It does not change for the thing owned: *fīlia eius*, *fīliī eius*. *\*Fīlia eia* is not Latin.

**Do not use *id* for every "it".** *\*Silvam spectō; id magna est* gives a feminine noun a neuter pronoun and a mismatched adjective. "It" follows the noun's gender: *ea magna est*. For *mūrus*: *is altus est*.

**Do not use *eae* for a masculine plural.** The plural "they" for men or a mixed group is *eī* (or *iī*): *\*Eae labōrant* for "the men are working" is wrong.

**Do not use *is* to point at something in view.** "*This* sword here" is not *\*is gladius*. *Is* points back in the text. For pointing at things present, Latin uses *hic* and *ille* (next lesson).

**Do not confuse *eī* the dative with *eī* the plural.** *Eī pecūniam dō* is "I give him money"; *Eī labōrant* is "they are working". The verb and the other nouns decide.

**Do not translate *eius* as "its" too readily.** It can be "its", but most often it refers to a person. Check what was mentioned last.

## Connections

Spanish and French third-person pronouns come from *ille*, the stronger demonstrative of the next lesson, not from *is*: Spanish *él* is from *ille*, and French *il* from its plural *illī* (Wiktionary). Japanese works in a similar way: 彼 "he" and 彼女 "she" are little used in ordinary speech, and speakers say その人 "that person" or use the name, a demonstrative in the place of a pronoun.

## Exercises

1. Replace the noun with a form of *is*: *Puellās videō* ("I see them").
2. Say "I am giving her a gift".
3. Take apart *Lēgātus adest; fīlium eius exspectāmus* and translate it.
4. Say "the mistress of the house praises her (own) daughter", with *domina*.
5. Say "the mistress of the house praises her (another woman's) daughter".
6. *Mūrum spectāmus; \*id altum est.* Correct and explain.
7. Translate *Eōrum fāma magna est*.
8. Why does Latin say *eam* in John 1:5, where English says "it"?

## Worked answers

1. *Puellās* is feminine plural accusative, so the pronoun is too: *Eās videō*.
2. "Her" is the receiver: dative singular *eī*, the same for all genders. *Eī dōnum dō*.
3. *Lēgāt-us*: the envoy, subject. *Adest*: is here. *Fīli-um*: the son, object. *Eius*: his, the envoy's. *Exspectā-mus*: we are waiting for. "The envoy is here; we are waiting for his son."
4. The owner is the subject, so *suus*, agreeing with *fīliam*: *Domina fīliam suam laudat*.
5. The owner is someone else, so *eius*: *Domina fīliam eius laudat*.
6. *Mūrus* is masculine, so "it" is *is*, and the adjective is masculine: *Mūrum spectāmus; is altus est*.
7. *Eōrum*: of them, genitive plural, standing for some men or a mixed group. *Fāma*: reputation. "Their reputation is great."
8. *Eam* refers to *lūx*, "light", which is feminine. Latin "it" takes the gender of the noun it stands for.
