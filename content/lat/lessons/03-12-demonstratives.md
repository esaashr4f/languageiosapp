## The idea

*Is* refers back to something already mentioned. It does not point. For pointing, Latin has three stronger demonstratives, and they divide space by person, like the three persons of the verb (ag1903 §297):

- *hic, haec, hoc* "this": near the speaker, "this here by me". Allen and Greenough call it the demonstrative of the first person.
- *iste, ista, istud* "that": near the person spoken to, "that by you". The demonstrative of the second person.
- *ille, illa, illud* "that": away from both, "that over there". The demonstrative of the third person.

The same division works in time and in thought. *Hic* is what is present or just said; *ille* is what is distant, past, or famous. Two further words complete the set. *Ipse* "self" adds emphasis: "the man himself", "that very thing". *Īdem* "the same" marks identity.

Like *is*, all five can stand beside a noun as adjectives (*hic liber* "this book") or alone as pronouns (*hic* "this man") (ag1903 §296). All agree with their noun in gender, number and case. Spanish and Japanese divide space in the same three ways: *este*, *ese*, *aquel*; これ, それ, あれ.

The forms explain themselves once you see how the words were built. Allen and Greenough explain that *hic* is an old stem *ho-* with a pointing particle *-ce*, "here", most of which wore away: *hunc* was once *hum-ce*, and the *-c* of *hic*, *haec*, *hoc*, *huic* is what is left of it (ag1903 §146). *Ipse* is *is* plus a particle *-pse*; *īdem* is *is* plus *-dem*, so *īdem* declines *is* and keeps *-dem* unchanged: *eundem*, *eōdem*, with *m* turned into *n* before *d* (ag1903 §146). *Ille* replaced an older *ollus* (ag1903 §146).

## How it is built

*Ille*, *iste* and *ipse* go like *bonus*, with three differences, the pronominal endings you met in *is* (ag1903 §§113, 146):

1. genitive singular *-īus* for all genders: *illīus*, *istīus*, *ipsīus*;
2. dative singular *-ī* for all genders: *illī*, *istī*, *ipsī*;
3. neuter nominative and accusative singular *-ud* for *ille* and *iste*: *illud*, *istud*. *Ipse* has *ipsum*.

The *ī* of *illīus* and the others is long in prose; poets may shorten it (Wiktionary marks it as either).

*Hic* is irregular because of the *-c*. Learn it from the table: *hic, haec, hoc*; accusative *hunc, hanc, hoc*; genitive *huius*; dative *huic*; ablative *hōc, hāc, hōc*; plural *hī, hae, haec*, *hōs, hās, haec*, *hōrum, hārum, hōrum*, *hīs*. *Huius* is said with a doubled consonantal *i*, like *eius*; *huic* is one syllable. The pronoun *hic* has a short *i*; the adverb *hīc* "here" from part 2 has a long one (Wiktionary).

*Īdem* is *is* with *-dem*: *īdem, eadem, idem*; *eundem, eandem, idem*; *eiusdem*; *eīdem*; *eōdem, eādem, eōdem*; plural *eīdem* or *īdem*, *eaedem*, *eadem*, and so on.

To choose the word:

1. Is the thing near me, or the one just spoken of? *Hic*.
2. Is it yours, near you, or (in a speech) my opponent's? *Iste*.
3. Is it far off, earlier, or well known? *Ille*.
4. Do I only need to refer back, with no pointing? *Is*.
5. Do I need "himself", "the very"? *Ipse*. "The same"? *Īdem*.

*Ipse* is not the reflexive. *Sē* is the object pointing back to the subject: *sē laudat* "he praises himself". *Ipse* stresses a noun or pronoun: *ipse labōrat* "he works himself", "he in person" (ag1903 §298).

## Where it applies

- ***Hic***: what is near the speaker in place, time or thought: *hic liber*, *haec* "these things", *hic* "this man", sometimes the speaker himself. Also "the latter" of two things just named (ag1903 §297).
- ***Ille***: what is far off, past or famous. After its noun it often means "that famous": *Mēdēa illa*, "the celebrated Medea". Also "the former" of two (ag1903 §297). Spanish *él* and French *il* come from it (lesson 11 of this part).
- ***Iste***: what is near or belongs to the listener: *ista cūra* "that worry of yours". In speeches it often points at the opponent, with contempt (ag1903 §297).
- **Pointing forward**: *hic*, *ille* and *is* can point at something about to be said: *illud*, "this (that follows)" (ag1903 §297).
- **Neuters for ideas**: *hoc*, *illud*, *haec* "this, these things" (ag1903 §297).
- ***Ipse***: "self, very, in person", with any noun or pronoun: *rēx ipse*, *tē ipsum*, *id ipsum* "that very thing"; alone, "the master himself" (ag1903 §298).
- ***Ipse* with a reflexive** agrees with the subject: *mē ipse laudō* (ag1903 §298).
- ***Īdem***: "the same": *eundem librum*. Where English says "also", "at the same time": *īdem* with a second description (ag1903 §298). "The same as" uses *īdem* with *atque* or a relative clause (ag1903 §384).
- **The pronominal endings** *-īus*, *-ī* also belong to nine adjectives such as *ūnus*, *sōlus*, *tōtus*, *nūllus*, *alius* (ag1903 §113). Part 5.
- **Adverbs of place** built on the same stems: *hīc* "here", *hūc* "to here", *hinc* "from here"; *illūc* "to there"; *inde* "from there".

## Watch out

**Do not use *is* to point.** *\*Is liber meus est* for "this book is mine" (with the book in your hand) is weak. Pointing needs *hic*: *Hic liber meus est*.

**Do not use *ipse* as the reflexive.** *\*Puer ipsum lavat* does not mean "the boy washes himself". English "himself" covers two Latin words. The object is *sē*; *ipse* adds stress: *Puer sē lavat*; *puer ipse lavat*, "the boy does the washing himself" (ag1903 §298).

**Do not give *ipse* the case of the object out of English habit.** "I praise *myself*" stresses the object, but Latin puts *ipse* with the subject: *Mē ipse laudō*, not usually *\*mē ipsum laudō* (ag1903 §298).

**Do not decline *hic* like *bonus*.** *\*Hī liber*, *\*hum*, *\*hōius*: the forms are *hic*, *hunc*, *huius*. The old *-c* is part of most forms.

**Do not confuse the pronoun *hic* with the adverb *hīc*.** *Hic labōrat* is "this man is working"; *Hīc labōrat* is "he is working here". In unmarked texts, context decides.

**Do not give *ille* and *iste* the neuter *-um*.** *\*Illum templum*, *\*istum*: the neuter is *illud*, *istud*: *illud templum*.

**Do not make the genitive and dative agree in gender.** *\*Illae* for "to that woman": the dative singular is *illī* for all genders, the genitive *illīus*.

**Do not use *īdem* for "himself".** Spanish *mismo* means both "same" and "self" (*el mismo rey*, *el rey mismo*). Latin splits them: *īdem rēx* "the same king", *rēx ipse* "the king himself".

**Do not expect English "that of".** "Caesar's army defeated that of Pompey" has no *ille* in Latin; the genitive is repeated or the phrase rebuilt (ag1903 §297).

## Connections

Spanish keeps the three-way split: *este* (from *iste*), *ese* (from *ipse*), *aquel* (from *eccum ille*, "look, that one") (Wiktionary), with the meanings shifted. Japanese これ, それ, あれ and Korean 이, 그, 저 divide space the same way as *hic*, *iste*, *ille*: near me, near you, away from us both.

## Exercises

1. Say "this temple" and "that temple over there", as subjects.
2. Say "I am giving this girl a gift".
3. Take apart *Illīus fāma magna est* and translate it.
4. Say "the consul himself is here".
5. Correct *\*Puer ipsum lavat* (meaning "the boy is washing himself").
6. Say "we live in the same town" (*in* with the ablative).
7. Translate *Haec cōnsul videt, ille nōn*.
8. Why does Cicero use *hic* for Catiline in *hic tamen vīvit*, and not *is*?

## Worked answers

1. *Templum* is neuter: *hoc templum*, *illud templum*.
2. The girl receives: dative *huic puellae*. *Huic puellae dōnum dō*.
3. *Illīus*: of that man, genitive singular of *ille*. *Fāma*: reputation. "That man's reputation is great."
4. *Cōnsul ipse adest*, or *Cōnsul ipse hīc est*. *Ipse* is masculine nominative singular, agreeing with *cōnsul*.
5. The object pointing back to the subject is *sē*: *Puer sē lavat*. With extra stress: *Puer sē ipse lavat*.
6. *Oppidum* is neuter, ablative *oppidō*: *In eōdem oppidō habitāmus*.
7. *Haec*: these things, neuter plural object. *Cōnsul videt*: the consul sees. *Ille nōn*: that man does not (the verb is understood). "The consul sees these things; that man does not."
8. Catiline was present in the senate, near the speaker. *Hic* points at him there; *is* would only refer back to someone mentioned.
