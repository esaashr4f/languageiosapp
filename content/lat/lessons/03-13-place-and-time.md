## The idea

Lesson 5 of this part gave the normal way to say where: a preposition and a case. *In silvā* "in the forest", *in silvam* "into the forest", *ē silvā* "out of the forest" (ag1903 §426). This lesson covers the places where Latin leaves the preposition out, and the expressions of time that work the same way.

The rule rests on one small class of nouns. With the names of towns and small islands, and with three common words, *domus* "home", *rūs* "the country" and *humus* "the ground", Latin uses the bare case (ag1903 §427):

- **place to which**: the accusative alone: *Rōmam* "to Rome", *domum* "home(wards)", *rūs* "to the country";
- **place from which**: the ablative alone: *Rōmā* "from Rome", *domō* "from home", *rūre* "from the country";
- **place where**: a special case, the **locative**: *Rōmae* "at Rome", *domī* "at home", *rūrī* "in the country", *humī* "on the ground".

Why only these nouns? The bare cases are the older system. Allen and Greenough explain that in the earliest Latin the cases alone gave the sense, and prepositions were added later for precision as the endings lost force (ag1903 §219). The accusative already meant "goal", the ablative "from", and the locative "at". Prepositions spread to ordinary nouns, but names of towns and a few everyday words of place kept the old, short construction.

The locative is the case Allen and Greenough mention in their list of cases as a trace of a seventh (ag1903 §35). It survives only in these words (ag1903 §421). Its forms have merged with others. In the first and second declensions singular it looks like the genitive: *Rōmae*, *Corinthī*, *domī*. Elsewhere it looks like the ablative: *Athēnīs* "at Athens", *Carthāgine* "at Carthage" (ag1903 §§43, 49, 80). It is still a separate meaning, and the gloss marks it LOC.

Time works like place. The time when something happens is the ablative, the "in" ablative of lesson 4: *prīmā lūce* "at first light", *hieme* "in winter". How long it lasts is the accusative, the case of extent: *multōs annōs* "for many years" (ag1903 §423). Allen and Greenough explain the pair: the ablative of time was in origin a locative, and the accusative of time is the same as the accusative of extent of space (ag1903 §423).

## How it is built

To say where, with a town name:

1. Decide the relation: at, to or from.
2. To: accusative, no preposition. *Rōma*, *Rōmam*.
3. From: ablative, no preposition. *Rōmā*.
4. At: the locative. For a singular noun of the first or second declension, use the form in *-ae* or *-ī*: *Rōmae*, *Corinthī*, *Brundisiī*. For a plural name or a third-declension name, use the ablative form: *Athēnīs*, *Carthāgine* or *Carthāginī* (ag1903 §§427, 80).

For "home" and "the country": *domī*, *domum*, *domō*; *rūrī*, *rūs*, *rūre* (ag1903 §427).

For all other places, the general words *urbs*, *oppidum*, *īnsula* among them, keep the prepositions (ag1903 §428): *in urbe*, *ad urbem*, *ex urbe*. When a name and a general word come together, the general word takes the preposition: *Rōmae in urbe* "in the city of Rome" (ag1903 §428).

To say when:

1. A point or a period within which: ablative. *Hōrā tertiā* "at the third hour"; *aestāte* "in summer"; *eō diē* "on that day".
2. A stretch through which something goes on: accusative. *Tōtam noctem*, "all night"; *multōs diēs* "for many days".

## Where it applies

**Place**

- **Names of towns and small islands**: *Rōmae*, *Rōmam*, *Rōmā*; *Corinthī*; *Athēnīs*; *Rhodī* "at Rhodes" (ag1903 §427).
- ***Domus*, *rūs*, *humus***: *domī*, *domum*, *domō*; *rūrī*, *rūs*, *rūre*; *humī* "on the ground" (ag1903 §427).
- **Other fixed locatives**: *bellī* "on campaign", contrasted with *domī*; *vesperī* "in the evening" (ag1903 §427).
- **Countries and large islands take prepositions**: *in Graeciā*, *in Graeciam*, *ex Graeciā*; *in Siciliā* (ag1903 §428).
- **Near a town, not in it**: *ad* with the accusative: *ad Carthāginem*, "near Carthage"; *apud* for "among, at the house of" (ag1903 §428).
- **Leaving a country**: *patriā* alone for "from one's country" in a few set phrases (ag1903 §428).
- **Poets** often leave out prepositions that prose needs (ag1903 §428).

**Time**

- **Time when**, ablative: *prīmā lūce*, *hōrā tertiā*, *hieme*, *aestāte*, *merīdiē*, *eō diē* (ag1903 §423).
- **Time within which**, ablative: *paucīs diēbus* "within a few days" (ag1903 §423).
- **Duration**, accusative: *multōs annōs*, *tōtam noctem*; with *per* for emphasis: *per tōtam noctem* (ag1903 §§423-424).
- **Some events named as times**: *lūdīs* "at the games", *bellō* "in the war" (ag1903 §424).
- **"Before" and "after"**: *ante bellum*, *post bellum*, with prepositions (lesson 5 of this part; ag1903 §424).
- **Adverbs of time** that are old case forms: *hodiē* "today" (an old locative, lesson 9 of this part), *māne* "in the morning".

**Space**

- **Extent of distance or size**, accusative, like duration: "they march ten miles", "a trench fifteen feet wide" (ag1903 §425). Numbers come in part 5.

## Watch out

**Do not add *in* to a town name.** Spanish *en Roma* and French *à Rome* tempt *\*In Rōmā habitāmus*. Classical prose uses the locative: *Rōmae habitāmus*.

**Do not add *ad* to a town you go to.** *\*Ad Rōmam properant* means "towards Rome, to its neighbourhood" (ag1903 §428). Going to the city itself: *Rōmam properant*.

**Do not use the bare case with ordinary nouns.** *\*Urbem properant* and *\*Silvae manent* treat *urbs* and *silva* like town names. They need prepositions: *ad urbem*, *in silvā*.

**Do not use the bare case with countries.** *\*Graeciae habitat*, *\*Graeciam nāvigant*: countries are not towns. *In Graeciā habitat*, *in Graeciam nāvigant* (ag1903 §428).

**Do not read every *Rōmae* as a genitive.** *Rōmae* can be "of Rome" or "at Rome". *Rōmae habitāmus* has no noun for a genitive to hang on, and *habitō* invites a place: locative.

**Do not use the accusative for a point of time.** *\*Prīmam lūcem ambulat* treats a moment as a stretch. A point is ablative: *prīmā lūce*.

**Do not use the ablative for a stretch out of English habit.** "For many years" is *multōs annōs*, not *\*multīs annīs*, which would mean "within many years" (ag1903 §423).

**Do not say *\*in hieme* for "in winter".** Time when takes no preposition: *hieme*.

**Do not use *domum* for "at home".** *\*Domum est* is "is homewards". "At home" is *domī*.

## Connections

English keeps a trace of the same pattern: "go home", "stay home", with no preposition, as Latin *domum*, *domī*. Japanese and Korean mark time and place with particles that Latin often leaves to the bare case: 冬に "in winter", 東京に住む "live in Tokyo". Spanish *en invierno* and French *en hiver* need a preposition where Latin says *hieme*.

## Exercises

1. Say "we are staying at Corinth", then "we are sailing to Corinth", then "from Corinth".
2. Say "the boys are at home".
3. Take apart *Athēnīs Rōmam nāvigat* and translate it.
4. Translate *Aestāte rūrī habitant, hieme Rōmae*.
5. Say "he works for many hours" and "he works at the third hour".
6. Correct *\*In Rōmā manent* and *\*Ad Athēnās nāvigant* (meaning to the city itself).
7. Why does *urbs* need a preposition when *Rōma* does not?
8. Translate *Multōs mēnsēs Brundisiī manet*.

## Worked answers

1. Where: locative *Corinthī manēmus*. To: accusative *Corinthum nāvigāmus*. From: ablative *Corinthō nāvigāmus*.
2. *Puerī domī sunt*. *Domī* is the locative.
3. *Athēn-īs*: from Athens, ablative (the verb is of motion, so not "at"). *Rōm-am*: to Rome. *Nāviga-t*: he sails. "He is sailing from Athens to Rome."
4. *Aestāt-e*, *hiem-e*: time when, ablative. *Rūr-ī*, *Rōm-ae*: locatives. "In summer they live in the country, in winter in Rome."
5. Duration: *Multās hōrās labōrat*. Time when: *Hōrā tertiā labōrat*.
6. *Rōmae manent*: the locative, no *in*. *Athēnās nāvigant*: the plain accusative; *ad* would mean only towards or near Athens.
7. Only names of towns and small islands, and *domus*, *rūs* and *humus*, keep the old bare cases. *Urbs* is a general word and follows the ordinary rule (ag1903 §428).
8. *Mult-ōs mēns-ēs*: for many months, accusative of duration. *Brundisi-ī*: at Brundisium, locative. *Mane-t*: he stays. "He has been staying at Brundisium for many months."
