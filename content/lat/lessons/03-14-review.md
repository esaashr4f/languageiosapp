## Before you start

This review covers part 3: the six cases and the dictionary entry (lesson 1), the genitive, dative and ablative (lessons 2 to 4), prepositions (lesson 5), the vocative and the full first and second declensions (lesson 6), the third, fourth and fifth declensions (lessons 7 to 9), the pronouns *ego*, *tū*, *sē* and *is* (lessons 10 and 11), the demonstratives (lesson 12), and place and time (lesson 13). It also uses the present tense, *sum*, agreement and word order from part 2.

Work each exercise before reading its answer. Every answer names the lesson to reread if you went wrong.

## Passage

**A. Two brothers.** A constructed passage, using only words and grammar from parts 2 and 3. One word is new: *inquit* "says", used only beside a quotation.

1. Mārcus et Quīntus frātrēs sunt. Rōmae habitant, sed pater eōrum rūrī habitat.
2. Hieme frātrēs domī manent; aestāte rūs properant et ibi patrī in agrīs labōrant.
3. Vesperī pater fīliīs fābulās dē bellō narrat. In eius fābulīs multī hostēs et magna perīcula sunt.
4. Quīntus fābulīs patris gaudet; Mārcus autem tacet, nam bellum timet. "Mī Mārce," inquit pater, "nōn ego bellum amō, sed patriam. Hic ager noster est, et nōs ipsī eum servāmus."

**B. Martial, Epigrams 1.32.** The whole two-line poem whose words you met in parts 2 and 3. Attested; wording from The Latin Library, in this guide's spelling. New words, glossed here: *possum* "I can", *dīcere* "to say", *quārē* "why", *tantum* "only".

*Nōn amō tē, Sabidī, nec possum dīcere quārē:*

*hoc tantum possum dīcere, nōn amō tē.*

## Exercises

1. Translate sentence 1 of passage A.
2. Translate sentence 2.
3. Translate sentence 3.
4. Translate sentence 4.
5. Translate passage B.
6. In passage A, explain the forms *Rōmae*, *rūrī*, *domī* and *rūs*, and why none has a preposition.
7. Why does sentence 1 say *pater eōrum* and not *pater suus*?
8. What case is *patrī* in sentence 2, and why?
9. In sentence 4, *fābulīs* and in sentence 3, *fīliīs*, both end in *-īs*. Give the case of each and say how you know.
10. In passage B, explain the forms *Sabidī* and *hoc*.
11. Say "the soldiers' leader gives the citizens of the city a reward".
12. Say "we are sailing from Rome to Athens with our friends".
13. Say "in winter the sea harms ships".
14. Say "Marcus, the teacher is teaching you your letters".
15. Say "the guards stay in the camp for many hours".
16. Correct *\*Mīlitī in castra manent* ("the soldiers are staying in the camp").
17. Correct *\*Caesar exercitum eius laudat*, meant as "Caesar praises his own army".
18. Correct *\*In Rōmā habitat* ("he lives in Rome").
19. Correct *\*Rēgem dōnum dat* ("he gives the king a gift").
20. Correct *\*Cum gladiō pugnat* ("he fights with a sword").
21. Correct *\*Corpus magnus est* and *\*fāma nautōrum*.
22. *Exercitūs* appears in *Metus exercitūs magnus est* and in *Exercitūs properant*. Explain both.
23. Give the genitive plural of *cīvis*, *rēx* and *canis*, with the reason for each.
24. In *Hic hīc habitat*, explain the two words that look alike.

## Worked answers

1. "Marcus and Quintus are brothers. They live in Rome, but their father lives in the country." *Frātrēs* is the nominative plural of *frāter* (lesson 7), a predicate noun after *sunt*. *Rōmae* and *rūrī* are locatives (lesson 13). *Eōrum* is the genitive plural of *is*, "of them" (lesson 11).
2. "In winter the brothers stay at home; in summer they hurry to the country and there they work in the fields for their father." *Hieme* and *aestāte*: time when, ablative (lesson 13). *Domī*: locative; *rūs*: accusative of the goal, no preposition (lesson 13). *Patrī*: dative (lesson 3). *In agrīs*: *in* with the ablative of rest (lesson 5).
3. "In the evening the father tells his sons stories about the war. In his stories there are many enemies and great dangers." *Vesperī* is a locative used for time (lesson 13). *Fīliīs* is dative, *fābulās* accusative (lesson 3). *Dē bellō*: "about", with the ablative (lesson 4). *Eius*: his, the father's (lesson 11). *Sunt* here states existence, "there are" (part 2, lesson 5).
4. "Quintus enjoys his father's stories; Marcus, however, says nothing, for he is afraid of war. 'My dear Marcus,' says his father, 'it is not war I love, but my country. This field is ours, and we ourselves protect it.'" *Fābulīs*: ablative of cause with *gaudet* (lesson 4). *Patris*: genitive (lesson 2). *Mī Mārce*: vocative (lesson 6). *Ego* is there for contrast (lesson 10). *Hic*: "this", near the speaker (lesson 12). *Ipsī* agrees with the subject *nōs* (lesson 12). *Eum* stands for *ager*, masculine (lesson 11).
5. "I don't like you, Sabidius, and I can't say why: I can only say this: I don't like you." *Sabidī* is the vocative of *Sabidius* (lesson 6). *Tē* is the accusative of *tū* (lesson 10). *Hoc* is the neuter of *hic*, pointing forward to what follows (lesson 12). *Nec* is "and not" (part 2, lesson 6).
6. *Rōmae* and *rūrī* are locatives, "at Rome", "in the country"; *domī* is the locative "at home"; *rūs* is the accusative of the goal, "to the country". Names of towns, and *domus*, *rūs* and *humus*, keep the old bare cases (lesson 13).
7. The owner, the brothers, is not the subject of *habitat*; the father is. *Suus* would refer back to the father himself. For an owner who is not the subject, Latin uses *eius* or *eōrum* (lessons 10 and 11).
8. Dative: the father is the person the work is done for, the dative of advantage (lesson 3).
9. *Fīliīs* is dative: the sons receive the stories after *narrat*. *Fābulīs* is ablative: with *gaudet* it gives the cause of the joy. Both are first or second declension, where the dative and ablative plural are always alike, so the verb decides (lessons 3 and 4).
10. *Sabidī*: names in *-ius* have the vocative in *-ī* (lesson 6). *Hoc*: neuter accusative singular of *hic*, the object of *dīcere*; the neuter refers to an idea, here the words that follow (lesson 12).
11. *Dux mīlitum cīvibus urbis praemium dat*. *Mīlitum*: genitive plural of a consonant stem (lessons 2 and 7). *Cīvibus*: dative plural, the receivers (lessons 3 and 8). *Urbis*: genitive singular (lesson 2). *Praemium*: the object.
12. *Rōmā Athēnās cum amīcīs nostrīs nāvigāmus*. Town names take the bare ablative for "from" and the bare accusative for "to" (lesson 13). Company takes *cum* with the ablative (lesson 4).
13. *Hieme mare nāvibus nocet*. *Hieme*: time when, ablative (lesson 13). *Noceō* takes the dative (lesson 3); *nāvibus* is the dative plural of the i-stem *nāvis* (lesson 8).
14. *Mārce, magister tē litterās docet*. *Mārce*: vocative (lesson 6). *Doceō* takes two accusatives, the person and the thing (lesson 5). *Tē*: accusative of *tū* (lesson 10). "Your" is left unsaid, as Latin usually does when the owner is obvious.
15. *Custōdēs multās hōrās in castrīs manent*. *Custōdēs*: plural of *custōs, custōdis* (lesson 7). Duration takes the accusative (lesson 13). *In castrīs*: rest, ablative (lesson 5).
16. *Mīlitēs in castrīs manent*. Third-declension plurals end in *-ēs*, not *-ī* (lesson 7). Staying is rest, so *in* takes the ablative (lesson 5).
17. *Caesar exercitum suum laudat*. The owner is the subject, so the reflexive *suus*, agreeing with *exercitum* (lessons 10 and 11).
18. *Rōmae habitat*. The name of a town takes the locative, with no preposition (lesson 13).
19. *Rēgī dōnum dat*. The receiver is dative, the thing given accusative (lesson 3).
20. *Gladiō pugnat*. A tool takes the plain ablative; *cum* is for company (lesson 4).
21. *Corpus magnum est*: *corpus, corporis* is a neuter of the third declension, so the adjective is neuter (lesson 7). *Fāma nautārum*: *nauta* is first declension, genitive plural *-ārum*, whatever its gender (lesson 2).
22. In the first, *exercitūs* depends on *metus* and the verb is singular: genitive singular, "the army's fear". In the second, it is the subject of a plural verb: nominative plural, "the armies are hurrying" (lesson 9).
23. *Cīvium*: *cīvis, cīvis* has the same number of syllables in both forms, an i-stem (lesson 8). *Rēgum*: *rēx, rēgis* is a consonant stem (lesson 7). *Canum*: *canis* looks like an i-stem but is one of the exceptions (lesson 8).
24. The first *hic* is the pronoun, "this man", with a short *i*. The second, *hīc*, is the adverb "here", with a long *ī* (lesson 12). "This man lives here."
