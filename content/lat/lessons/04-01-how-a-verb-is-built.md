## The idea

So far you have used one tense, the present, and built it from one stem. A Latin verb has three stems, and every one of its forms is built on one of them. The dictionary shows you all three by listing four forms of the verb. These four forms are the **principal parts**: the forms from which every other form can be built.

*Amō, amāre, amāvī, amātum* is a full entry for "love". It reads: "I love", "to love", "I loved" (or "I have loved"), and a rare verbal noun called the supine, *amātum*.

Why four, when English makes do with "love, loved"? Because the three stems cannot be predicted from one another. Allen and Greenough explain that a Latin verb is not one system worked out from a single root. It is a set of separate formations, some inherited and some new, that were grouped together over time (ag1903 §168, note 1).

The clearest case is the perfect. Its stem is built in at least five different ways. One kind adds *-v-* or *-u-* (*amā-v-ī*, *mon-u-ī*): Allen and Greenough call this a Latin creation of uncertain origin (ag1903 §177). Another adds *-s-* (*mān-s-ī* "I stayed", *rī-s-ī* "I laughed"): this is an old aorist, the past tense of the parent language, and *dīxī* "I said" has the same build as Greek *ἔδειξα* "I showed" (ag1903 §177). A third repeats the first consonant (*ste-t-ī* from *stō*). A fourth lengthens the root vowel (*vīd-ī* from *videō*). The perfect has so many shapes because it continues two older verb categories, the aorist and the perfect, which merged into one Latin tense (Weiss, *Outline*; Sihler, *New Comparative Grammar*). Each verb kept whichever formation it happened to inherit, so you have to learn it.

Grammars call the forms built on the present stem the **present system** and those built on the perfect stem the **perfect system**. Some use the Latin names *infectum* ("not completed") and *perfectum* ("completed").

## How it is built

Take the entry *videō, vidēre, vīdī, vīsum* and read it in four steps.

1. **First part: the "I" form of the present.** *Videō*, "I see". You met it in part 2. It is a real form, not a stem.
2. **Second part: the present infinitive**, "to see" (lesson 3 of this part). Remove *-re* and you have the **present stem**, *vidē-*. The vowel before *-re* names the conjugation (ag1903 §171):
   - *-āre*: first conjugation (*amāre*);
   - *-ēre*, with a long *ē*: second conjugation (*vidēre*);
   - *-ere*, with a short *e*: third conjugation (*mittere*);
   - *-īre*: fourth conjugation (*venīre*).
3. **Third part: the perfect**, "I saw" or "I have seen". Remove the *-ī* and you have the **perfect stem**, *vīd-*.
4. **Fourth part: the supine**, *vīsum*. Remove the *-um* and you have the **supine stem**, *vīs-*. The supine itself is a rare verbal noun (part 7). Its stem matters because it builds the perfect participle, *vīsus* "seen" (lesson 12 of this part), and the future participle (part 5).

Without macrons, the second and third conjugations look alike: *vidēre* and *mittere* both end in *-ere*. The first part separates them. A second-conjugation verb ends in *-eō* (*videō*); a third-conjugation verb ends in plain *-ō* (*mittō*). A small group of third-conjugation verbs ends in *-iō*, like *capiō, capere* "take". Its infinitive in short *-ere* shows that it is not fourth conjugation (*veniō, venīre*). Lesson 2 of this part explains this group.

Each stem has its own territory (ag1903 §§168-169, 179):

- **The present stem** builds the present, the imperfect (lesson 4) and the future (lesson 5), the present infinitive, the commands (lesson 9), and the passive of all of these (lesson 11).
- **The perfect stem** builds the perfect (lesson 6), the pluperfect and the future perfect (lesson 8), all in the active.
- **The supine stem** builds the perfect participle, and with it the passive of the perfect, pluperfect and future perfect (lesson 12).

The subjunctive, a separate set of forms (lessons 17 and 18), follows the same split.

Some verbs lack a part. *Timeō, timēre, timuī* has no supine, so it has no perfect participle. Where the supine is missing but a future participle exists, dictionaries give that instead: *sum, esse, fuī, futūrus*. Other verbs give only three parts with *sum* in the third place: *gaudeō, gaudēre, gāvīsus sum* "rejoice" has a perfect that looks passive. Lesson 13 explains why.

## Where it applies

The principal parts are what you look up, and what you work back to, every time you meet a verb.

- **Building forms.** Every tense in this part starts with the question "which stem?". The imperfect and future come from the second part, the perfect tenses from the third, the perfect passive from the fourth.
- **Reading forms.** A dictionary lists a verb under its first part only. If you meet *mānsit*, you have to know that *mān-s-* is the perfect stem of *maneō* "stay" before you can find it. Learning the four parts together lets you run the steps backwards.
- **Telling verbs apart.** *Venit* "he comes" and *vēnit* "he came" differ only in the length of one vowel (part 1). The third principal part is what tells you that *vēn-* is a perfect stem.
- **Irregular first and second conjugations.** Most first-conjugation verbs go *-āre, -āvī, -ātum* and most fourth go *-īre, -īvī, -ītum* (ag1903 §179). But some first-conjugation verbs do not: *dō, dare, dedī, datum*; *stō, stāre, stetī, statum*; *iuvō, iuvāre, iūvī, iūtum*; *vetō*; *sonō* (ag1903 §209). The second conjugation has a common type in *-uī, -itum* (*moneō, monuī, monitum*) and many exceptions (*videō, vīdī, vīsum*; *iubeō, iussī, iussum*).
- **The third conjugation.** Here every kind of perfect and supine occurs, because the perfect is formed from the root, not from the present stem (ag1903 §179). There is no default to fall back on.
- **Verbs with missing parts.** Some have no supine (*timeō*, *pateō*), some give a future participle in its place (*sum*), and some have a passive-looking perfect (*gaudeō, gāvīsus sum*, lesson 13).
- **Nouns from verbs.** The supine stem also builds nouns. Allen and Greenough connect the endings *-tus* and *-tiō* with it (ag1903 §238): *sēnsus* "feeling" from *sentiō*, *cursus* "running" from *currō*. You already know these nouns. Seeing the stem in them helps you remember the fourth part.

## Watch out

**Do not build every perfect like *amāvī*.** *\*Vidēvī*, *\*manēvī* and *\*stāvī* are not Latin. The perfects are *vīdī*, *mānsī* and *stetī*. Even inside the first conjugation, *\*vetāvī* is wrong: the perfect of *vetō* is *vetuī*.

**Do not confuse the second and third conjugations.** *Mittere* is not *\*mittēre*. Look at the first part: *-eō* means second conjugation, plain *-ō* third. The difference decides every other present-system form, as lessons 2, 4 and 5 show.

**Do not take *capiō* for a fourth-conjugation verb.** *Capiō* and *veniō* both end in *-iō*, but *capere* has a short *e* and *venīre* a long *ī*. The infinitive decides: *\*capīre* does not exist.

**Do not read the fourth part as "loved".** *Amātum* is the supine, a verbal noun. The adjective "loved" is the participle built on its stem, *amātus, -a, -um* (lesson 12). They share a stem, not a meaning.

**Do not assume vowel length carries over.** The root vowel can change length from stem to stem: *videō* but *vīdī*; *veniō* but *vēnī*; *sedeō* but *sēdī*. Learn the parts with their macrons.

**The dictionary form is a full word.** *Amō* means "I love". The stem is *amā-*, which is never said on its own except as a command (lesson 9).

**Spanish will not sort the conjugations for you.** Spanish *tener* continues *tenēre* (second conjugation) and *hacer* continues *facere* (third, Wiktionary): both became Spanish *-er*. Only the Latin vowel length tells them apart.

## Connections

English takes many words from the supine stem: *mission* from *missum*, *vision* from *vīsum*, *motion* from *mōtum* (*moveō*). Spanish and French irregular pasts often continue Latin perfects: Spanish *hice* "I did" and French *je fis* both go back to *fēcī*, the perfect of *faciō*.

## Exercises

1. Give the conjugation and the three stems of *iubeō, iubēre, iussī, iussum* and of *mittō, mittere, mīsī, missum*.
2. Without macrons, *monere* and *regere* look alike. Their first parts are *moneō* and *regō*. Which conjugation is each, and how do you know?
3. Which conjugation is *capiō, capere* and which is *audiō, audīre*? Explain.
4. You meet *mānsit* in a text. Which principal part does its stem come from, and which verb is it?
5. From the entries for *ārdeō* and *ōrō*, say "they are burning" and "we pray".
6. Sort these perfects by how their stems are formed: *amāvī*, *monuī*, *rīsī*, *vīdī*, *stetī*.
7. Why does a Latin dictionary need four principal parts, when English "love, loved" needs two?
8. Translate *Lēx hoc vetat*, and say what is unusual about *vetō* among first-conjugation verbs.
9. *Pateō* has only three principal parts. What does that tell you about the forms it lacks?

## Worked answers

1. *Iubeō*: second conjugation (*-eō*, *-ēre*). Present stem *iubē-*, perfect stem *iuss-*, supine stem *iuss-*. *Mittō*: third conjugation (plain *-ō*, short *-ere*). Present stem *mitte-*, perfect stem *mīs-*, supine stem *miss-*.
2. *Moneō* ends in *-eō*, so it is second conjugation, and its infinitive is *monēre* with a long *ē*. *Regō* ends in plain *-ō*, so it is third conjugation, and its infinitive is *regere* with a short *e*.
3. *Capiō, capere* is third conjugation: the infinitive has a short *-ere*. *Audiō, audīre* is fourth: the infinitive has *-īre*. Both first parts end in *-iō*, so only the infinitive decides.
4. *Mān-s-* is the stem of the third part, *mānsī*: it is a perfect stem, and the verb is *maneō, manēre, mānsī, mānsum* "stay". *Mānsit* is "he stayed" (lesson 6 builds it).
5. *Ārdeō, ārdēre*: present stem *ārdē-*, and the vowel shortens before *nt*: *ārdent*. *Ōrō, ōrāre*: present stem *ōrā-*: *ōrāmus*.
6. *Amāvī* and *monuī* add *-v-* or *-u-*. *Rīsī* adds *-s-*. *Vīdī* lengthens the root vowel. *Stetī* repeats the first consonant.
7. The present, perfect and supine stems cannot be worked out from each other. The perfect alone has several formations, inherited from two older categories that merged (ag1903 §177; Weiss). The dictionary has to show each stem.
8. "The law forbids this." Most first-conjugation verbs make the perfect in *-āvī*; *vetō* has *vetuī*, and its supine is *vetitum*, not *\*vetātum*.
9. It has no supine, so it has no perfect participle and no perfect passive. Since "be open" acts on nothing, it has no passive at all (Wiktionary).
