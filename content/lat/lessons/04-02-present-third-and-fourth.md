## The idea

In part 2 every verb had a long vowel at the end of its stem: *amā-*, *vidē-*. The endings sat on that vowel, and the vowel shortened only in three places. The third conjugation does not work like this. Its stem ends in a short vowel, and that vowel changes shape from ending to ending: *reg-ō* "I rule", *reg-it* "he rules", *reg-unt* "they rule". This lesson explains where that vowel comes from, and then adds the fourth conjugation, which has a long *ī* and borrows one form from the third.

The reason lies in the parent language, Indo-European. Allen and Greenough describe two kinds of verb in it (ag1903 §174). In one kind, the endings were joined straight to the root. Only a handful of these survive in Latin, and they are the irregular verbs: *es-t* "is" is one. In the other kind, a short vowel stood between the root and the ending. Grammars call it the **thematic vowel**: a linking vowel, *e* or *o*, that carries no meaning of its own. Greek still shows it plainly: *λέγ-ε-τε* "you say", *λέγ-ο-μεν* "we say".

Latin kept many of these verbs, and they are its third conjugation. In Latin the linking vowel appears as *i* or *u* (*reg-i-t*, *reg-u-nt*), and as *e* before *r* (*reg-e-re*). Allen and Greenough set *legunt* "they read" beside an older *\*leg-o-nti*, with the same *-nti* as Doric Greek (ag1903 §174). That is why the third person plural has a *u*: it is the old *o*.

The first, second and fourth conjugations come from verbs of a different build. A suffix *-ye-* or *-yo-* was added to a stem ending in a vowel, and the vowels contracted into the long *ā*, *ē* and *ī* you see in *amāre*, *vidēre*, *audīre* (ag1903 §174). That contraction is why those three conjugations have a long stem vowel and the third does not.

One group sits between the two. Where the *-yo-* suffix met a root ending in a consonant, the result is verbs like *capiō* "take" and *veniō* "come". Some went into the third conjugation, some into the fourth, and some have forms of both (ag1903 §174). Allen and Greenough call their rule for these verbs a working rule only: "the actual explanation of the forms of such verbs is not fully understood" (ag1903 §188, note). This guide calls them **-iō verbs**.

## How it is built

**Third conjugation, *regō, regere*.** Take the infinitive and remove *-re*: the stem is *rege-*, with a short *e* (lesson 1 of this part). Then follow the linking vowel.

1. Before *-ō* it disappears: *regō*.
2. Before *-s*, *-t*, *-mus* and *-tis* it is *i*: *regis*, *regit*, *regimus*, *regitis*.
3. Before *-nt* it is *u*: *regunt*.

In the glosses this guide cuts these forms after the consonant of the stem, so that the linking vowel is shown with the ending: *reg-it*, *reg-unt*. You can then read off the six endings of this conjugation: *-ō*, *-is*, *-it*, *-imus*, *-itis*, *-unt*.

Because the vowel is short, the stress falls differently from part 2. *Regimus* has a light penult, *gi*, so the stress goes back to *RE·gi·mus* (part 1). Compare *a·MĀ·mus*, where the long *ā* holds the stress.

**-iō verbs, *capiō, capere*.** The stem is *cape-*. These verbs behave like *regō* except in two places, where an *i* appears before the ending's vowel (ag1903 §188):

1. *capiō*, not *\*capō*;
2. *capiunt*, not *\*capunt*.

Everywhere else they match *regō*: *capis*, *capit*, *capimus*, *capitis*. The *i* is lost before *i*, so there is no *\*capiit* (ag1903 §179c).

**Fourth conjugation, *audiō, audīre*.** The stem is *audī-*, with a long *ī*. Most forms work like *amā-* in part 2: the ending sits on the vowel, which stays long before *-s*, *-mus* and *-tis* (*audīs*, *audīmus*, *audītis*) and shortens before a vowel and before final *-t* (*audiō*, *audit*). The third person plural takes the third-conjugation ending: *audiunt* (ag1903 §179d).

To build any present, then:

1. Find the stem from the infinitive.
2. Name the conjugation from the vowel and its length: *-ere* third, *-īre* fourth. Check the first principal part for *-iō*.
3. Pick the person ending, and apply that conjugation's vowel.

Say "the soldiers are seizing the citadel". The verb is *capiō, capere*: short *-ere*, so third conjugation, and its first part ends in *-iō*. The subject is plural, third person: *capiunt*, with the *i* kept before *-unt*. The object is *arcem*. *Mīlitēs arcem capiunt.*

## Where it applies

- **The largest class of basic verbs.** Many of the commonest verbs are third conjugation: *agō*, *dīcō*, *dūcō*, *petō*, *pōnō*, *regō*, *scrībō*, *vincō*. They are old verbs, inherited from the parent language. New Latin verbs were made in the first and fourth conjugations, from nouns and adjectives (ag1903 §174), so the third is small in number but large in use.
- **All the uses of the present.** Everything lesson 1 of part 2 said about the present holds here: an act going on now (*Rēx populum regit*), a general truth (*Omnia vincit Amor*, *Hominēs dum docent discunt*), a vivid narrative present, and an attempt (*fugit* can be "tries to run away" in a story).
- **The -iō verbs.** A fixed list. The commonest in this lesson are *capiō*, *faciō*, *fugiō* and *rapiō*, and their compounds (lesson 16 of this part). Learn them as a group: the first principal part ends in *-iō* and the infinitive in short *-ere*.
- **The fourth conjugation.** *Audiō*, *sciō*, *sentiō*, *dormiō*, *veniō* and *inveniō*. Some are built on nouns: Allen and Greenough derive *fīnīre* "to limit" from the stem of *fīnis* "end" (ag1903 §176, footnote).
- **Verbs that mix conjugations.** A verb is classed by its present stem, even when its perfect looks like another class (ag1903 §173a). *Petō, petere, petīvī* is third conjugation, though its perfect looks fourth. *Quaerō, quaerere, quaesīvī* is the same.
- **Verbs with a case other than the accusative.** *Crēdō* "believe, trust" takes the person in the dative (ag1903 §367): *amīcō crēdō*, "I trust my friend". The thing believed is accusative: *fābulam crēdō*. *Petō* takes the accusative of what you aim at or attack: *urbem petunt*, "they make for the city".

## Watch out

**Do not give the third conjugation a long vowel.** Without macrons, *regere* looks like *vidēre*, and you may be tempted to write *\*regēmus* or *\*regētis*. The third conjugation has *i* there, short: *regimus*, *regitis*. (A form like *regēmus* does exist, but it is the future, lesson 5 of this part.)

**Do not write *-ent* or *-int* in the third person plural.** *\*Regent* and *\*regint* are not presents. The ending is *-unt*: *regunt*, *scrībunt*, *currunt*. *Regent* is the future "they will rule" (lesson 5).

**Do not treat *capiō* as fourth conjugation.** *\*Capīs*, *\*capīmus* and *\*capiit* are wrong. *Capiō* keeps its *i* only before *-ō* and *-unt*; elsewhere it is *capis*, *capimus*, *capit*, exactly like *regis*, *regimus*, *regit*.

**Do not drop the *i* from the fourth conjugation's plural.** *\*Audunt* and *\*audint* are wrong; *\*audiint* is wrong too. The form is *audiunt*: the stem *audi-* plus the third-conjugation *-unt*.

**Do not stress the linking vowel.** *\*re·GI·mus* and *\*ca·PI·tis* break the stress rule: a short vowel in an open penult cannot take the stress. Say *RE·gi·mus*, *CA·pi·tis*. In the fourth conjugation the *ī* is long: *au·DĪ·mus*.

**Do not sort Latin verbs by their Spanish or French descendants.** Spanish *decir* "say" comes from third-conjugation *dīcere*, and *dormir* from fourth-conjugation *dormīre*. Both ended up in Spanish *-ir*; the Latin classes did not survive intact. The Latin infinitive and its vowel length are the only guide.

**Do not translate "believe someone" with an accusative.** *\*Tē crēdō* for "I believe you" is wrong. The person trusted is in the dative: *tibi crēdō* (part 3, lesson 3). Spanish *te creo* and French *je te crois* use an object pronoun, which misleads.

**Do not read *dīcit* as a past.** *Dīcit* "he says" and *dīxit* "he said" differ by one letter. The *x* (*c* + *s*) belongs to the perfect stem, *dīx-* (lesson 1 of this part). The present always shows the present stem, *dīc-*.

## Connections

Greek preserves the linking vowel that Latin turned into *i* and *u*: *λέγ-ο-μεν* "we say" beside Latin *leg-i-mus* "we read". Spanish *vivir* and French *vivre* both continue *vīvere*, and Spanish *dormir*, French *dormir* continue *dormīre*: the third and fourth conjugations fell together in Spanish.

## Exercises

1. Conjugate *dūcō, dūcere* in the present, all six persons, and mark the stress of the first and second persons plural.
2. Build "they are making" from *faciō, facere* and "they are coming" from *veniō, venīre*. Explain why both have an *i* before the ending.
3. Take apart *scrībitis*, *sentīmus* and *rapit*: conjugation, person, number, meaning.
4. Translate *Mīlitēs arcem petunt et urbem relinquunt.*
5. Say "the boy is reading the letter and the girl is listening".
6. Why does *legunt* have a *u*, when *legit* has an *i*? Use the history.
7. Correct *\*Hostēs fugint* ("the enemy are running away") and *\*Puerī capīunt* ("the boys are taking").
8. Say "I trust the consul" with *crēdō*.
9. Without macrons, *dicitis* and *audītis* look alike in their endings. What decides how each is pronounced and stressed?

## Worked answers

1. *Dūcō, dūcis, dūcit, dūcimus, dūcitis, dūcunt*. The stem is *dūce-*, third conjugation. Stress: *DŪ·ci·mus*, *DŪ·ci·tis*. The penult *ci* is light, so the stress falls on the antepenult.
2. *Faciunt*: an -iō verb keeps its *i* before *-unt*. *Veniunt*: a fourth-conjugation verb takes the third-conjugation *-unt* on its stem *veni-*. Both *i*s go back to the old *-yo-* suffix on a consonant root (ag1903 §174); *faciō* went into the third conjugation and *veniō* into the fourth.
3. *Scrīb-itis*: third conjugation, second person plural, "you (more than one) write". *Sentī-mus*: fourth conjugation, first person plural, "we feel"; the *ī* is long. *Rap-it*: an -iō verb, third person singular, "he snatches"; no *i* before *-it*.
4. "The soldiers make for the citadel and leave the city." *Pet-unt* and *relinqu-unt* are both third-conjugation plurals. *Arcem* and *urbem* are accusative objects (part 3, lessons 7 and 8).
5. *Puer epistulam legit et puella audit.* *Leg-it*: third conjugation, linking vowel *i*. *Audi-t*: fourth conjugation, the *ī* short before final *-t*.
6. The *u* is the old linking vowel *o*, kept before *-nt*: Allen and Greenough compare *legunt* with an older *\*leg-o-nti* (ag1903 §174). Before the other endings the same vowel appears as *i*.
7. *Hostēs fugiunt*: an -iō verb has *-iunt*, never *-int*. *Puerī capiunt*: the *i* is short (it stands before a vowel), and *capiō* is third conjugation, not fourth.
8. *Cōnsulī crēdō.* *Crēdō* takes the person trusted in the dative (ag1903 §367): *cōnsul-ī*, third declension (part 3, lesson 7).
9. Vowel length. *Dīcitis* is third conjugation, with a short *i*, so the penult is light: *DĪ·ci·tis*. *Audītis* is fourth, with a long *ī*, so the penult is heavy: *au·DĪ·tis*. The infinitives, *dīcere* and *audīre*, tell you which is which.
