## The idea

Every verb form you have built so far has a person: *amat* is "he loves", *regunt* "they rule". Sometimes you need the action with no one attached: "to love", "to rule". That form is the **infinitive**: the form of a verb that names the action without saying who does it. You have already met it as the second principal part (lesson 1 of this part).

The infinitive has no person ending, so it cannot make a sentence on its own. Its main job is to hang on another verb that needs an action to complete it. "I can" is incomplete; "I can sing" is not. Latin works the same way: *possum* "I can" plus *cantāre* "to sing" gives *possum cantāre*. Allen and Greenough call this the **complementary infinitive**: an infinitive that completes the meaning of a verb, with the same doer (ag1903 §456).

Where does the infinitive come from? Wiktionary traces the ending *-ere* to an older *\*-ezi*, from an Indo-European *\*-esi*: a locative case form of a noun for an action. So *regere* began as something like "in ruling", a noun of action in the case for "at" or "in". That is why the infinitive still behaves like a noun: it can be the subject of a sentence, and it is then neuter (ag1903 §452).

The *s* of that ending became *r* between vowels. This is **rhotacism**, the change you met with *corpus, corporis* (part 3). It explains why most infinitives end in *-re* (*amā-re*, *regĕ-re*) but *esse* "to be" keeps its *s*: there the *s* was never between vowels.

## How it is built

**The present infinitive.** It is the second principal part. For the four regular conjugations:

- first: *amāre*;
- second: *vidēre*;
- third: *regere*, and for -iō verbs *capere* (no *i*);
- fourth: *audīre*.

**Five verbs that take an infinitive.** Two are regular, three are irregular.

1. ***Dēbeō*, "owe; ought, must"** (part 3, lesson 3). It is a regular second-conjugation verb: *dēbeō, dēbēs, dēbet*. Allen and Greenough explain it as *dē* "from" plus *habeō* "have", contracted: "to have from someone", hence "to owe" (ag1903 §15).
2. ***Soleō*, "be used to"**: regular second conjugation. *Solet labōrāre*, "he usually works".
3. ***Possum*, "can, be able"**. It is *pot-* "able" plus *sum* (Wiktionary: from *potis* "in control" plus *sum*). Before a vowel the *t* stays: *pot-es*, *pot-est*, *pot-estis*. Before *s* it becomes *s*: *pos-sum*, *pos-sumus*, *pos-sunt*. Learn it as *sum* with *pot-* or *pos-* in front. The infinitive is *posse*.
4. ***Volō*, "want, be willing"**. Irregular: *volō, vīs, vult, volumus, vultis, volunt*; infinitive *velle*. The odd forms are old: Allen and Greenough count *volō* among the verbs that add some endings straight to the root (ag1903 §197). The forms *vīs* and *velle* are not explained here.
5. ***Nōlō*, "not want, refuse"**, is *ne* "not" plus *volō* (Wiktionary). ***Mālō*, "prefer"**, is *magis* "more" plus *volō*, "want more" (Wiktionary). Both contracted: *nōlō, nōlumus, nōlunt*; *mālō, mālumus, mālunt*. In the forms where *volō* has *v-*, they show the join: *nōn vīs*, *nōn vult*, *nōn vultis*; *māvīs*, *māvult*, *māvultis*. Infinitives *nōlle*, *mālle*.

**Building the sentence.** Say "the soldiers do not want to flee".

1. The main verb is "not want", *nōlō*. The subject is "the soldiers", third person plural: *nōlunt*.
2. "To flee" is the infinitive of *fugiō, fugere*: *fugere*.
3. The infinitive has no ending to change. It stays *fugere* whoever the doer is.
4. Order: in neutral prose the infinitive often comes just before the verb it depends on. *Mīlitēs fugere nōlunt.*

With *mālō*, the thing preferred against comes after *quam* "than": *mālō labōrāre quam dormīre*, "I prefer working to sleeping". *Quam* in this sense is taught with comparison (part 5); here it is a fixed pattern.

## Where it applies

- **After verbs of ability, will and obligation:** *possum*, *volō*, *nōlō*, *mālō*, *dēbeō* (this lesson's examples).
- **After verbs of habit, daring and starting or stopping:** *soleō* "be used to", *audeō* "dare", *incipiō* "begin", *dēsinō* "stop", *pergō* "go on" (ag1903 §456). *Dīcere audeō*, "I dare to say".
- **After verbs of wanting, trying and deciding:** *cupiō*, *optō*, *temptō*, *cōnstituō* "decide". Caesar writes *bellum gerere cōnstituit*, "he decided to wage war" (Gallic War 4.6, quoted at ag1903 §457).
- **After verbs of knowing how, learning and hesitating:** *sciō* "know how to", *discō* "learn to", *dubitō* "hesitate to", *timeō* "be afraid to", *properō* "hurry to".
- **As the subject of *est*:** *Vincere pulchrum est*; *Vīvere mīlitāre est*. The adjective is neuter singular (ag1903 §452).
- **As a noun in other places.** The infinitive can be the object of a few verbs, or stand after *est* as the predicate, as *mīlitāre* does above. It does not take prepositions and has no case endings.
- **With a different doer.** "I want *you* to come" needs a subject in the accusative beside the infinitive: *volō tē venīre*. This is a different construction (ag1903 §456, note), taught with reported statements (part 6). *Iubeō* "order" works the same way: *iubeō tē venīre*, "I order you to come".
- **"Can", "must" and "want" in every tense.** *Possum*, *volō* and *dēbeō* have imperfects and futures like any verb (lessons 4 and 5). The infinitive after them never changes.

## Watch out

**Do not put a person ending on the second verb.** *\*Possumus labōrāmus* is two sentences jammed together. Only the first verb carries the person: *labōrāre possumus*.

**Do not build *possum* from Spanish or Italian.** Spanish *puedo* and Italian *posso* tempt you towards *\*possō*. The Latin "I can" is *possum*: *sum* with *pos-* in front. Likewise *\*potsum* is wrong: *t* becomes *s* before *s*.

**Do not regularise *volō*.** *\*Volēre*, *\*volāre* and *\*volit* are not forms of "want". The infinitive is *velle* and the third person singular *vult*. (*Volāre* exists, but it is a different verb, "fly".)

**Do not say *nōn volō* for "I don't want".** Latin contracts it: *nōlō*, *nōlumus*, *nōlunt*. The forms *nōn vīs*, *nōn vult* and *nōn vultis* are the second and third persons of *nōlō* itself, not a mistake.

**Do not translate English "to" with a preposition.** *\*Ad cantāre* for "to sing" is impossible: *ad* takes a noun in the accusative (part 3, lesson 5), and the infinitive already means "to sing". Nor does Spanish *para* or French *pour* have a place here: "I want to sing" is *cantāre volō*.

**Do not use *habeō* for "have to".** English "I have to work" and Spanish *tengo que trabajar* suggest *\*habeō labōrāre*. In classical Latin, obligation is *dēbeō*: *labōrāre dēbeō*. (The Romance futures, French *je chanterai*, come from a later "*cantāre habeō*", but that is not classical usage.)

**Do not put the predicate after *esse* in the accusative.** *\*Vir bonum esse vult* is wrong: the man is the subject of both verbs, so the adjective is nominative, *bonus* (ag1903 §458). The accusative appears only when the sentence names a separate subject for the infinitive (part 6).

**Do not add an *i* to the -iō infinitive.** *\*Capiere* and *\*faciere* do not exist. The -iō verbs lose the *i* before short *e*: *capere*, *facere*, *fugere* (ag1903 §179c).

## Connections

Spanish and French keep the infinitive with the same job: *quiero cantar*, *je veux chanter*, *puedo leer*. Their infinitive endings *-ar, -er, -ir* and *-er, -ir, -re* all continue Latin *-āre, -ēre, -ere, -īre*. Japanese and Korean put the dependent verb first and the "can" or "want" after it, as Latin's neutral order does: *Cantāre possum* has the shape of Japanese *utau koto ga dekiru*.

## Exercises

1. Give the present infinitive of *mittō*, *moneō*, *rapiō*, *sentiō* and *laudō*, and name each conjugation.
2. Say "we can see the sea" and "they cannot see the sea".
3. Say "the boy wants to read, but the girl prefers to write".
4. Translate *Servī labōrāre nōlunt; dormīre mālunt.*
5. Take apart *potestis* and *possumus*, and explain the *t* and the *s*.
6. Say "Marcus is usually happy", with *soleō* and *esse*.
7. Correct *\*Volō dormiō* ("I want to sleep") and *\*Nōn volunt pugnāre* ("they don't want to fight").
8. Translate *Amīcīs crēdere dēbēmus.* Why is *amīcīs* dative?
9. Why is the infinitive neuter when it is a subject? Use its history.

## Worked answers

1. *Mittere*, third conjugation (short *e*). *Monēre*, second (long *ē*). *Rapere*, third -iō (no *i* before short *e*). *Sentīre*, fourth. *Laudāre*, first.
2. *Mare vidēre possumus.* *Mare vidēre nōn possunt.* *Possumus* and *possunt* have *pos-* before *s*. *Mare* is neuter, so accusative like nominative (part 3, lesson 8).
3. *Puer legere vult, sed puella scrībere māvult.* *Vult* and *māvult* are third person singular. Both infinitives are third conjugation, with short *e*.
4. "The slaves refuse to work; they would rather sleep." *Nōlunt* is *ne* + *volunt*; *mālunt* is *magis* + *volunt*. Each takes an infinitive.
5. *Pot-estis*: *pot-* "able" plus *estis*; the *t* stays before a vowel. *Pos-sumus*: *pot-* plus *sumus*; *t* becomes *s* before *s*.
6. *Mārcus laetus esse solet.* *Laetus* is nominative: the subject of *solet* is also the subject of *esse* (ag1903 §458).
7. *Dormīre volō*: only the main verb carries a person ending; "to sleep" is the infinitive. *Pugnāre nōlunt*: "not want" is one verb, *nōlō*, and its third person plural is *nōlunt*.
8. "We ought to trust our friends." *Crēdō* takes the person trusted in the dative (lesson 2 of this part), and the infinitive keeps the construction of its verb: *amīcīs crēdere*.
9. The infinitive began as a case form of a noun of action, a locative in *\*-esi* (Wiktionary, *-ere*). It still behaves as a noun, and as a noun it is treated as neuter singular: an adjective agreeing with it is neuter (ag1903 §452). Why neuter rather than another gender is not explained here.
