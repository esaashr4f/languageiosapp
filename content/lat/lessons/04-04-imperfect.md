## The idea

Latin has two everyday past tenses, and they do not divide the past by time. They divide it by how the speaker looks at the action. This lesson gives the first, the **imperfect**: a past tense that shows an action as going on, repeated, or as the state of things at the time. Its name means "not completed": the action is seen from inside, still in progress. The other past tense, the perfect, comes in lesson 6, and lesson 7 shows how to choose between them.

Allen and Greenough put it in one line: the imperfect "represents a present tense transferred to past time" (ag1903 §471). Everything the present does for now, the imperfect does for then. *Rēx regit* is "the king rules" or "the king is ruling"; *rēx regēbat* is "the king was ruling", "the king used to rule", or "the king ruled" in the sense "was the ruler at that time".

The imperfect is the tense of description. A storyteller uses it for the scene, the background and the habits: the sea was calm, the soldiers were sleeping, the farmers used to plough these fields. Allen and Greenough call it "a descriptive tense" for "a state of things as actually observed" (ag1903 §470, note).

Its marker is *-bā-*, as in *amā-ba-t* "he was loving". Wiktionary derives it from the Indo-European root *\*bʰuH-* "be, become", the same root as Latin *fuī* "I was" and English *be*. Allen and Greenough explain the form as once a separate verb meaning "I was", added to a noun-like form of the main verb: *vidē-bam* would first have meant something like "I was a-seeing" (ag1903 §168, footnote 2). The details of this history are debated among historical linguists (Weiss; Sihler), but the root is agreed. The idea fits the meaning well: "was ... -ing" is exactly what the tense says.

## How it is built

The imperfect is built on the present stem, the second principal part minus *-re* (lesson 1 of this part).

1. **Find the present stem.** *amā-*, *vidē-*, *rege-*, *cape-*, *audī-*.
2. **Adjust the stem vowel.** First and second conjugations keep their long vowel: *amā-*, *vidē-*. The third conjugation lengthens its short *e* to *ē*: *regē-* (ag1903 §179c). The -iō verbs and the fourth conjugation have *iē*: *capiē-*, *audiē-* (ag1903 §179c-d).
3. **Add *-bā-*,** the imperfect marker.
4. **Add the person ending.** The first person singular is *-m*, not *-ō*: *amābam* "I was loving". The other five are the endings you know: *-s*, *-t*, *-mus*, *-tis*, *-nt*.
5. **Check the length of *ā*.** It is shortened before *-m*, *-t* and *-nt*, the same rule as in part 2 (ag1903 §167): *amābam*, *amābat*, *amābant*, but *amābās*, *amābāmus*, *amābātis*.

Say "the slaves were working in the fields". The verb is *labōrō, labōrāre*: stem *labōrā-*. Add *-bā-*, then *-nt* for "they", with *a* short before *nt*: *labōrābant*. *Servī in agrīs labōrābant.*

Say "I used to read books". *Legō, legere*: stem *lege-*, lengthened to *legē-*. Add *-bā-* and *-m*: *legēbam*. *Librōs legēbam.*

**Sum and its family.** *Sum* has its own imperfect with no *-bā-*: *eram, erās, erat, erāmus, erātis, erant*, "I was" and so on. The root *es-* became *er-* between vowels (ag1903 §170): rhotacism again, as in the infinitive (lesson 3 of this part). *Possum* follows *sum*: *poteram, poterās, poterat*. *Volō*, *nōlō* and *mālō* are regular in this tense: *volēbam*, *nōlēbam*, *mālēbam*.

The fourth conjugation once had a shorter imperfect in *-ībam*. Allen and Greenough list it as an old form found chiefly in poetry (ag1903 §183). Classical prose uses *audiēbam*.

## Where it applies

Allen and Greenough list the imperfect's uses (ag1903 §§470-471). Each is a present use moved into the past.

- **Action in progress at a past moment.** *Mīlitēs dormiēbant*, "the soldiers were sleeping" (when something else happened).
- **Habit or repetition.** *Librōs saepe legēbam*, "I used to read". Words like *saepe*, *semper* and *quondam* "once, formerly" go naturally with it.
- **Description and background.** *Āra vetus stābat*; *Erant itinera duo*. In narrative, the imperfect paints the scene and the perfect (lesson 6) moves the story.
- **A state that lasted.** *Rēx populum regēbat*; *Hī agrōs habēbant*. Allen and Greenough note that *rēx erat* "describes the condition", while the perfect *rēx fuit* "only states it" (ag1903 §470, note).
- **Action going on for some time before a past moment.** With *iam diū* "for a long time now", the imperfect is translated by English "had been": *iam diū labōrābat*, "he had been working for a long time" (ag1903 §471b). This mirrors the present with *diū* in part 2.
- **An attempt or a beginning.** *Fugiēbant* can be "they tried to flee" or "they began to flee" (ag1903 §471c). Context decides.
- **"Could not", "would not" over a stretch of time.** *Capere nōn poterant*, "they could not take it" (ag1903 §471f).
- **A discovery.** In comedy, *tū hīc erās!* means "so you're here!": the person was there all along (ag1903 §471d). Rare outside dialogue.
- **Letters.** A Roman writing a letter sometimes puts himself at the reader's time and uses the imperfect for "I am writing" (ag1903 §479). You will meet this in the readings.

## Watch out

**Do not use the imperfect for a single completed event.** "Caesar took the city" in a narrative of what happened is not *\*Caesar urbem capiēbat*, which would mean "was taking" or "tried to take". The plain event is the perfect (lesson 6). The Spanish and French imperfects (*cantaba*, *chantait*) are close to the Latin in meaning, so trust that instinct.

**Do not write *-bō* in the first person.** *\*Amābō* is not "I was loving"; it is the future "I shall love" (lesson 5 of this part). The imperfect "I" ends in *-m*: *amābam*.

**Do not use *-bā-* with *sum*.** *\*Esbam* and *\*sumbam* are not words. *Sum* has *eram*, and *possum* has *poteram*, not *\*possēbam*.

**Do not keep the short *e* in the third conjugation.** *\*Regebam* with a short *e* is wrong. The third conjugation has *ē* before *-bā-*: *regēbam* (ag1903 §179c). The stress shows it: *re·GĒ·bam*.

**Do not drop the *i* of -iō and fourth-conjugation verbs.** *\*Capēbam* and *\*audēbam* are wrong. Both have *iē*: *capiēbam*, *audiēbam*. (*Audēbam* exists, but it is from *audeō* "dare".)

**Do not lengthen *a* before *-m*, *-t*, *-nt*.** *\*Amābāt* and *\*amābānt* break the shortening rule; say *amābat*, *amābant*.

**Do not translate every imperfect as "was ...-ing".** English "used to", "would" (of habit), "kept ...-ing", "began to", "tried to", "could" and a plain past all render it, depending on the sentence. *Rōmānī deōs colēbant* is best "the Romans worshipped the gods".

**English "was" with an adjective is usually *erat*, not a form of a verb.** "The road was long" is *Via longa erat*. Do not look for a separate verb; *sum* in the imperfect does the work.

## Connections

Spanish and French kept the imperfect and its meaning: Latin *amābam*, Spanish *amaba*, Italian *amavo*, and Spanish *-aba* still has the Latin *b*. French *aimait* has lost it. Japanese says "was doing" with a helping verb, *-te ita*; Latin builds the same meaning into one form with *-bā-*.

## Exercises

1. Build the imperfect of *pugnō*, *moneō*, *mittō*, *fugiō* and *sentiō* in the third person plural. Explain each stem vowel.
2. Translate *Quondam in hāc vīllā habitābāmus; senex agrōs colēbat et pecus alēbat.*
3. Say "the river was flowing through the fields".
4. Say "we could not see the city", using *possum*.
5. Take apart *claudēbātis* and *eram*.
6. Translate *Iam diū flēbat*, and explain why English needs "had been".
7. Correct *\*Puerī in viā currēbānt* and *\*Hostēs fugēbant*.
8. Say "the poet used to be wretched", and explain why the adjective is nominative and masculine.
9. Why does the imperfect marker contain a *b*? Give the explanation and say how certain it is.

## Worked answers

1. *Pugnābant*: first conjugation keeps *ā*. *Monēbant*: second keeps *ē*. *Mittēbant*: third lengthens its short *e* to *ē* (ag1903 §179c). *Fugiēbant*: -iō verbs have *iē*. *Sentiēbant*: fourth conjugation has *iē* too.
2. "Once we lived in this farmhouse; the old man farmed the fields and raised the herd." *Hāc* is ablative of *hic* after *in* (part 3, lesson 12). *Pecus* is neuter, so its accusative is the same as its nominative.
3. *Flūmen per agrōs fluēbat.* *Fluō, fluere*: third conjugation, stem *flue-*, lengthened to *fluē-*, plus *-ba-t*.
4. *Urbem vidēre nōn poterāmus.* *Poterāmus* is *pot-* plus *erāmus*; the infinitive *vidēre* stays as it is (lesson 3 of this part).
5. *Claudē-bā-tis*: third conjugation (*claudō, claudere*), imperfect, second person plural, "you (more than one) were shutting"; the *ā* is long before *-tis*. *Eram*: imperfect of *sum*, first person singular, "I was"; no *-bā-*, and *er-* from older *es-* (ag1903 §170).
6. "She had been weeping for a long time." The weeping began before the past moment of the story and was still going on then, so English uses "had been"; Latin uses the imperfect (ag1903 §471b).
7. *Puerī in viā currēbant*: the *a* is short before *-nt*. *Hostēs fugiēbant*: an -iō verb keeps its *i*: *fugiē-*.
8. *Poēta quondam miser erat.* *Miser* is nominative because after *erat* the adjective describes the subject (part 2, lesson 5); masculine because *poēta* is masculine (part 2, lesson 7).
9. The *b* goes back to the Indo-European root *\*bʰuH-* "be, become", the root of *fuī* (Wiktionary, *-bam*). Allen and Greenough think the marker was once a separate verb, "I was", joined to a noun-like form of the main verb (ag1903 §168). The root is agreed; how exactly the form was put together is debated (Weiss; Sihler), so treat that part as a likely explanation, not a fact.
