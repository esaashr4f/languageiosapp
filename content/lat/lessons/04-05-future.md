## The idea

The **future** is the tense for what will happen: "I shall love", "they will rule". English builds it with a helping word, "will" or "shall". Latin builds it into the verb, like the imperfect. But Latin has two different futures, and which one a verb takes depends on its conjugation.

- The first and second conjugations add *-bi-*: *amā-bi-t* "he will love", *vidē-bi-t* "he will see".
- The third and fourth conjugations change the vowel before the ending to *ē*: *reg-e-t* "he will rule", *audi-e-t* "he will hear". The first person singular has *a*: *regam* "I shall rule".

Why two? Because the two futures have different origins, and each group of verbs kept the one it had.

The *b* future is a sister of the imperfect. Wiktionary derives both *-bam* and *-bō* from the Indo-European root *\*bʰuH-* "be, become", the root of *fuī*. Allen and Greenough explain *-bō* as once a present of that root with a future meaning, "I am to be", added to a noun-like form of the verb, just as *-bam* "I was" was added for the imperfect (ag1903 §168, footnotes 2-3). So *amābō* and *amābam* differ in the same way as "is to be" and "was".

The *ē* future is older and quite different. Allen and Greenough explain that its endings "are really Subjunctive endings used in a future sense" (ag1903 §168d). The subjunctive is the mood of what is wished or expected (lesson 17 of this part); a form meaning "I may rule" or "I am to rule" came to mean "I shall rule". Its vowel was once long throughout and was shortened before *-m*, *-t* and *-nt* (ag1903 §§167, 168d).

Some fourth-conjugation verbs once used the *b* future too: older Latin has *scībō* "I shall know" and *audībō* "I shall hear". Wiktionary and Allen and Greenough treat these as old forms, found mostly in early writers and poetry (ag1903 §183). In classical prose the fourth conjugation goes with the third: *sciam*, *audiam*.

## How it is built

**First and second conjugations.**

1. Take the present stem with its long vowel: *amā-*, *vidē-*.
2. Add the future marker. It looks like a little third-conjugation verb: *-b-* before *-ō*, *-bi-* before *-s*, *-t*, *-mus*, *-tis*, and *-bu-* before *-nt*. (Wiktionary notes that it runs exactly like the present of the third conjugation, *reg-ō, reg-is, reg-unt*.)
3. Add the ending: *amābō, amābis, amābit, amābimus, amābitis, amābunt*.

**Third and fourth conjugations.**

1. Take the stem without its final vowel: *reg-*, *capi-*, *audi-*. The -iō verbs and the fourth conjugation keep their *i*.
2. In the first person singular add *-am*: *regam*, *capiam*, *audiam*.
3. In every other person add *ē* plus the ending, with *ē* shortened before *-t* and *-nt*: *regēs, reget, regēmus, regētis, regent*.

Say "the general will lead the army". *Dūcō, dūcere* is third conjugation, so the future has *ē*. The subject is third person singular, and the vowel is short before *-t*: *dūcet*. *Dux exercitum dūcet.*

Say "we shall praise the soldiers". *Laudō, laudāre* is first conjugation, so the future has *-bi-*: *laudābimus*. *Mīlitēs laudābimus.*

**Sum and its family.** The future of *sum* is *erō, eris, erit, erimus, eritis, erunt*: *er-* from *es-* again (ag1903 §170), with endings like the *-bō* future without the *b*. *Possum* follows it: *poterō, poteris, poterit*. *Volō*, *nōlō* and *mālō* take the *ē* future: *volam, volēs, volet*; *nōlam, nōlēs*; *mālam, mālēs*.

## Where it applies

- **What will happen.** *Dux exercitum dūcet* (ag1903 §472).
- **A promise or a threat.** *Crās labōrābō*; *Hostēs fugient*.
- **A polite or firm instruction.** The future can stand for a command, as English "you will report to me tomorrow" (ag1903 §472a). *Hoc faciēs*, "you will do this". Commands proper come in lesson 9 of this part.
- **Inside clauses about the future, where English uses a present.** English says "when he arrives, he will see"; Latin puts both verbs in the future: "when he will arrive, he will see" (ag1903 §472b). You will meet this with clauses of time and condition in part 6. The future perfect (lesson 8) does the same job for an event completed first.
- **A general truth seen as always about to be true.** *Rōma semper erit.*
- **Not for what you want to do now.** "I'll help you" as an offer is a future, *tē iuvābō*. But "will you help me?" asking about willingness is better *vīsne mē iuvāre?*, with *volō* (lesson 3).
- **The present for the near future.** In everyday speech the present can stand for what is about to happen (part 2, lesson 1). The future states it.

## Watch out

**Do not give the third conjugation a *b*.** *\*Regēbit*, *\*dūcēbit* and *\*capiēbit* are not Latin. The *b* future belongs to the first and second conjugations; the third has *reget*, *dūcet*, *capiet*.

**Do not give the first conjugation an *ē*.** *\*Amet* is not "he will love". It is a real form, but it is the present subjunctive "let him love" (lesson 17). The future is *amābit*.

**Do not confuse the third-conjugation future with the second-conjugation present.** *Regēs* "you will rule" and *vidēs* "you see" have the same ending. Only the conjugation tells you the tense: *regō* is third, so *regēs* is future; *videō* is second, so *vidēs* is present. This is the strongest reason to learn the principal parts.

**Do not confuse future and present in the third conjugation.** *Regit* "he rules" and *reget* "he will rule"; *vīvis* "you live" and *vīvēs* "you will live". Without macrons, *regis* and *reges* still differ by *i* and *e*. Read the vowel.

**Do not mistake *legēs* for *lēgēs*.** *Legēs* (short *e*, from *legō*) is "you will read". *Lēgēs* (long *ē*, from *lēx*) is "laws". The macron is the only difference.

**Do not use *-am* outside the first person singular.** *\*Regam* is "I shall rule", not "he will rule". The other persons all have *ē*: *reget*, *regent*. The first person *-am* also looks like a first-declension accusative: *regam* is a verb, *puellam* a noun.

**Do not use the present after "when" and "if" about the future.** English "if you come, I shall be glad" has a present; Latin uses the future in both halves (ag1903 §472b; part 6).

**Do not use the future for "will" meaning willingness.** English "he won't listen" often means "he refuses to listen": *audīre nōn vult* (lesson 3 of this part). *Nōn audiet* is a plain prediction, "he will not hear". Choose by the meaning, not the English word.

## Connections

Neither Latin future survived into Spanish or French. Wiktionary notes that Late Latin built a new future from the infinitive plus the present of *habeō*, "I have to sing", and that this became the Romance future: Spanish *cantaré*, French *chanterai* still contain the old *habeō*. Japanese uses its plain non-past form for most futures, much as Latin can use the present for what is about to happen.

## Exercises

1. Give the future of *nūntiō*, *doceō*, *mittō*, *rapiō* and *veniō* in the third person plural. Say which pattern each takes and why.
2. Translate *Crās Rōmam veniēmus et cōnsulem vidēbimus.*
3. Say "the king will spare the citizens", using *parcō* with the dative.
4. Tense and person of *dīcēs*, *dīcis*, *movēs*, *movēbis*. Translate each.
5. Say "I shall be happy, but you will be sad", using *miser* for "sad".
6. Correct *\*Hostēs urbem capiēbunt* ("the enemy will take the city") and *\*Mīlitēs pugnent* ("the soldiers will fight").
7. Why does the third conjugation use *ē*, not *b*, for its future? Give the source's explanation.
8. Translate *Fāta nōn mūtābis*, and explain *mūtābis*.
9. Say "we shall be able to read the letters".

## Worked answers

1. *Nūntiābunt* and *docēbunt*: first and second conjugation, so the *b* future, *-bu-* before *-nt*. *Mittent*, *rapient* and *venient*: third, -iō and fourth, so the *ē* future, short before *-nt*; *rapiō* and *veniō* keep their *i*.
2. "Tomorrow we shall come to Rome and see the consul." *Rōmam*: goal of motion with a town name, no preposition (part 3, lesson 13). *Veni-ē-mus*: fourth-conjugation future. *Vidē-bi-mus*: second-conjugation future.
3. *Rēx cīvibus parcet.* *Parcō* takes the dative (*cīvibus*, part 3, lesson 8). Third conjugation, so *parc-e-t*.
4. *Dīcēs*: future, "you will say". *Dīcis*: present, "you say". *Movēs*: present, "you move" (second conjugation, where *ē* is the stem vowel). *Movēbis*: future, "you will move".
5. *Ego laetus erō, tū autem miser eris.* *Erō* and *eris* are futures of *sum*. *Ego* and *tū* are stated because the two people are contrasted (part 3, lesson 10). *Autem* stands second (part 2, lesson 8).
6. *Hostēs urbem capient*: *capiō* is third conjugation -iō, so the *ē* future, with no *b*. *Mīlitēs pugnābunt*: *pugnō* is first conjugation, so the *b* future; *pugnent* is a subjunctive (lesson 17).
7. Allen and Greenough explain that the third- and fourth-conjugation future endings were originally subjunctive endings used with a future meaning (ag1903 §168d). The *b* future was a separate formation, built with the root of *fuī* (Wiktionary, *-bō*), and it took hold only in the first and second conjugations.
8. "You will not change fate." *Mūtā-bi-s*: first conjugation, so the *b* future, second person singular. *Fāta* is the neuter plural of *fātum*, accusative.
9. *Epistulās legere poterimus.* *Poterimus* is *pot-* plus *erimus*. *Litterae* would also do for "a letter" (part 3).
