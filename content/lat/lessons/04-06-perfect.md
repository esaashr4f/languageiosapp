## The idea

The imperfect (lesson 4 of this part) looks at a past action from inside, while it was going on. The **perfect** looks at it from outside, as one completed whole: "he took the city", "she has finished". Its name means "completed". It is the tense that tells what happened, and it is the commonest past tense in Latin narrative.

The perfect differs from every tense so far in two ways.

First, it is not built on the present stem. It has its own stem, the **perfect stem**, which you find in the third principal part: *amāvī* gives *amāv-*, *rēxī* gives *rēx-*, *cēpī* gives *cēp-* (lesson 1 of this part). Lesson 1 showed why: the perfect stem is made in several different ways, because the Latin perfect continues two older categories of the parent language, a past tense (the aorist) and a perfect, which merged (Weiss; Sihler). Allen and Greenough trace the *s*-perfect to the aorist (ag1903 §177, footnote 2) and the *-ī* of "I" to a perfect tense sign (§163, footnote 3).

Second, it has its own person endings. They are not *-ō, -s, -t* but *-ī, -istī, -it, -imus, -istis, -ērunt*. Allen and Greenough say these "special terminations" are partly of different origin from the others (ag1903 §163):

- the *-ī* of "I" is not a person ending in origin but an old tense sign of the Indo-European perfect;
- in *-istī* and *-istis*, the *-tī* and *-tis* are person endings, and the *-is-* belongs to the same element seen in the perfect infinitive *amāv-isse* (ag1903 §169c, note);
- in *-it* and *-imus*, *-t* and *-mus* are the ordinary endings, and the *i* "is of uncertain origin";
- *-ērunt* and its variant *-ēre* are "of doubtful origin", though *-ērunt* contains the ordinary *-nt*.

So the perfect endings are a patchwork from two sources, like the perfect stems. The parts that match the present endings (*-t*, *-mus*, *-nt*) make them easier to learn.

## How it is built

The method is the same for every verb, regular or not.

1. **Find the perfect stem.** Take the third principal part and remove the *-ī*: *amāvī* → *amāv-*; *mōvī* → *mōv-*; *dīxī* → *dīx-*; *cēpī* → *cēp-*; *audīvī* → *audīv-*. You cannot work it out from the present; it has to be learned with the verb.
2. **Add the perfect ending for the person.**

   - "I" *-ī*, "you" (one) *-istī*, "he, she, it" *-it*;
   - "we" *-imus*, "you" (more than one) *-istis*, "they" *-ērunt* (or *-ēre*).
3. **Nothing else changes.** There are no conjugation differences in the perfect: once you have the stem, *amāv-*, *mon-u-*, *rēx-*, *cēp-* and *audīv-* all take the same endings.

Say "the enemy fled". *Fugiō, fugere, fūgī*: the perfect stem is *fūg-*, with a long *ū* (the root vowel lengthened, lesson 1). "They" takes *-ērunt*: *fūgērunt*. *Hostēs fūgērunt.*

Say "you (one person) have written a letter". *Scrībō, scrībere, scrīpsī*: stem *scrīps-*. Second person singular, *-istī*: *scrīpsistī*. *Epistulam scrīpsistī.*

**Sum and its family.** The perfect of *sum* comes from a different root, the one in *-bam* and *-bō*: *fuī, fuistī, fuit, fuimus, fuistis, fuērunt* (ag1903 §170, note). *Possum* has *potuī*, *volō* has *voluī*, *nōlō* *nōluī*, *mālō* *māluī*: all regular from there.

**Shortened forms.** In perfects with *v* between vowels, the *v* and the syllable after it often drop out (ag1903 §181):

- *amāvistī* → *amāstī*, *amāvistis* → *amāstis*, *amāvērunt* → *amārunt*;
- fourth-conjugation *audīvī* often loses its *v*: *audiī*, *audiit*, *audiērunt*; and *audīvistī* → *audīstī*.

You will meet these in poetry and in prose. The full forms are never wrong.

**The third person plural in *-ēre*.** *Amāvēre* means the same as *amāvērunt*. Allen and Greenough call both of doubtful origin (ag1903 §163, footnote 3). Poets use *-ēre* freely. Do not mistake *-ēre* for an infinitive: *amāvēre* has the perfect stem.

## Where it applies

Allen and Greenough give the uses (ag1903 §§161, 473-476). Lesson 7 of this part shows how to choose between the perfect and the imperfect; here is the full range.

- **A single past event: "did".** *Hostēs fūgērunt*; *Mīlitēs portam frēgērunt*. Allen and Greenough call this the historical perfect, matching the English simple past and the Greek aorist (ag1903 §161).
- **A completed act whose result holds now: "have done".** *Epistulam scrīpsistī*, "you have written a letter" (it is written). This is the perfect proper, the English perfect with "have" (ag1903 §473).
- **A long but finished stretch of time.** *Rēx urbem diū rēxit*: the perfect sums up the whole reign. The length does not make it imperfect; what matters is that the speaker sees it as complete.
- **Something that has ceased to be.** *Fuit ista virtūs*; *Fuimus Trōēs* (ag1903 §474). The perfect of *sum* in particular can mean "is no more".
- **A general truth, often with a negative.** Latin can say "no one ever won without effort" in the perfect, meaning it never happens (ag1903 §475). This "gnomic" perfect is rare in prose.
- **Verbs whose perfect has a present meaning.** *Nōvī*, from *nōscō* "get to know", means "I have got to know", that is, "I know"; *ōdī* "I hate" and *meminī* "I remember" have only perfect forms (ag1903 §476). Lesson 15 of this part covers them.
- **The perfect stem builds more.** The pluperfect and future perfect (lesson 8), the perfect infinitive *amāvisse* "to have loved" (part 6), and the perfect and pluperfect subjunctive (lesson 18) all use it.

## Watch out

**Do not put present endings on the perfect stem.** *\*Amāvō*, *\*amāvs*, *\*amāvunt* are not words. The perfect has its own set: *amāvī*, *amāvistī*, *amāvērunt*. Only *-it* and *-imus* look like present endings.

**Do not put perfect endings on the present stem.** *\*Fugiī*, *\*regērunt* and *\*scrībistī* mix the two systems. Find the perfect stem first: *fūgī*, *rēxērunt*, *scrīpsistī*.

**Do not confuse *-it* in the present and the perfect.** *Venit* "he comes" and *vēnit* "he came"; *legit* "he reads" and *lēgit* "he read"; *fugit* "he flees" and *fūgit* "he fled". Third conjugation and -iō verbs often differ only by the length of the root vowel. In *regit* and *rēxit*, *capit* and *cēpit*, the stem tells you. In a text without macrons, the context must decide *venit*, *legit*, *fugit*.

**Do not read *-ēre* as an infinitive.** *Fulsēre*, *amāvēre*, *fuēre* have the perfect stem and mean "they shone", "they loved", "they were". The present infinitive is built on the present stem: *fulgēre*, *amāre*, *esse*.

**Do not form the second person singular as *-ist* or *-isti* without the long *ī*.** It is *-istī*: *amāvistī*, *fuistī*. The contracted *amāstī* is a real alternative; *\*amāvist* is not.

**Do not assume that a first-conjugation verb has *-āvī*.** *Dō, dedī*; *stō, stetī*; *iuvō, iūvī*; *vetō, vetuī* (lesson 1 of this part). Always check the third principal part.

**Do not confuse *cecidī* and *cecīdī*.** *Cecidī* (short *i*) is from *cadō* "fall"; *cecīdī* (long *ī*) is from *caedō* "cut, kill". The length is all that separates "I fell" from "I killed".

**Do not translate every perfect with English "have".** In a story, "he has fled, he has seen" is wrong: *fūgit* is "he fled". Use "have" only where the result still holds at the time of speaking.

## Connections

Spanish and French simple pasts descend from the Latin perfect: Spanish *amé* and French *aimai* go back to *amāvī* (Wiktionary, Spanish *-é* and French *-ai*, from Latin *-āvī*). Irregular perfects survive too: Spanish *hice* "I did" and *dije* "I said" continue *fēcī* and *dīxī* (Wiktionary). Greek kept the two categories apart that Latin merged: an aorist for "did" and a perfect for "have done".

## Exercises

1. Give the perfect stem and the third person singular perfect of *mittō*, *pōnō*, *faciō*, *videō*, *maneō* and *dō*.
2. Translate *Victor urbem cēpit et mūrōs frēgit.*
3. Say "the citizens heard the voice and ran to the gate".
4. Take apart *pepulērunt*, *fefellistī* and *nōvimus*.
5. Say "we stayed in the city for a long time" with *maneō*.
6. Correct *\*Hostēs fugērunt* and *\*Scrībistis epistulās*.
7. What does *amārunt* stand for? Explain the shortening.
8. Translate *Parentēs tuī fuērunt clārī* and say what the perfect of *sum* adds.
9. Why does the perfect have its own endings? Say what the sources know and what they do not.

## Worked answers

1. *Mittō, mīsī*: stem *mīs-*, *mīsit*. *Pōnō, posuī*: *posu-*, *posuit*. *Faciō, fēcī*: *fēc-*, *fēcit*. *Videō, vīdī*: *vīd-*, *vīdit*. *Maneō, mānsī*: *māns-*, *mānsit*. *Dō, dedī*: *ded-*, *dedit*.
2. "The conqueror took the city and broke the walls." *Cēp-it* and *frēg-it*: perfect stems with a lengthened, changed root vowel (*capiō, cēpī*; *frangō, frēgī*).
3. *Cīvēs vōcem audīvērunt et ad portam cucurrērunt.* *Audīv-ērunt*: regular fourth-conjugation perfect. *Cucurr-ērunt*: *currō, cucurrī*, a reduplicated perfect.
4. *Pepul-ērunt*: *pellō, pepulī*, "they drove away", reduplicated. *Fefell-istī*: *fallō, fefellī*, "you deceived", reduplicated. *Nōv-imus*: *nōscō, nōvī*, "we have got to know", so "we know" (ag1903 §476).
5. *Diū in urbe mānsimus.* *Māns-imus*: the s-perfect of *maneō*. The stay is long but seen as finished, so perfect (see lesson 7).
6. *Hostēs fūgērunt*: the perfect stem has a long *ū*; *fugērunt* is not a form. *Scrīpsistis epistulās*: the perfect stem is *scrīps-*, from the third principal part *scrīpsī*.
7. *Amāvērunt*. Between vowels, the *v* and the following vowel drop out and the vowels contract (ag1903 §181a): *amā(vē)runt* → *amārunt*.
8. "Your parents were famous." The perfect states the fact as finished, and it can suggest that they are no longer alive or no longer famous (ag1903 §474). The imperfect *erant* would describe how things stood at the time.
9. The perfect continues two older categories, and its endings were assembled from different sources. Allen and Greenough explain the *-ī* of "I" as an old perfect tense sign and the *-tī*, *-tis* as person endings, but call the *i* of *-it* and *-imus* "of uncertain origin" and *-ērunt* and *-ēre* "of doubtful origin" (ag1903 §163). The full history is not settled.
