## The idea

You now have two past tenses, and English gives you no help in choosing between them. English "he ruled" can be *regēbat* or *rēxit*. The choice is not about when the action happened. Both are past. It is about how the speaker presents it.

That difference has a name: **aspect**, the way a verb form presents the inside of an action, as one complete whole or as something going on. The perfect presents an action whole, from outside, with its end in view: "he ruled (and that was that)". The imperfect presents it from inside, as going on, as repeated, or as the state of things, with no end in view: "he was ruling", "he used to rule", "he was king (at the time)". Allen and Greenough describe the imperfect as "a descriptive tense" that shows "an action conceived as in progress or a state of things as actually observed", while the perfect "merely states the fact" (ag1903 §470).

The same real event can be told either way. *Rēx erat* and *rēx fuit* can both be true of the same man; "the former describes the condition while the latter only states it" (ag1903 §470, note). So the question is never "which is correct for this fact?" but "what am I doing with it in this sentence?"

Spanish and French make the same choice, in much the same way. Spanish *reinaba* and *reinó*, French *il régnait* and *il régna*, divide the past as *regēbat* and *rēxit* do. If you know which Spanish tense you would use, you usually know the Latin one.

How central aspect is to the Latin verb is a matter of debate. The Latin perfect continues both an old past tense and an old perfect (Weiss; Sihler), so it carries both "did" and "have done", and some grammarians organise the whole verb system round aspect while others treat it mainly as a system of tenses (pinkster2015). This guide teaches the practical choice, which all agree on, and leaves the theory open.

## How it is built

There are no new forms in this lesson. There is a decision to make, in steps.

1. **Is it an event that moves the story on?** "Then he did X." Use the perfect. A run of perfects is a run of events, one after another: *apparāvit, suscēpit, cōnfēcit*.
2. **Is it the background to an event?** What was going on, what things were like, who was there. Use the imperfect. *Mīlitēs dormiēbant* (the scene); *subitō hostēs portam frēgērunt* (what happened).
3. **Is it repeated?** If it happened as a habit, with no count, use the imperfect: *saepe pugnābat*, "he used to fight often". If it is counted or summed up as a fact, use the perfect: *ter pugnāvit*, "he fought three times".
4. **Did it last a long time?** Length alone does not decide. If the stretch is seen as finished and measured, use the perfect: *multōs annōs rēgnum tenuit*, "he held the throne for many years". If it is the state of affairs at the time of the story, use the imperfect: *rēgnum tenēbat*, "he was on the throne".
5. **Is it "have done", a result that holds now?** Use the perfect. This is its second sense, the English perfect: *epistulam scrīpsī*, "I have written the letter".

**Reading the two senses of the perfect.** The perfect form *scrīpsī* means both "I wrote" and "I have written" (ag1903 §161). Latin does not separate them; English must. Look at the context:

- In a story about the past, or with a past time word (*ōlim*, *tum*, *quondam*), it is "did".
- In speech about the present situation, or with *iam* "already", *nunc*, *hodiē*, it is often "have done".
- With *nōndum* "not yet" or *numquam* "never" about one's life so far, it is "have not done", "have never done".

Say "the enemy were attacking the camp, but the soldiers defended it". The attack is the situation; the defence is what happened. *Hostēs castra petēbant, sed mīlitēs ea dēfendērunt.*

## Where it applies

- **Narrative.** Every story in Latin alternates the two: imperfects for the setting and for what was going on, perfects for the events. Caesar's *Gallic War* (part 8) is the clearest model.
- **Description of a place or a people.** Imperfect throughout: *Erant itinera duo*; *Hī agrōs habēbant* (lesson 4 of this part).
- **Habits and customs of the past.** Imperfect: *Rōmānī deōs colēbant*.
- **A fact summed up.** Perfect, even of something long or repeated: *diū rēxit*, *ter pugnāvit*, *multōs annōs tenuit*.
- **A state that has ended.** Perfect, often emphatic: *Fuimus Trōēs* (lesson 6; ag1903 §474).
- **"Have done", about now.** Perfect: *Iam scrīpsī*.
- **With a negative.** Allen and Greenough note that the perfect is often used in negative statements where a positive one would take the imperfect (ag1903 §475a). But the imperfect with *nōn* is the normal way to say "could not", "would not" over a period: *capere nōn poterant* (ag1903 §471f).
- **A state that began at a point.** Some perfects describe the result of a change: *nōvī* "I have learnt", so "I know" (lesson 6). Here the perfect behaves like a present.
- **Variation by author.** Writers differ. Comedy uses the imperfect in conversation where later prose would use the perfect (ag1903 §471e), and historians use the vivid present for events as well (part 2, lesson 1). The rule of thumb is a tendency, not a law (pinkster2015).

## Watch out

**Do not choose by English "was ...-ing" alone.** English "he lived in Rome for ten years" has no "-ing", but if it describes the setting of a story it is *Rōmae habitābat*; if it sums up a finished period it is *decem annōs Rōmae habitāvit*. Decide by what the sentence is doing.

**Do not use the imperfect for a counted repetition.** *\*Ter pugnābat* sounds natural after "he would fight", but a counted series is summed up: *ter pugnāvit*.

**Do not use the perfect for scene-setting.** *\*Mīlitēs dormīvērunt; subitō hostēs portam frēgērunt* turns the sleep into an event: "the soldiers slept (and then woke up)". For the background, *dormiēbant*.

**Do not translate every perfect with "have".** In a narrative, *\*Caesar has come, has seen, has conquered* is wrong English for *vēnit, vīdit, vīcit*. Use "have" only where the result still matters at the time of speaking.

**Do not look for a separate "have done" form.** Spanish has *he cantado* beside *canté*, French *j'ai chanté* beside *je chantai*. Latin has one form, *cantāvī*, for both. Do not try to build *\*habeō cantātum*; it is not classical Latin.

**Do not read every *fuit* as a plain "was".** Latin also uses the perfect of *sum* for something that no longer exists (ag1903 §474). *Fuit rēx* can mean "he was king once, and is no longer". Check the context before translating.

**Do not switch tense without a reason.** In a passage, each change from imperfect to perfect means a change from background to event. Read the changes as signals.

## Connections

Spanish *pretérito* and *imperfecto*, French *passé simple* and *imparfait*, continue the Latin perfect and imperfect, and they divide the past in nearly the same way. Japanese and Korean have no grammatical counterpart to this split in the past; they mark an action in progress with a helping verb (Japanese *-te ita*, Korean *-go isseotda*) when they need to.

## Exercises

1. Choose the tense and translate: "The Romans were waiting in the camp. Suddenly the enemy attacked." Use *exspectō* and *petō*.
2. Translate *Ter cōnsul fuit*, and explain why the perfect is used.
3. Translate *Cōnsul plēbem saepe dēfendēbat*, then change it to say he defended them once, and translate.
4. Translate *Iam vōcem tuam audīvī*, and say which sense of the perfect it has.
5. Say "the slaves were dragging the ship; then they sat down".
6. *Hostēs tēla iaciēbant.* Give two possible English translations.
7. Correct the tense: *\*Caesar exercitum cōgēbat et statim Rōmam properāvit*, meant as "Caesar collected an army and at once hurried to Rome".
8. Why can *rēx erat* and *rēx fuit* both describe the same man? Use the source.

## Worked answers

1. *Rōmānī in castrīs exspectābant. Subitō hostēs petīvērunt.* The waiting is the scene, so imperfect; the attack is the event, so perfect (*petō, petīvī*).
2. "He was consul three times." The number sums up the repetition as a fact, so perfect, *fuit* (step 3).
3. "The consul often used to defend the common people": a habit, imperfect. *Cōnsul plēbem semel dēfendit*, "the consul defended the common people once": a counted event, perfect (*dēfendō, dēfendī*; *dēfendit* is also the present, so *semel* and the context decide).
4. "I have already heard your voice." *Iam* and the present situation make it the "have done" sense (ag1903 §161).
5. *Servī nāvem trahēbant; deinde sēdērunt.* The dragging is going on, so imperfect; sitting down is the next event, so perfect (*sedeō, sēdī*).
6. "The enemy were throwing spears" (in progress) or "the enemy kept throwing spears" (repeated). It could also be "began to throw" (ag1903 §471c).
7. *Caesar exercitum coēgit et statim Rōmam properāvit.* Both acts are events in sequence, so both perfect (*cōgō, coēgī*).
8. The imperfect describes the condition, the perfect only states it (ag1903 §470, note). The fact is the same; the speaker's view of it differs. That difference of view is aspect.
