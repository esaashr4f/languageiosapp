## The idea

The perfect says that an action is complete now. Two more tenses move that point of view. The **pluperfect** says that an action was complete at some moment in the past: "they *had* fled (when the cavalry arrived)". The **future perfect** says that an action will be complete at some moment in the future: "I *shall have* finished (by tomorrow)". Both are built on the perfect stem, so together with the perfect they make up the **perfect system**: the three tenses of completed action (lesson 1 of this part).

You already know the endings, in a sense. The pluperfect looks like the perfect stem plus *eram*, "I was": *amāv-eram*, "I had loved". The future perfect looks like the perfect stem plus *erō*, "I shall be": *amāv-erō*, "I shall have loved". The resemblance helps you learn them, and it is partly real.

Allen and Greenough explain the history this way (ag1903 §169b-c). The future perfect was originally a subjunctive of the old *s*-past (the aorist), something like *\*amāv-is-ō*. The *-is-* is the same element you saw in *amāv-istī* (lesson 6). Between vowels the *s* became *r* (rhotacism, lesson 3), giving *-erō*. The pluperfect was then formed on the analogy of the future perfect, from an older *\*-is-ām*, and it was pulled towards *eram* because *eram* stands to *erō* as the pluperfect stands to the future perfect. So the likeness to *sum* is partly inheritance and partly analogy. Allen and Greenough present these as probable explanations, not certainties.

One difference from *erō* matters. The third person plural of the future perfect is *-erint*, not *-erunt*: *amāverint* "they will have loved". *Amāvērunt*, with a long *ē* and *-unt*, is the perfect "they loved".

## How it is built

**Pluperfect.**

1. Find the perfect stem from the third principal part: *amāv-*, *rēx-*, *fūg-*, *fu-*.
2. Add *-era-* and the person ending, exactly like *eram, erās, erat*: *-eram, -erās, -erat, -erāmus, -erātis, -erant*.

**Future perfect.**

1. Find the perfect stem.
2. Add *-er-ō* in the first person singular, *-eri-* plus the ending elsewhere: *-erō, -eris, -erit, -erimus, -eritis, -erint*.

The pluperfect of *sum* is *fueram* "I had been"; the future perfect is *fuerō* "I shall have been". *Possum* has *potueram*, *potuerō*.

Say "the enemy had already fled". The action was complete before a past moment, so pluperfect. *Fugiō, fūgī*: stem *fūg-*. Third person plural: *fūgerant*. *Hostēs iam fūgerant.*

Say "I shall have done my duty". The action will be complete by a future moment, so future perfect. *Praestō, praestitī* "make good, perform": stem *praestit-*. First person singular: *praestiterō*. *Officium meum praestiterō.*

**Building the sentence with two events.** Most pluperfects and future perfects come in sentences with two events, one of them a clause beginning "when" or "if". Those clauses are taught in part 6. The rule for the tenses is simple, and you need it now to read:

- If the earlier event is past, put it in the pluperfect, the later one in the perfect or imperfect.
- If the earlier event is future, put it in the future perfect, the later one in the future. English uses a present or a perfect for the earlier one ("when he comes", "when he has come"); Latin uses the future perfect (ag1903 §478, note).

## Where it applies

**Pluperfect** (ag1903 §477):

- **An act complete before a past moment.** *Hostēs iam fūgerant* (when we arrived). Often with *iam* "already" or *anteā* "before".
- **The earlier of two past events.** *Epistulam lēgeram; tum tē vīdī.*
- **A state that had come about.** With verbs like *nōscō*, the pluperfect *nōveram* means "I knew" (I had got to know), as the perfect *nōvī* means "I know" (lesson 6; ag1903 §476).
- **Inside clauses of time, cause and relation in the past** (part 6): "the place which our men had chosen" is *quem locum nostrī dēlēgerant* (ag1903 §477).
- **Repeated earlier action** in sentences like "whenever he had given an order, he did not think it done" (ag1903 §477, part 2). Part 6 explains these clauses.
- **In letters,** for a past seen from the reader's time (ag1903 §479).

**Future perfect** (ag1903 §478):

- **An act complete by a future moment.** *Ante noctem labōrem cōnfēcerimus.*
- **The earlier of two future events.** *Cum vēnerit, canēmus*: Latin marks that the coming will be over before the singing. English says "when he comes" or "when he has come".
- **In "if" clauses about the future** (part 6): *sī vēneris, gaudēbō*, "if you come, I shall be glad". English uses the present.
- **Looking ahead to a final reckoning.** *Officium praestiterō*: "I shall turn out to have done my duty".
- **Instead of a plain future.** Allen and Greenough note the Romans' "fondness for representing an action as completed": *vīderō*, "I shall see (to it)", where English would say "I'll see" (ag1903 §478, note).
- **The future perfect is much commoner in Latin than in English** (ag1903 §478, note). When you translate into English, a present or perfect is often best.

## Watch out

**Do not use the future or the present for the earlier future event.** *\*Cum veniet, canēmus* or *\*cum venit, canēmus*, built from the English "when he comes", miss the point. The coming will be complete before the singing: *cum vēnerit, canēmus*. This is the commonest mistake with the future perfect.

**Do not write *-erunt* for "they will have".** *\*Amāverunt* is neither tense. "They will have loved" is *amāverint*; "they loved" is *amāvērunt*, with a long *ē*.

**Do not put *-erā-* on the present stem.** *\*Fugeram*, *\*regeram* and *\*amāram* from *amā-* mix the systems. The pluperfect is built on the perfect stem: *fūgeram*, *rēxeram*, *amāveram*. (A genuine contracted *amāram* does exist, from *amāveram*, ag1903 §181; it comes from the perfect stem.)

**Do not confuse *eram* and *fueram*.** *Eram* is "I was" (imperfect of *sum*); *fueram* is "I had been". Both contain *er-*, but only *fueram* has the perfect stem *fu-*.

**Do not use the pluperfect just because English has "had".** English "I had a book" is possession in the past, *librum habēbam*. Only "had" + past participle ("had read") is a pluperfect.

**Do not read every *-erit* as a future perfect.** The perfect subjunctive (lesson 18) has almost the same forms: *amāverit*, *amāverimus*. In main clauses of plain statement, *-erit* is the future perfect; the subjunctive shows itself by its use.

**Do not translate the future perfect word for word every time.** "I shall have come" is clumsy for *cum vēnerō*; English says "when I come". Translate by sense; build by the Latin rule.

## Connections

The Latin pluperfect lives on in Spanish, with a new meaning. Wiktionary derives the Spanish *-ra* forms (*amara*, *fuera*) from Latin *-eram*: the old pluperfect indicative became an imperfect subjunctive, "that I might love". French, Spanish and English all build their pluperfect and future perfect with a helping verb ("had", *había*, *avait*; "will have", *habrá*, *aura*); Latin does it with a single word.

## Exercises

1. Give the pluperfect and future perfect, third person singular, of *capiō*, *vincō*, *audiō* and *dō*.
2. Translate *Cīvēs iam urbem relīquerant.*
3. Say "the soldiers had opened the gate".
4. Say "you (one person) will have read the book before night".
5. Take apart *fuerint*, *fuērunt* and *fuerant*, and translate each.
6. Translate *Cum epistulam lēgerō, respondēbō*, and explain both tenses.
7. Correct *\*Cum veniēs, gaudēbō* ("when you come, I shall be glad").
8. Why does the pluperfect look like the perfect stem plus *eram*? Say what Allen and Greenough suggest.
9. Translate *Officium meum praestiterō*, and explain why a Roman might say it rather than *officium meum praestābō*.

## Worked answers

1. *Capiō, cēpī*: *cēperat*, *cēperit*. *Vincō, vīcī*: *vīcerat*, *vīcerit*. *Audiō, audīvī*: *audīverat*, *audīverit*. *Dō, dedī*: *dederat*, *dederit*. Each is built on the perfect stem from the third principal part.
2. "The citizens had already left the city." *Relīqu-era-nt*: *relinquō, relīquī*, pluperfect, third person plural.
3. *Mīlitēs portam aperuerant.* *Aperiō, aperuī*: stem *aperu-*, plus *-erant*.
4. *Ante noctem librum lēgeris.* *Legō, lēgī*: stem *lēg-*, plus *-eris*.
5. *Fu-erint*: future perfect, "they will have been". *Fu-ērunt*: perfect, "they were, they have been". *Fu-erant*: pluperfect, "they had been".
6. "When I have read the letter, I shall reply." The reading will be finished before the reply, so it is future perfect (*lēg-er-ō*); the reply is future (*respondēbō*, second conjugation, so the *b* future). English uses a perfect for the earlier event (ag1903 §478).
7. *Cum vēneris, gaudēbō.* The coming will be complete before the joy, so future perfect; English uses the present, Latin does not (ag1903 §478).
8. Allen and Greenough suggest that the future perfect began as *\*-is-ō*, a subjunctive of the old *s*-past, with *s* turning to *r* between vowels; and that the pluperfect was formed on its model, from *\*-is-ām*, and drawn towards *eram* because *eram* is to *erō* as pluperfect is to future perfect (ag1903 §169b-c).
9. "I shall have done my duty." The future perfect looks ahead to the moment of judgement: whatever happens, he will be found to have done it. Latin likes to present an action as completed (ag1903 §478, note). *Praestābō* would only promise to do it.
