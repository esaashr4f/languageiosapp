## The idea

Every form so far has stated something: "he loves", "they will rule". A command does not state; it tells. Latin has a separate set of forms for this, the **imperative**: the form of the verb used to give a command or make a request. Grammars call the set of forms a **mood**: a way the verb presents an action, as a fact (the indicative, every form you know so far), as a command (the imperative), or as something wished or imagined (the subjunctive, lesson 17).

The basic imperative is the shortest form a Latin verb has. For "you" (one person) it is the bare present stem: *amā* "love!", *monē* "warn!", *audī* "listen!" (ag1903 §179e). Nothing is added. Even English and Spanish make their commonest command short: "come!", *¡ven!*. A command is addressed to someone present, so there is no need to name the person.

For "you" (more than one), Latin adds *-te*: *amā-te*, *audī-te*. This *-te* is the same *t* as in *-tis*, the ordinary ending for "you (plural)" (ag1903 §163b).

A command is always about the future: you ask for something not yet done. Latin also has a second imperative, the **future imperative**, in *-tō*, for commands meant for a later time or for all time, as in laws: "the safety of the people *shall be* the highest law" (ag1903 §449). It is rare in conversation but common in legal and formal language, and a few verbs use it as their ordinary imperative.

To forbid, Latin does not put *nōn* in front of an imperative. It uses the imperative of *nōlō* "not want" with an infinitive: *nōlī timēre*, literally "be unwilling to fear", meaning "don't be afraid". Allen and Greenough call this the most courteous of the classical ways to forbid, and the commonest (ag1903 §450).

## How it is built

**The present imperative.**

1. Take the present stem, the infinitive minus *-re*: *amā-*, *monē-*, *rege-*, *cape-*, *audī-*.
2. For one person, use it as it is: *amā*, *monē*, *rege*, *cape*, *audī*. The -iō verbs use the stem without *i*: *cape*, not *\*capie* (ag1903 §179e).
3. For more than one person, add *-te*. In the third conjugation the short *e* becomes *i* before *-te*, as it does before *-tis*: *regite*, *capite*. The others keep their long vowel: *amāte*, *monēte*, *audīte*.

**Four short forms.** *Dīcō*, *dūcō*, *faciō* and *ferō* (lesson 14) drop the final vowel in the singular: *dīc* "say!", *dūc* "lead!", *fac* "do!", *fer* "carry!" (ag1903 §182). Early Latin still has *dīce*, *dūce*, *face*. The plurals are regular: *dīcite*, *dūcite*, *facite*.

***Sum*.** *Es* "be!", *este* "be! (plural)". The future imperative *estō* is commoner.

**The future imperative.** Add *-tō* for "you" (one) and "he, she, it", *-tōte* for "you" (more than one), and *-ntō* for "they": *amātō, amātōte, amantō*; *regitō, regitōte, reguntō*; *audītō, audītōte, audiuntō*; *estō, estōte, suntō*. *Sciō* "know" regularly uses *scītō* "know!" for its singular imperative (ag1903 §§182a, 449a).

**Forbidding.** Take *nōlī* (for one person) or *nōlīte* (for more), and add the infinitive of what is forbidden.

1. "Don't run away" to one person: *nōlī* + *fugere*: *Nōlī fugere.*
2. "Don't be afraid" to several: *nōlīte* + *timēre*: *Nōlīte timēre.*

**Adding a name.** A command often comes with the vocative (part 3, lesson 6): *Venī, Mārce* "come, Marcus". The vocative is set off by commas and usually does not come first.

## Where it applies

- **Orders and instructions.** *Mīlitēs, urbem dēfendite!* (ag1903 §448).
- **Requests and entreaties.** The same form serves for "please": *audī* can be a plea as well as an order (ag1903 §448). Romans soften it with *quaesō* "please" or *amābō* "please (lit. I shall love you)", which you will meet in comedy.
- **Greetings and farewells.** *Salvē*, *salvēte*; *valē*, *valēte*. Letters end with *valē*.
- **Prohibitions.** *Nōlī* or *nōlīte* with the infinitive (ag1903 §450). Two other classical ways use the subjunctive: *cavē* with the present subjunctive, and *nē* with the perfect subjunctive (*nē fēceris* "don't do it"). They come with the subjunctive (lessons 17 and 18).
- **Laws, rules and wills.** The future imperative: *Salūs populī suprēma lēx estō* (ag1903 §449).
- **A command for a stated later time.** The future imperative again: "ask tomorrow" (ag1903 §449). In ordinary prose the plain imperative or the future indicative (lesson 5) is also used: *hoc faciēs*, "you'll do this".
- **Commands to "he" or "they".** The future imperative has third persons (*amātō*, *amantō*), but outside laws Latin uses the subjunctive for "let him ...", "let them ..." (ag1903 §448a; lesson 19).
- **"Let us ..."** is not an imperative either; it is the subjunctive (lesson 19).
- **The imperative as an "if" clause.** "Ask, and it shall be given" can mean "if you ask, it will be given". Part 6 treats this.

## Watch out

**Do not forbid with *nōn* and the imperative.** *\*Nōn timē* is built like Spanish *no temas* or English "don't fear", but classical prose does not use it. Say *nōlī timēre*.

**Do not put *nōlī* with a finite verb.** *\*Nōlī timēs* is wrong: after *nōlī* comes the infinitive, *timēre*.

**Do not use *nōlī* for more than one person.** Speaking to a crowd, use *nōlīte*: *nōlīte timēre*. The *-te* marks the plural here too.

**Do not add *-ō* or a person ending to the singular.** *\*Amās!* or *\*Amā-s!* is a statement, "you love". The command is the bare stem, *amā*.

**Do not give the third conjugation a long vowel or an *ē*.** *\*Regē* and *\*regēte* are wrong. The stem has short *e*: *rege*, and *e* becomes *i* before *-te*: *regite*. Without macrons, *rege* and *monē* both end in *-e*; the conjugation decides.

**Do not keep the *i* of -iō verbs in the singular.** *\*Capie* and *\*fugie* are wrong. The imperative is *cape*, *fuge*; the plural *capite*, *fugite*.

**Do not write *\*dīce*, *\*dūce*, *\*face* in classical Latin.** These four verbs use *dīc*, *dūc*, *fac*, *fer*. Their compounds follow them (*ēdūc*, *addūc*), except compounds of *faciō* in *-ficiō*, which keep the vowel: *cōnfice* "finish!" (ag1903 §182).

**Do not confuse *es* "be!" with *es* "you are".** They are spelt alike. *Bonus es* is normally "you are good"; read it as a command only if the context demands it.

**Do not use the nominative for the person addressed.** *\*Audī, Mārcus!* is wrong: second-declension names in *-us* have a vocative in *-e*: *Mārce* (part 3, lesson 6).

## Connections

Spanish and French also make their singular command from the bare stem: Latin *cantā*, Spanish *¡canta!*, French *chante !*; and Spanish has a short irregular set like Latin's four, *di*, *haz*, *pon*. Korean and Japanese have whole sets of command endings graded by politeness; Latin has one form and softens it with words like *quaesō* "please".

## Exercises

1. Give the singular and plural present imperative of *videō*, *pōnō*, *fugiō*, *veniō* and *dūcō*.
2. Say "Marcus, write a letter to your father".
3. Say "boys, don't run in the temple".
4. Translate *Nōlī mē relinquere; manē mēcum.*
5. Translate *Ecce, hostēs! Arma capite et portās claudite!*
6. Correct *\*Nōn fuge, Quīnte!* and *\*Nōlīte timētis!*
7. Translate *Parentibus tuīs pārētō*, and say what kind of command it is.
8. Why is the singular imperative the shortest form of the verb? Explain with the present stem.
9. Say "forgive me, friends" with *ignōscō*, and explain the case of "me".

## Worked answers

1. *Vidē, vidēte*; *pōne, pōnite*; *fuge, fugite* (no *i*); *venī, venīte*; *dūc, dūcite* (one of the four short forms).
2. *Mārce, epistulam ad patrem scrībe.* *Mārce* is the vocative; *scrībe* is the third-conjugation singular imperative. "Your" is understood (part 3, lesson 10).
3. *Puerī, nōlīte in templō currere.* Plural prohibition: *nōlīte* + infinitive (ag1903 §450). *In templō*: place where, ablative (part 3, lesson 5).
4. "Don't leave me; stay with me." *Nōlī relinquere*: prohibition. *Manē*: second-conjugation imperative, the bare stem. *Mēcum*: *cum* attached to *mē* (part 3, lesson 10).
5. "Look, the enemy! Take up your arms and shut the gates!" *Capite* and *claudite*: third-conjugation plurals, *e* becoming *i* before *-te*.
6. *Nōlī fugere, Quīnte!* Classical Latin forbids with *nōlī* and the infinitive, not *nōn* with the imperative. *Nōlīte timēre!* After *nōlīte* comes the infinitive.
7. "Obey your parents." *Pārētō* is a future imperative, the form of rules and precepts meant for all time (ag1903 §449). *Pāreō* takes the dative: *parentibus*.
8. The singular imperative is the present stem with nothing added (ag1903 §179e). Every other form adds a marker or a person ending to that stem, so the command is the base on which the rest are built.
9. *Ignōscite mihi, amīcī.* *Ignōscō* takes the dative of the person forgiven (ag1903 §367), so "me" is *mihi*. *Ignōscite* is the third-conjugation plural imperative.
