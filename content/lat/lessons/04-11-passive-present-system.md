## The idea

"The enemy take the city" puts the doer first. "The city is taken by the enemy" turns the sentence round: the thing acted on becomes the subject, and the doer becomes an optional extra. The second kind of sentence is **passive**: its subject undergoes the action instead of doing it. The ordinary kind is **active**. The two are called **voices**.

English makes its passive with a helping verb: "is taken", "was being taken", "will be taken". Latin, in the tenses built on the present stem, makes it with a new set of person endings. *Capit* is "he takes"; *capitur* is "he is taken". The *-t* becomes *-tur*. Nothing else changes: the stem, the tense marker and the conjugation stay as you know them.

The passive endings have an *r* in most persons: *-r*, *-ris*, *-tur*, *-mur*, *-ntur*. Allen and Greenough say the passive is "an old Middle Voice, peculiar to the Italic and Celtic languages, and of uncertain origin" (ag1903 §163, footnote 2). A **middle** is a voice for actions that affect the subject itself: "wash oneself", "turn round". So the *r* is something Latin shares with its Italic and Celtic neighbours, and where it comes from is not known.

The middle meaning survives. Allen and Greenough note that the Latin passive "often has a reflexive meaning": *Turnus vertitur*, "Turnus turns round" (ag1903 §156a). This is why some verbs have passive forms but active meanings, the deponents of lesson 13.

One ending is odd. "You (more than one) are loved" is *amāminī*, with no *r*. Allen and Greenough suggest that *-minī* is a remnant of a participle, like the Greek *-μενος*, which replaced the proper ending (ag1903 §163, footnote 1). It is the one passive ending you must learn as a word apart.

## How it is built

**The passive endings.** Put them where the active endings go.

- "I": *-r* (after *ō*: *-or*). Active *-ō*, *-m*.
- "you" (one): *-ris* (or *-re*). Active *-s*.
- "he, she, it": *-tur*. Active *-t*.
- "we": *-mur*. Active *-mus*.
- "you" (more than one): *-minī*. Active *-tis*.
- "they": *-ntur*. Active *-nt*.

**Present.**

1. First conjugation: *amor, amāris, amātur, amāmur, amāminī, amantur*. The *ā* merges with *-or* as with *-ō*, and is short before *-ntur*, as before *-nt*.
2. Second: *moneor, monēris, monētur, monēmur, monēminī, monentur*.
3. Third: *regor, regeris, regitur, regimur, regiminī, reguntur*. The linking vowel is *e* before *r* (ag1903 §176b): *regeris*, not *\*regiris*.
4. -iō verbs: *capior, caperis, capitur, capimur, capiminī, capiuntur*.
5. Fourth: *audior, audīris, audītur, audīmur, audīminī, audiuntur*.

**Imperfect.** Take the active imperfect and change the person ending: *amābam* → *amābar*; *amābat* → *amābātur*; *regēbant* → *regēbantur*.

**Future.** The same method. The *b* future: *amābor, amāberis, amābitur, amābimur, amābiminī, amābuntur* (note *-be-* before *-ris*). The *ē* future: *regar, regēris, regētur, regēmur, regēminī, regentur*; *capiar, capiēris*; *audiar, audiēris*.

**Infinitive.** Change *-re* to *-rī*: *amārī* "to be loved", *monērī*, *audīrī*. The third conjugation and the -iō verbs use plain *-ī* on the bare stem: *regī* "to be ruled", *capī* "to be taken" (ag1903 §173).

**The doer.** In a passive sentence the person who does the action, the **agent**, goes in the ablative with *ā* or *ab* (part 3, lesson 4): *ab hostibus*, "by the enemy" (ag1903 §405). A thing used as a tool or means goes in the plain ablative, with no preposition: *saxīs*, "by stones", "with stones".

Say "the city is being taken by the enemy". The verb is *capiō*; the city undergoes the taking, so passive. Third person singular: *capitur*. The enemy are persons, so *ab hostibus*. *Urbs ab hostibus capitur.*

Say "the walls were being broken with stones". *Frangō*, third conjugation: imperfect *frangēbant*, passive *frangēbantur*. Stones are a tool: plain ablative. *Mūrī saxīs frangēbantur.*

## Where it applies

- **When the thing acted on is the topic.** *Urbs ab hostibus capitur*: the sentence is about the city. Latin, like English, can choose which participant to make the subject.
- **When the doer is unknown or does not matter.** *Epistula crās legētur*. Most Latin passives have no agent.
- **With an agent:** *ā* or *ab* with the ablative for persons, and for things treated as persons (ag1903 §405, note 3): *ā cōnsule*, *ab hīs*.
- **With a means or tool:** the plain ablative (ag1903 §405, note 2): *saxīs*, *ventīs*. The test is whether the noun could have been the subject of the active verb as a doer.
- **Reflexive and middle meanings.** The passive can mean "oneself": *vertitur* "he turns round", *movētur* "it moves" (ag1903 §156a). Context decides between "is turned" and "turns".
- **Infinitive:** *amārī*, *regī*, after *possum*, *volō*, *dēbeō* and the rest: *capī nōn potest*, "it cannot be taken".
- **All tenses of the present system:** present, imperfect, future, and the passive imperative (rare outside deponents, lesson 13).
- **Verbs with a dative or no object at all.** These can be passive only impersonally, in the third person singular with no subject: *mihi parcitur*, "I am spared", literally "it is spared to me" (ag1903 §372). *Pugnātur*, "there is fighting". Part 7 treats this impersonal passive.
- **The perfect, pluperfect and future perfect passive** are built differently, with a participle and *sum* (lesson 12).

## Watch out

**Do not build the passive with *sum*.** *\*Est amat* or *\*est amātur* copy English "is loved". In the present system the passive is one word: *amātur*. (The perfect passive does use *sum*, lesson 12, but with a participle.)

**Do not make a passive from a verb that takes the dative.** *\*Ego parcor* for "I am spared" and *\*tū crēderis* for "you are believed" follow English. The verb takes a dative, so its passive is impersonal: *mihi parcitur*, *tibi crēditur* (ag1903 §372). Poets sometimes break this; prose does not.

**Do not put a tool or a thing in *ā* + ablative.** *\*Mūrī ā saxīs franguntur* makes the stones into persons. A tool takes the plain ablative: *saxīs*.

**Do not put a person in the plain ablative.** *\*Urbs hostibus capitur* reads "by means of the enemy", as if they were an instrument. A person as agent needs *ab*: *ab hostibus*.

**Do not confuse *regeris* and *regēris*.** *Regeris* (short *e*) is the present "you are ruled"; *regēris* (long *ē*) is the future "you will be ruled". Only the vowel length differs.

**Do not read every *-re* as an infinitive.** *Amāre* can be the active infinitive "to love" or the passive "you are loved", the shorter form of *amāris*. The context, especially a second-person subject, decides.

**Do not give the third-conjugation infinitive *-erī*.** *\*Regerī* and *\*caperī* are wrong. The third conjugation adds *-ī* to the consonant: *regī*, *capī*. *Audīrī*, *amārī* and *monērī* keep *-rī*.

**Do not forget *-minī*.** *\*Amātur* for "you (plural) are loved" or *\*amāris* for the plural miss the odd ending. "You (more than one)" is *amāminī*, *regiminī*, *audīminī*.

**Do not make the receiver the subject.** English "I was given a book" makes the receiver the subject. Latin cannot: the book is the subject, the receiver dative: *mihi liber datur*, "a book is given to me".

## Connections

Neither Spanish nor French kept the Latin *-r* passive; both build passives with "be" and a participle, as English does (*la ciudad es tomada*, *la ville est prise*), the same build as the Latin perfect passive of lesson 12. Japanese and Korean, like Latin, mark the passive on the verb itself, with a suffix (Japanese *-(r)are-*, Korean *-i-*, *-hi-*, *-li-*, *-gi-*).

## Exercises

1. Make *Hostēs urbem capiunt* passive, keeping the enemy as agent.
2. Give the present passive of *mittō* in all six persons.
3. Translate *Nāvēs ventīs agitābantur et nautae timōre turbābantur.*
4. Say "the letters will be read by the consul".
5. Say "the soldiers were being called back by the general".
6. Give the present passive infinitive of *vocō*, *videō*, *dūcō*, *capiō* and *sentiō*.
7. Correct *\*Ā gladiō vulnerātur* ("he is wounded by a sword") and *\*Urbs mīlitibus dēfenditur* meant as "the city is defended by the soldiers" (as agents).
8. Correct *\*Ego ā rēge parcor* ("I am spared by the king").
9. Translate *Turnus vertitur*, and explain the two possible meanings of the passive.
10. *Regeris*, *regēris*, *regere*: give every possible meaning of each.

## Worked answers

1. *Urbs ab hostibus capitur.* The object *urbem* becomes the subject *urbs*; the subject *hostēs* becomes the agent *ab hostibus*; *capiunt* becomes singular passive *capitur* to agree with *urbs*.
2. *Mittor, mitteris, mittitur, mittimur, mittiminī, mittuntur.* Third conjugation: *e* before *-ris*, *i* before *-tur*, *-mur*, *-minī*, *u* before *-ntur* (ag1903 §176b).
3. "The ships were being tossed about by the winds, and the sailors were thrown into confusion by fear." *Ventīs* and *timōre* are things, so plain ablative (ag1903 §405). Both verbs are first-conjugation imperfect passives, *-bantur*.
4. *Epistulae ā cōnsule legentur.* Third-conjugation future passive: *leg-e-ntur*. The consul is a person: *ā cōnsule*.
5. *Mīlitēs ab imperātōre revocābantur.* Imperfect passive of *revocō*: *revocā-ba-ntur*.
6. *Vocārī*, *vidērī*, *dūcī*, *capī*, *sentīrī*. Third conjugation and -iō verbs: *-ī*; the others: *-rī*.
7. *Gladiō vulnerātur*: a sword is a tool, so plain ablative (ag1903 §405, note 2). *Urbs ā mīlitibus dēfenditur*: persons as agents take *ā* or *ab*.
8. *Mihi ā rēge parcitur.* *Parcō* takes the dative, so its passive is impersonal, with the person in the dative (ag1903 §372).
9. "Turnus turns (himself) round." The passive can be a true passive, "Turnus is turned", or keep its old middle sense of action on oneself (ag1903 §156a). Context decides.
10. *Regeris*: present passive, "you are ruled". *Regēris*: future passive, "you will be ruled". *Regere*: present active infinitive, "to rule", or the shorter form of *regeris*, "you are ruled".
