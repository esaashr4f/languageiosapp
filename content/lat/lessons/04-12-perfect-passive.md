## The idea

The *-r* endings of lesson 11 work only in the present system. For the perfect, pluperfect and future perfect passive, Latin does what English does all the time: it uses a form of "be" with an adjective made from the verb. "The city has been taken" is *urbs capta est*: literally "the city is taken", in the sense "is in the state of having been taken".

The adjective is the **perfect participle**. A **participle** is an adjective made from a verb: it describes a noun, as an adjective does, but it keeps the verb's meaning and can have an agent or an object. The perfect participle is passive and past: *captus* means "taken", "having been taken"; *amātus* "loved"; *missus* "sent". It is built on the supine stem, the fourth principal part minus *-um* (lesson 1 of this part), and it declines like *bonus, -a, -um* (part 2, lesson 7).

The participle and the supine share their *t*. Allen and Greenough explain that the perfect participle, the future participle and the supine are separate formations, each with its own suffix, but all begin with *t* and all undergo the same sound changes, so the Romans felt them as one system (ag1903 §164, footnote 1). The sound changes are what make many participles look irregular (ag1903 §178):

- *g* + *t* became *ct*: *reg-* gives *rēctus* "ruled";
- *b* + *t* became *pt*: *scrīb-* gives *scrīptus* "written";
- *d* + *t* and *t* + *t* became *s* or *ss*: *vert-* gives *versus* "turned", *cad-* gives *cāsum*, *mitt-* gives *missus*;
- a few verbs took *s* by analogy: *fallō*, *falsus*; *pellō*, *pulsus*.

You have met these forms already as nouns and adjectives: *cursus*, *sēnsus*, *falsus*, *nōtus*. Each is a participle or a noun built on the same stem.

Because the participle is an adjective, it agrees with its noun in gender, number and case. In the perfect passive it agrees with the subject: *urbs capta est* (feminine), *mūrus captus est* (masculine), *oppidum captum est* (neuter), *urbēs captae sunt* (plural).

## How it is built

**The participle.**

1. Take the fourth principal part: *amātum*, *captum*, *missum*, *vīsum*.
2. Remove *-um* to get the supine stem: *amāt-*, *capt-*, *miss-*, *vīs-*.
3. Add the endings of *bonus, -a, -um*: *amātus, amāta, amātum*; *captī, captae, capta*.

**The perfect passive.** Participle + present of *sum*.

1. *amātus sum* "I have been loved, I was loved";
2. *amātus es*, *amātus est*;
3. *amātī sumus*, *amātī estis*, *amātī sunt*.

The participle agrees with the subject: a woman says *amāta sum*; a group of women *amātae sumus*; a neuter subject takes *amātum est*.

**The pluperfect passive.** Participle + imperfect of *sum*: *amātus eram* "I had been loved", *capta erat* "it (f.) had been taken".

**The future perfect passive.** Participle + future of *sum*: *amātus erō* "I shall have been loved".

Notice that each passive uses the tense of *sum* one step "earlier" than its name: perfect passive with *sum* (present), pluperfect with *eram* (imperfect), future perfect with *erō* (future). The participle already carries the completion; *sum* places it in time (ag1903 §179g).

Say "the city was taken by Caesar". A single event, so perfect. The perfect passive of *capiō*: *captus* + *est*. The subject *urbs* is feminine singular: *capta est*. The agent is a person: *ā Caesare*. *Urbs ā Caesare capta est.*

Say "the letters had been written". Pluperfect: participle + *erant*. *Epistulae* is feminine plural: *scrīptae erant*. *Epistulae scrīptae erant.*

**The participle on its own.** It can describe a noun like any adjective: *urbs capta* "the captured city"; *epistula scrīpta* "the written letter". Part 5 develops this, and part 6 builds a whole clause from it (the ablative absolute).

## Where it applies

- **The perfect passive, in both senses of the perfect.** *Urbs capta est*: "the city was taken" (event) or "has been taken" (result), as in the active (lesson 7).
- **The pluperfect and future perfect passive.** *Scrīptae erant*; *factum erit*. All the uses of lesson 8 apply.
- **A state described by the participle.** *Gallia est dīvīsa*: "Gaul is divided". Here the participle is a predicate adjective, and *est* has its plain meaning. Context tells you whether *dīvīsa est* is an event ("was divided") or a state ("is divided").
- **As an adjective.** *Urbs capta*, *epistula scrīpta*. It can stand before or after its noun (part 2, lesson 8).
- **As a noun.** *Captī* "the captured, the prisoners"; *factum* "a thing done, a deed" (lesson 5 of this part). Part 5 treats adjectives as nouns.
- **With the agent.** The perfect passive takes *ā* or *ab* + ablative for a person (lesson 11): *ā Caesare capta est*.
- **In the perfect passive infinitive.** *Captum esse* "to have been taken": the participle with *esse*. It is used in reported statements (part 6).
- **Deponent and semi-deponent verbs** use the same build with an active meaning: *gāvīsus sum* "I rejoiced" (*gaudeō*), *ausus sum* "I dared" (*audeō*). Lesson 13 explains.
- **Verbs with no supine** have no perfect participle and no perfect passive: *timeō*, *pateō*, *discō* (lesson 1).

## Watch out

**Do not read *amātus est* as "he is loved".** English "is" + participle is a present passive; Latin *amātus est* is a perfect: "he was loved" or "he has been loved". "He is loved" is *amātur* (lesson 11). The exception is a participle used as a plain adjective of state, like *dīvīsa est* above.

**Do not forget agreement.** *\*Urbs captus est* gives a feminine subject a masculine participle. The participle takes the gender, number and case of the subject: *urbs capta est*; *urbēs captae sunt*; *oppida capta sunt*.

**Do not use *-tur* with the perfect.** *\*Captātur* and *\*cēpitur* mix systems. The *-r* endings belong to the present system only; the perfect passive is *captus est*.

**Do not take the participle as active.** *Amātus* is "loved", never "having loved". Latin has no active perfect participle for ordinary verbs. "Having heard the news, he left" must be said another way (part 6). Deponents are the exception (lesson 13).

**Do not build the participle from the present stem.** *\*Regātus*, *\*mittītus*, *\*vidētus* do not exist. The participle comes from the fourth principal part: *rēctus*, *missus*, *vīsus*. Learn it with the verb; the sound changes of ag1903 §178 explain the shapes but do not let you predict them all.

**Do not use *fuit* to build the perfect passive.** English "was taken" tempts you towards *\*captus fuit* as a past of *captus est*. The standard perfect passive is *captus est*: the present of *sum*, because the participle already carries the past.

**Do not confuse the participle with the supine or the noun of the same shape.** *Sēnsus* can be the participle "felt" or the noun "feeling" (part 3); *factum* can be "done" (neuter participle) or "a deed". The agreement and the sentence decide.

## Connections

Spanish and French build their whole passive this way: *la ciudad fue tomada*, *la ville a été prise*. English "the die is cast" is a translation of *iacta ālea est*, and many English words are Latin perfect participles: *fact* (*factum*), *script* (*scrīptum*), *verse* (*versus*), and *mission* comes from a noun built on *missum*.

## Exercises

1. Give the perfect participle, masculine nominative singular, of *laudō*, *moneō*, *dūcō*, *faciō* and *audiō*.
2. Make *Hostēs urbem cēpērunt* passive.
3. Say "the gates had been opened by the guards".
4. Say "the girls were praised by the teacher".
5. Translate *Templum ā barbarīs dēlētum est.*
6. Correct *\*Epistula scrīptus est* and *\*Mīlitēs vocātus sunt*.
7. Translate *Mūrī dēlētī erunt*, and name the tense.
8. *Vīsus est* and *vidētur*: what is the difference in tense?
9. Why does *vertō* have *versus*, and *scrībō* *scrīptus*? Use the sound changes.

## Worked answers

1. *Laudātus*, *monitus*, *ductus*, *factus*, *audītus*. Each comes from the fourth principal part: *laudātum*, *monitum*, *ductum*, *factum*, *audītum*.
2. *Urbs ab hostibus capta est.* The object becomes the subject; the participle agrees with *urbs*, feminine singular; *est* for the perfect; the agent goes into *ab* + ablative.
3. *Portae ā custōdibus apertae erant.* Pluperfect passive: participle + *erant*. *Aperiō, apertum*: *apertae*, feminine plural, to agree with *portae*.
4. *Puellae ā magistrō laudātae sunt.* Feminine plural participle with *sunt*.
5. "The temple was destroyed by the barbarians." *Dēlētum*: neuter, to agree with *templum*. *Ā barbarīs*: persons as agents.
6. *Epistula scrīpta est*: *epistula* is feminine. *Mīlitēs vocātī sunt*: the subject is plural, so the participle is plural.
7. "The walls will have been destroyed." Future perfect passive: participle + *erunt* (ag1903 §179g).
8. *Vīsus est* is perfect passive, "he was seen" or "has been seen". *Vidētur* is present passive, "he is seen" (in practice often "he seems", which part 6 covers).
9. *Vert-* + *t* gave *versus*: *t* + *t* became *s* (ag1903 §178, note 1). *Scrīb-* + *t* gave *scrīptus*: *b* became *p* before the voiceless *t* (ag1903 §178, note 1; §15).
