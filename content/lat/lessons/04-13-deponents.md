## The idea

*Sequitur* looks like a passive: it has the *-tur* of *amātur*. But it does not mean "he is followed". It means "he follows". *Loquor* is "I speak", *hortātur* "he urges", *mortuus est* "he died". These are **deponent** verbs: verbs with passive forms and active meanings. The name is Latin *dēpōnēns*, "laying aside": the old grammarians thought these verbs had laid aside their active forms.

The real history runs the other way. The Latin passive began as a **middle** voice, for actions that concern the subject itself (lesson 11 of this part). Many verbs were always middle: speaking, following, being born, dying, getting angry, trying. When the middle turned into a passive in most verbs, these kept their middle forms and their active-looking meaning. Allen and Greenough put it this way: "Deponents are really passive (or middle) verbs whose active voice has disappeared" (ag1903 §191, note), and "most deponents are intransitive or reflexive in meaning, corresponding to what in Greek is called the Middle Voice" (ag1903 §190e).

Look at the meanings of the commonest deponents and you can see the middle in them. *Nāscor* "be born", *morior* "die", *orior* "rise", *proficīscor* "set out", *īrāscor* "get angry", *vereor* "be in awe of, fear", *mīror* "wonder at": things that happen in or to the subject, or that the subject does on its own behalf.

You already know every form a deponent has. It is conjugated exactly like the passive of its conjugation: *hortor* like *amor*, *vereor* like *moneor*, *sequor* like *regor*, *patior* like *capior*, *experior* like *audior*. The only new thing is the meaning.

## How it is built

**Reading the dictionary entry.** A deponent has three principal parts, all passive in form:

- *hortor, hortārī, hortātus sum* "urge";
- *vereor, verērī, veritus sum* "fear";
- *sequor, sequī, secūtus sum* "follow";
- *patior, patī, passus sum* "suffer, allow";
- *experior, experīrī, expertus sum* "try, test".

The first is "I ...", the second the infinitive, the third the perfect. The *-or* of the first part and the *-ī* or *-rī* of the infinitive tell you at once that the verb is deponent. The infinitive also gives the conjugation: *-ārī* first, *-ērī* second, *-ī* third, *-īrī* fourth.

**Building forms.**

1. Find the conjugation and the stem as for any verb.
2. Use the passive endings of lesson 11 for the present, imperfect and future: *sequitur*, *sequēbātur*, *sequētur*.
3. Use the participle with *sum* for the perfect, pluperfect and future perfect (lesson 12): *secūtus est*, *secūtus erat*, *secūtus erit*.
4. Translate actively: "he follows", "he was following", "he will follow", "he followed", "he had followed", "he will have followed".

**Commands.** The imperative of a deponent is the passive form in *-re* for one person and *-minī* for more: *sequere* "follow!", *sequiminī* "follow! (plural)"; *hortāre*, *verēre*. This looks like an infinitive active, but deponents have no active infinitive, so there is no clash.

**The perfect participle is active.** *Secūtus* means "having followed", *locūtus* "having spoken", *hortātus* "having urged" (ag1903 §190b). Ordinary verbs have no such form (lesson 12). This is one of the great conveniences of deponents.

**Semi-deponents.** Four verbs are active in the present system and deponent in the perfect system (ag1903 §192): *audeō, audēre, ausus sum* "dare"; *gaudeō, gaudēre, gāvīsus sum* "rejoice"; *soleō, solēre, solitus sum* "be used to"; *fīdō, fīdere, fīsus sum* "trust". You met three of them in earlier lessons: now you know why their third part looks passive.

**Deponents with the ablative.** *Ūtor* "use" and *fruor* "enjoy" take the ablative, not the accusative: *gladiō ūtitur*, "he uses a sword" (literally "he serves himself with a sword"). The middle meaning explains the case.

Say "the soldiers followed their general". *Sequor* is deponent, third conjugation. A single past event: perfect, *secūtus sum*. The subject is masculine plural: *secūtī sunt*. *Mīlitēs ducem secūtī sunt.*

## Where it applies

- **A large class.** More than half of all deponents are first conjugation, and all of those are regular (ag1903 §191): *hortor*, *cōnor*, *mīror*, *precor*, *arbitror*, *moror*. The others must be learned with their parts: *sequor*, *loquor*, *patior*, *morior*, *nāscor*, *ūtor*, *fruor*, *queror*, *orior*, *proficīscor*, *ēgredior*, *ingredior*, *experior*, *fateor*, *vereor*, *īrāscor* (ag1903 §191).
- **Transitive deponents** take an object in the accusative: *ducem sequuntur*, *cīvēs hortātur*.
- **Intransitive deponents** take none: *nāscitur*, *moritur*, *proficīscitur*.
- **Deponents with other cases:** *ūtor* and *fruor* with the ablative; *īrāscor* "be angry with" with the dative.
- **The active perfect participle:** *secūtus* "having followed", *hortātus* "having urged". Some, such as *ratus* "thinking", *veritus* "fearing", *ausus* "daring", are used almost as presents (ag1903 §491).
- **Participles of both voices.** Deponents also have active present and future participles, *sequēns* "following", *secūtūrus* "about to follow" (ag1903 §190a; part 5).
- **The gerundive is passive** even in deponents: *hortandus* "to be urged" (ag1903 §190d; part 7).
- **A few deponents can be passive.** Rarely, a deponent is used with a passive sense (ag1903 §190f), and about twenty verbs exist in both forms with the same meaning, like *mereō* and *mereor* "deserve" (ag1903 §190g).
- **Semi-deponents:** *audeō*, *gaudeō*, *soleō*, *fīdō* (ag1903 §192).

## Watch out

**Do not translate a deponent as passive.** *Sequitur* is "he follows", not "he is followed". *Locūtus est* is "he spoke", not "he was spoken". Check the dictionary: an entry in *-or* with an infinitive in *-ī* or *-rī* is deponent.

**Do not invent active forms.** *\*Sequō*, *\*sequit*, *\*loquere* as an infinitive, *\*hortāre* as an infinitive: none of these is the active they look like. "To follow" is *sequī*, "to urge" *hortārī*, "to speak" *loquī*.

**Do not try to make a deponent passive.** "He is followed" cannot be built from *sequor*. Use another construction, for example make the follower the subject: *eum sequuntur*, "they follow him".

**Do not use *est* with an ordinary verb for an active perfect.** *\*Laudātus est* for "he praised" is wrong; it means "he was praised". Only deponents use the participle + *sum* in an active sense.

**Do not put the thing used in the accusative.** *\*Gladium ūtitur* follows English "uses a sword". *Ūtor* takes the ablative: *gladiō ūtitur*. Likewise *\*vītam fruitur* → *vītā fruitur*.

**Do not take *mortuus* for a regular participle.** It is *mortuus*, not *\*moritus*; and it is used as an adjective, "dead": *mortuus est* can be "he died" or "he is dead".

**Do not mix the semi-deponents.** *\*Ausī* for "I dared" and *\*gaudēvī* for "I rejoiced" do not exist. The perfects are *ausus sum*, *gāvīsus sum*. The presents are active: *audeō*, *gaudeō*.

**Spanish and French will not warn you.** Their descendants of deponents are ordinary active verbs, because later Latin had already made them active: Wiktionary derives Spanish *seguir* from a spoken *\*sequīre*, replacing *sequī*, and *nacer* from a later active *nāscere*. Classical Latin keeps the passive endings.

## Connections

Greek has a middle voice that is still a middle, and many Greek verbs are "deponent" in the same way: *ἔρχομαι* "I come" has middle endings and an active meaning. Spanish keeps a trace of the middle sense in its reflexive verbs: *se muere*, *se va*. Many English words come from deponent participles: *sequence* and *consecutive* from *secūtus*, *nation* from *nātus*, *patient* and *passion* from *patior*.

## Exercises

1. Conjugate *cōnor* in the present, all six persons.
2. Translate *Dux mīlitēs hortātus est, et mīlitēs bene pugnāvērunt.*
3. Say "we shall follow you".
4. Say "the citizens were complaining about the war", using *queror* with *dē*.
5. Translate *Rēx in hāc urbe nātus est et ibi mortuus est.*
6. Correct *\*Gladium ūtimur* ("we use a sword") and *\*Sequit ducem* ("he follows the general").
7. What are the infinitive and the imperative singular of *vereor* and *loquor*? Why do the imperatives look like active infinitives?
8. Why do so many verbs of being born, dying and rising happen to be deponent? Give the source's explanation.
9. Say "Marcus dared to speak", with *audeō*, in the perfect.

## Worked answers

1. *Cōnor, cōnāris, cōnātur, cōnāmur, cōnāminī, cōnantur*: the passive endings on the first-conjugation stem *cōnā-* (lesson 11), translated actively: "I try", and so on.
2. "The general encouraged the soldiers, and the soldiers fought well." *Hortātus est*: deponent perfect, active in meaning. *Pugnāvērunt*: ordinary active perfect.
3. *Tē sequēmur.* *Sequor* is third conjugation, so the *ē* future: *sequ-ē-mur*, with the passive ending *-mur*.
4. *Cīvēs dē bellō querēbantur.* Imperfect, *querē-ba-ntur*, for a complaint that went on.
5. "The king was born in this city and died there." *Nātus est*, *mortuus est*: deponent perfects, both active in meaning. *Hāc*: ablative of *hic* after *in* (part 3, lesson 12).
6. *Gladiō ūtimur*: *ūtor* takes the ablative. *Ducem sequitur*: *sequor* is deponent, so "he follows" is *sequitur*.
7. *Verērī*, *verēre*; *loquī*, *loquere*. The deponent imperative uses the passive ending *-re* (lesson 11). Since a deponent has no active infinitive, there is no form for it to be confused with.
8. Deponents are middle verbs whose active disappeared; most are intransitive or reflexive, actions that happen in or to the subject (ag1903 §§190e, 191, note). Birth, death and rising are of that kind.
9. *Mārcus loquī ausus est.* *Audeō* is semi-deponent: its perfect is *ausus sum* (ag1903 §192). *Loquī* is the deponent infinitive.
