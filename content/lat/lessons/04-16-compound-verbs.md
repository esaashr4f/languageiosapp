## The idea

Latin makes new verbs by putting a preposition in front of an old one. *Mittō* "send" gives *permittō* "let go through, allow", *āmittō* "let go, lose", *dīmittō* "send in different directions, dismiss", *committō* "bring together, entrust", *prōmittō* "send forward, promise". A verb made this way is a **compound verb**. Allen and Greenough explain that in these compounds the prepositions keep "their original adverbial sense" (ag1903 §267a): *ab* "away", *ad* "to", *con-* "together" or "thoroughly", *dē* "down, utterly", *ex* "out", *in* "in, on, against", *ob* "towards, in the way", *per* "through", *prae* "in front", *prō* "forward", *sub* "under, up from under", *trāns* "across". A few prefixes never stand alone as prepositions: *dis-* "apart", *re-* or *red-* "back, again", *sē-* "aside" (ag1903 §267b).

If you know the simple verb and the prefix, you can usually work out the compound. That is why one lesson can add thirty verbs: most of them you half know already.

Two things change when a prefix is added, and both have reasons.

**The prefix adapts to the next sound.** A consonant at the end of the prefix often takes on the sound that follows it: *ad* + *ferō* becomes *afferō*, *in* + *mittō* becomes *immittō*, *ob* + *ferō* becomes *offerō*, *con-* + *legō* becomes *colligō*. This is **assimilation**: one sound becoming like its neighbour, to make the word easier to say. Allen and Greenough warn that "usage varied considerably" and that editions spell some compounds both ways, *adferō* and *afferō* (ag1903 §16).

**The vowel of the simple verb weakens.** *Faciō* becomes *cōnficiō*; *capiō* becomes *accipiō*; *teneō* becomes *retineō*; *caedō* becomes *occīdō*. The rule is regular (ag1903 §267a, note 2; §15):

- short *a* in the root becomes *i* before one consonant (*fac-* → *-fic-*, *cap-* → *-cip-*) and *e* before two (*factum* → *cōnfectum*, *captum* → *acceptum*);
- short *e* becomes *i* before one consonant (*teneō* → *retineō*, *legō* → *colligō*);
- *ae* becomes *ī* (*caedō* → *occīdō*, *quaerō* → *requīrō*);
- long vowels stay (*āctum* → *perāctum*).

Allen and Greenough connect this with an early stage of Latin when the stress fell on the first syllable of every word (ag1903 §15, note). In *cōn-facere* the second syllable was unstressed, and its vowel was reduced. When the later stress rule took over (part 1), the reduced vowels stayed.

## How it is built

**Taking a compound apart.**

1. Find the prefix: *ac-cipiō*, *ef-ficiō*, *col-ligō*. Undo any assimilation: *ac-* is *ad*, *ef-* is *ex*, *col-* is *con-*.
2. Find the simple verb. Undo the vowel weakening: *-cipiō* is *capiō*; *-ficiō* is *faciō*; *-ligō* is *legō*.
3. Combine the meanings: *ad* "to" + *capiō* "take" → *accipiō* "take to oneself, receive"; *ex* "out" + *faciō* "make" → *efficiō* "make out, bring about"; *con-* "together" + *legō* "gather" → *colligō* "gather together, collect".
4. Check the dictionary. The combined meaning is a guide, not a guarantee: *intellegō* "understand" is *inter* "between" + *legō* "choose", "choose between", but the meaning has moved.

**Conjugating a compound.** A compound is conjugated like its simple verb, in the weakened form. *Accipiō* is a third-conjugation -iō verb like *capiō*: *accipit*, *accipiunt*, *accipiēbat*, *accipiet*. Its perfect and supine follow the simple verb's, with the same weakening: *capiō, cēpī, captum*; *accipiō, accēpī, acceptum*.

Two details:

- **Reduplication is usually lost.** The simple verbs *cadō, cecidī*; *tangō, tetigī*; *pellō, pepulī*; *currō, cucurrī* have compounds *occidō, occidī*; *contingō, contigī*; *expellō, expulī*; *occurrō, occurrī* (Wiktionary). The prefix takes the place of the repeated syllable. Why this happened is not explained here.
- **The compounds of *faciō* in *-ficiō*** have an ordinary passive (*cōnficitur* "it is finished") and a full imperative *cōnfice*, not *\*cōnfac* (ag1903 §§182, 204a). Only compounds that keep *a*, like *satisfaciō*, use *fīō* in the passive.

**What the compound governs.** A compound often takes a new case. Many compounds of *ad*, *con*, *in*, *ob*, *prae*, *sub* take a dative of the person or thing affected (ag1903 §370): *occurrō* "run to meet" takes the dative, *hostibus occurrunt*. Compounds of motion with *ad*, *in*, *trāns* can take an accusative directly: *flūmen trānseunt* (lesson 14). Learn the construction with the verb.

Say "the soldiers killed the guards". *Interficiō*: *inter* + *faciō*, "kill". Perfect: *faciō, fēcī* gives *interfēcī*. *Mīlitēs custōdēs interfēcērunt.*

## Where it applies

- **Building vocabulary.** Most Latin verbs beyond the commonest few hundred are compounds. Each simple verb you know opens a family: *mittō* (*āmittō*, *committō*, *dīmittō*, *permittō*, *prōmittō*), *capiō* (*accipiō*, *recipiō*, *suscipiō*, *ēripiō* from *rapiō*), *faciō* (*cōnficiō*, *efficiō*, *interficiō*, *dēficiō*), *cēdō* (*accēdō*, *discēdō*, *concēdō*, *prōcēdō*), *dūcō* (*addūcō*, *dēdūcō*).
- **Direction.** Many prefixes keep their spatial sense: *ex-* out, *ab-* away, *ad-* to, *re-* back: *ēripiō* "snatch out", *discēdō* "go apart, depart".
- **Completion.** *Con-*, *per-*, *ex-* and *dē-* often add the idea of doing the action fully: *faciō* "do", *cōnficiō* "finish", *efficiō* "bring about"; *dēleō* is "wipe out" (ag1903 §267a: *com-* "forcibly", *dē* "utterly").
- **New meanings.** Some compounds have moved far from their parts: *intellegō* "understand", *dīligō* "love, esteem" (from *dis-* + *legō*), *interficiō* "kill". Learn these as words.
- **New constructions.** Dative with compounds of *ad*, *con*, *in*, *ob*, *prae*, *sub* (ag1903 §370): *occurrō hostibus*, *praesum exercituī* "I command the army". But if the compound's meaning does not suit an indirect object, the simple verb's construction remains (ag1903 §370a): *hominem interficit*.
- **Compounds of irregular verbs.** They keep the irregularity: *abeō*, *redeō* like *eō*; *afferō*, *referō* like *ferō*; *adsum*, *absum*, *possum* like *sum* (lessons 3 and 14).
- **Verbs with a noun or adverb in front.** A few compounds are made from words, not prepositions: *benedīcō* "speak well of", *satisfaciō* "do enough for, satisfy" (ag1903 §266b). These keep the simple verb's vowel.

## Watch out

**Do not keep the unweakened vowel.** *\*Cōnfaciō*, *\*accapiō*, *\*retenēre* and *\*occaedō* are not Latin. The vowel weakens: *cōnficiō*, *accipiō*, *retinēre*, *occīdō*.

**Do not weaken the perfect stem when it has a long vowel.** *\*Interfīcī* is wrong; the perfect of *interficiō* is *interfēcī*, keeping the long *ē* of *fēcī*. Long vowels resist weakening (ag1903 §267a, note 2).

**Do not keep the reduplication.** *\*Occecidī* and *\*expepulī* repeat a syllable the compound has lost: *occidī*, *expulī*. A few compounds do keep it (*occurrī* beside *occucurrī*), so check the dictionary.

**Do not confuse *occidō* and *occīdō*.** *Occidō* (short *i*, from *cadō*) is "fall, die, set (of the sun)"; *occīdō* (long *ī*, from *caedō*) is "kill". The same length difference as *cecidī* and *cecīdī* (lesson 6).

**Do not give *cōnficiō* the passive *fīō*.** *\*Cōnfit* for "it is finished" is wrong in classical prose; the passive is *cōnficitur* (ag1903 §204a). Only *faciō* itself uses *fīō*.

**Do not trust the parts too far.** *Intellegō* is not "read between"; *dīligō* is not "choose apart" in use. The parts explain the meaning's origin, not its present sense.

**Do not drop the preposition just because the verb contains it.** *Accēdunt ad mūrōs* repeats *ad*: Latin often does this. But some compounds take a plain dative or accusative instead. Check each verb's construction.

**Spanish and French have the same compounds in a different shape.** Spanish *recibir*, French *recevoir* come from *recipere*; Spanish *perder* from *perdere*. The prefixes are recognisable, but the meanings and spellings have moved: do not build Latin forms from them.

## Connections

English is full of Latin compounds, often borrowed through French, and you can read their parts the same way: *accept*, *receive* (from *recipere*), *efficient*, *confection*, *permit*, *dismiss*, *promise*, *intellect*, *collect*, *elect*, *contact*, *retain*, *occur*. The vowel weakening shows in English too: *fact* beside *perfect* and *infect*, *capture* beside *recipient*.

## Exercises

1. Take apart *afferō*, *efficiō*, *colligō* and *occīdō*: prefix, simple verb, change, meaning.
2. Give the principal parts of *suscipiō* and explain each from *capiō*.
3. Translate *Caesar cōpiās ē castrīs ēdūxit et ad hostēs accessit* — *ēdūcō* "lead out" is new here, *ex* + *dūcō*.
4. Say "the general sent the soldiers away".
5. Say "I understand your words".
6. Correct *\*Mīlitēs urbem cōnfacere nōn possunt* and *\*Hostēs ducem interficiērunt*.
7. Why does *faciō* become *-ficiō* but *fēcī* stay *-fēcī* in compounds?
8. What is the difference between *occidit* and *occīdit*?
9. Translate *Nōlī spem āmittere* and explain the prefix.

## Worked answers

1. *Af-ferō*: *ad* + *ferō*, *d* assimilated to *f*: "bring to". *Ef-ficiō*: *ex* + *faciō*, *x* assimilated, *a* weakened to *i*: "bring about". *Col-ligō*: *con-* + *legō*, *n* assimilated to *l*, *e* weakened to *i*: "gather together". *Oc-cīdō*: *ob* + *caedō*, *b* assimilated, *ae* weakened to *ī*: "kill" (ag1903 §§16, 267a).
2. *Suscipiō, suscipere, suscēpī, susceptum*. *Sub* (as *sus-*) + *capiō*: *a* → *i* before one consonant (*-cipiō*), *a* → *e* before two (*-ceptum*); the long *ē* of *cēpī* stays (ag1903 §267a, note 2).
3. "Caesar led his troops out of the camp and approached the enemy." *Ēdūxit*: perfect, like *dūxit*. *Accessit*: perfect of *accēdō*, like *cessit*.
4. *Dux mīlitēs dīmīsit.* *Dīmittō, dīmīsī*: perfect like *mīsī*.
5. *Verba tua intellegō.* Third conjugation, like *legō*.
6. *Mīlitēs urbem capere nōn possunt* or, with a compound, *expugnāre* would be needed; but the error is *\*cōnfacere*: the compound is *cōnficere*. *Hostēs ducem interfēcērunt*: the perfect keeps the long *ē* of *fēcī*.
7. Short vowels weaken in the unstressed syllable of a compound; long vowels do not (ag1903 §267a, note 2). *Fac-* has a short *a*; *fēc-* a long *ē*.
8. *Occidit* (short *i*): "he falls, he dies" (present, from *cadō*). *Occīdit* (long *ī*): "he kills" or "he killed" (from *caedō*).
9. "Don't lose hope." *Āmittō* is *ab* + *mittō*, "let go away", hence "lose". *Nōlī* + infinitive forbids (lesson 9).
