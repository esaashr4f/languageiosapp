## The idea

Lesson 9 of this part introduced the idea of **mood**: the way a verb presents its action. Every form so far has been **indicative**, the mood of statement and question about fact (*amat* "he loves"), apart from the imperative, the mood of command (*amā* "love!"). Latin has a third mood, the **subjunctive**: the mood of what is wished, proposed, intended, expected or imagined, rather than stated as fact.

Compare:

- *amat*, "he loves" (a fact);
- *amet*, "may he love", "let him love", "he would love", "(so that) he may love".

English has almost lost its subjunctive ("if I *were* you", "God *save* the King"), so it uses helping words: "may", "let", "would", "should". Spanish and French still have a full subjunctive (*que ame*, *qu'il aime*), and in Spanish it continues the Latin one directly: Latin *amet*, Spanish *ame*.

The subjunctive has two great territories (ag1903 §438).

1. **In main clauses** it expresses a wish ("may the gods help us"), a proposal or command ("let us go"), a doubtful question ("what am I to do?") or a possibility ("someone might say"). Lesson 19 of this part covers these uses.
2. **In subordinate clauses**, after words like *ut* "so that" and *cum* "when, since", it marks purpose, result, reported questions and much more. That is most of part 6.

This lesson and the next build the forms. The subjunctive has four tenses: present, imperfect, perfect and pluperfect. There is no future subjunctive. This lesson gives the two built on the present stem; lesson 18 gives the two built on the perfect stem.

Where do the forms come from? Allen and Greenough explain (ag1903 §168e-f, with notes 1-2 and footnote 4):

- the *ē* of the first conjugation (*am-ē-s*) is an inherited subjunctive sign, "the thematic vowel *e* lengthened";
- the *ā* of the other conjugations (*mone-ā-s*, *reg-ā-s*, *audi-ā-s*) "is of uncertain origin";
- the *ī* of *sim, sīs* and *velim, velīs* is an old **optative**, a separate wishing mood of the parent language, which in Latin merged with the subjunctive;
- the imperfect subjunctive's *-rē-* "is of uncertain origin and is not found outside of Italic"; its *r* is probably an old *s* turned to *r* between vowels, as in *es-se-m*, and its *ē* probably the subjunctive sign.

So the Latin subjunctive, like the perfect, is a merger of older categories.

## How it is built

**Present subjunctive, active.** Change the vowel before the ending.

1. First conjugation: the *ā* becomes *ē*: *amem, amēs, amet, amēmus, amētis, ament*.
2. All the others: *ā* after the stem: *moneam, moneās*; *regam, regās*; *capiam, capiās*; *audiam, audiās*.
3. Shorten the vowel before *-m*, *-t*, *-nt* as always: *amet*, *regat*, *regant*.

A memory aid: the first conjugation, whose indicative has *ā*, takes *ē*; the others, which do not, take *ā*. The vowel swaps.

**Irregular present subjunctives, in *-im*.** *Sum*: *sim, sīs, sit, sīmus, sītis, sint*. *Possum*: *possim*. *Volō*: *velim*; *nōlō*: *nōlim*; *mālō*: *mālim*. *Dō*: *dem, dēs, det*. *Eō* and *ferō* are regular: *eam*, *feram*. *Fīō*: *fīam*.

**Imperfect subjunctive, active.** Take the present active infinitive and add the person endings, with *ē* long except before *-m*, *-t*, *-nt*:

- *amāre* → *amārem, amārēs, amāret, amārēmus, amārētis, amārent*;
- *regere* → *regerem*; *capere* → *caperem*; *audīre* → *audīrem*;
- *esse* → *essem*; *posse* → *possem*; *velle* → *vellem*; *ferre* → *ferrem*; *īre* → *īrem*; *fierī* → *fierem*.

This works for every verb, including the irregular ones, which is why it is worth learning as a rule.

**Passive.** Use the passive endings on the same forms: present *amer, amēris, amētur*; *regar, regāris, regātur*; imperfect *amārer, amārēris, amārētur*; *regerer*. Deponents use these with active meaning: *sequātur* "let him follow", *hortārētur*.

**The negative** of a main-clause subjunctive is *nē*, not *nōn*: *nē veniat* "let him not come" (ag1903 §439). A wish can be introduced by *utinam* "if only": *utinam veniat*, "if only he would come" (lesson 19).

Say "let us love". "Let us" is a proposal, so subjunctive. *Amō* is first conjugation, so *ē*: first person plural *amēmus*. *Amēmus.*

Say "let them read the letter". *Legō* is third conjugation, so *ā*: *legant*. *Epistulam legant.*

## Where it applies

This lesson builds the forms; here is where you will use them (ag1903 §438).

- **Proposals and commands** in the present: *amēmus*, *legant*, *interficiāmus* (ag1903 §439; lesson 19).
- **Wishes**: present for a wish that may come true, *dī tē servent*; imperfect with *utinam* for one that cannot, *utinam hīc essēs* (ag1903 §441; lesson 19).
- **Doubtful questions**: "what am I to do?" (ag1903 §444; lesson 19).
- **Possibility**: "someone might say" (ag1903 §446; lesson 19).
- **What should have been done**: the imperfect, *potius docēret* (ag1903 §439b).
- **Prohibitions**: *nē* with the perfect subjunctive (lesson 18), or *cavē* with the present: *cavē cadās* "mind you don't fall" (ag1903 §450).
- **Subordinate clauses**: purpose (*ut*, *nē*), result, fear, indirect command and question, *cum* clauses, conditions (part 6). The present and imperfect are the two you will use most there, because the "sequence of tenses" pairs them with present and past main verbs (part 6).

## Watch out

**Do not mix up the vowels.** *\*Amat* is indicative; the subjunctive is *amet*. *\*Regit* is indicative; the subjunctive is *regat*. And *\*amāmus* for "let us love" is the indicative "we love": say *amēmus*.

**Do not read *monet* as a subjunctive.** Second-conjugation *monet* has *e* because the stem is *monē-*; it is indicative. The subjunctive is *moneat*. The *ē* of the subjunctive belongs to the first conjugation only.

**Do not confuse the third-conjugation future and the subjunctive.** *Regam* is both "I shall rule" and "let me rule": the first person singular is identical (ag1903 §168d). In the other persons they differ: future *regēs, reget*, subjunctive *regās, regat*.

**Do not build the imperfect subjunctive from the perfect stem.** *\*Rēxerem* and *\*cēperem* are wrong. The rule uses the present infinitive: *regerem*, *caperem*. Even *esse* gives *essem*, and *ferre* gives *ferrem*.

**Do not use *nōn* with a subjunctive of command or wish.** *\*Nōn veniat* for "let him not come" follows the indicative. The negative is *nē*: *nē veniat* (ag1903 §439). (In subordinate clauses of result, part 6, *nōn* is used; learn that there.)

**Do not translate every subjunctive with "may".** English uses "let", "may", "should", "would", "could", or a plain verb, depending on the construction. The Latin form tells you the mood; the construction tells you the meaning.

**Do not give *sum* a regular subjunctive.** *\*Sam*, *\*seam*, *\*esam* do not exist. It is *sim, sīs, sit*; and *possum* follows it: *possim*, not *\*possam*.

**Spanish helps, but not with the imperfect.** Spanish *ame*, *tema*, *viva* continue the Latin present subjunctive. But the Spanish imperfect subjunctive *amara* comes from the Latin pluperfect indicative (lesson 8, Wiktionary), not from *amārem*.

## Connections

Spanish, Portuguese and Italian keep the Latin present subjunctive with its vowel swap: Spanish *amar* has subjunctive *ame*, *comer* has *coma*, *vivir* has *viva*, just as Latin *amāre* has *amet* and *regere* has *regat*. French *qu'il aime* has lost the vowel in speech. Korean and Japanese express wishes and proposals with special endings (Japanese *-ō*, *ikō* "let's go"; Korean *-ja*, *gaja*), much as Latin uses the subjunctive.

## Exercises

1. Give the present subjunctive, third person singular and plural, of *laudō*, *videō*, *mittō*, *faciō*, *veniō*.
2. Give the imperfect subjunctive, first person singular, of *dūcō*, *sum*, *possum*, *volō*, *eō*.
3. Say "let us sing" and "let them not fight".
4. Translate *Dī tibi bene faciant.*
5. Translate *Utinam pater meus vīveret!*
6. *Regam*: give both possible meanings.
7. Correct *\*Amāmus!* meant as "let's love!" and *\*Nōn timeant* meant as "let them not be afraid".
8. Where does the *ē* of *amem* come from, and the *ī* of *sim*? Use Allen and Greenough.
9. Give the present subjunctive passive, third person singular, of *amō*, *regō* and *audiō*, and of the deponent *sequor*.

## Worked answers

1. *Laudet, laudent* (first conjugation, *ē*). *Videat, videant*; *mittat, mittant*; *faciat, faciant*; *veniat, veniant* (the others, *ā*).
2. *Dūcerem*, *essem*, *possem*, *vellem*, *īrem*: each is the present infinitive plus *-m* (ag1903 §168f).
3. *Cantēmus*: first conjugation, *ē*. *Nē pugnent*: subjunctive with *nē* (ag1903 §439).
4. "May the gods treat you well." *Faciant*: present subjunctive of wish. *Dī*: nominative plural of *deus*.
5. "If only my father were alive!" *Utinam* with the imperfect subjunctive: a wish about the present that cannot come true (lesson 19).
6. "I shall rule" (future indicative) and "let me rule" (present subjunctive). The first person singular is identical in the third conjugation (ag1903 §168d).
7. *Amēmus!*: the subjunctive of a first-conjugation verb has *ē*. *Nē timeant*: a subjunctive of command takes *nē*, not *nōn*.
8. The *ē* of *amem* is an inherited subjunctive sign, "the thematic vowel *e* lengthened"; the *ī* of *sim*, *velim* is an old optative sign (ag1903 §168e, notes 1-2). The *ā* of *moneam*, *regam* is of uncertain origin.
9. *Amētur*, *regātur*, *audiātur*: the passive *-tur* on the subjunctive stems. *Sequātur* "let him follow": the deponent uses the passive form with active meaning (lesson 13).
