## The idea

The present and imperfect subjunctive (lesson 17) are built on the present stem and present an action as going on or not yet done. The **perfect** and **pluperfect subjunctive** are built on the perfect stem and present it as completed, exactly as the perfect and pluperfect indicative do. Together the four tenses make the whole subjunctive:

- present: *amet* "let him love, he may love";
- imperfect: *amāret* "he would love, he might love";
- perfect: *amāverit* "he may have loved";
- pluperfect: *amāvisset* "he would have loved, he might have loved".

The English renderings are only rough. The meaning depends on the construction, and most perfect and pluperfect subjunctives you meet will be in subordinate clauses (part 6). In main clauses they have a few precise uses, which this lesson and the next show: forbidding (*nē fēceris* "don't do it"), past wishes (*utinam vēnisset* "if only he had come"), and possibilities about the past.

The forms are easy, because they are built from pieces you know. Allen and Greenough explain their history (ag1903 §169d-e):

- The **perfect subjunctive** in *-erim* was originally an optative of the old *s*-past, with *-er-* from older *-is-* (the same element as in the future perfect, lesson 8) and the optative sign *ī* (the *ī* of *sim*, lesson 17). Its endings therefore look like the future perfect's, with an *i* that can be long: *amāverim, amāverīs, amāverit*.
- The **pluperfect subjunctive** in *-issem* was formed on the analogy of the pluperfect indicative and "influenced by *essem*": *amāv-issem* is built like *ess-em*.

## How it is built

**Perfect subjunctive, active.** Perfect stem + *-erim, -erīs, -erit, -erīmus, -erītis, -erint*.

- *amāverim*, *monuerim*, *rēxerim*, *cēperim*, *audīverim*; *fuerim*.

Wiktionary marks the *i* of *-erīs, -erīmus, -erītis* long. Allen and Greenough note that short forms also occur, through confusion with the future perfect (ag1903 §169d, note). Except for the first person singular (*-erim* against *-erō*), the perfect subjunctive and the future perfect are written alike without macrons.

**Pluperfect subjunctive, active.** Perfect stem + *-issem, -issēs, -isset, -issēmus, -issētis, -issent*. An easy way to build it: take the perfect active infinitive (*amāvisse* "to have loved", met fully in part 6) and add the person endings, as you added them to *amāre* for the imperfect subjunctive.

- *amāvissem*, *monuissem*, *rēxissem*, *cēpissem*, *audīvissem*; *fuissem*.

**Passive and deponent.** Use the perfect participle with the present or imperfect subjunctive of *sum*:

- perfect: *amātus sim, sīs, sit*; *amātī sīmus, sītis, sint*;
- pluperfect: *amātus essem, essēs, esset*; *amātī essēmus, essētis, essent*.

The participle agrees with the subject, as in the indicative (lesson 12). Deponents use the same forms in an active sense: *secūtus sit* "he may have followed"; *nē sīs admīrātus* "don't be surprised" (ag1903 §450).

**Contracted forms.** As in the indicative (lesson 6), *v* between vowels often drops: *amārim* for *amāverim*, *amāssem* for *amāvissem*, *audīssem* for *audīvissem* (ag1903 §181).

**Forbidding.** The classical way to forbid with a subjunctive is *nē* + perfect subjunctive, second person: *nē fēceris* "don't do it" (ag1903 §450). Despite the perfect form, it refers to the future. Allen and Greenough describe it as "less formal and more peremptory" than *nōlī* with the infinitive (lesson 9).

Say "don't tell Marcus". "Tell" is *dīcō*, perfect *dīxī*: stem *dīx-*. Perfect subjunctive, second person singular: *dīxeris*. The person told is dative. With *nē*: *Nē Mārcō dīxeris.*

Say "if only you had stayed!". A wish about the past that did not come true: *utinam* + pluperfect subjunctive. *Maneō, mānsī*: *mānsissēs*. *Utinam mānsissēs!*

## Where it applies

- **Prohibitions:** *nē* + perfect subjunctive, second person: *nē fēceris*, *nē dīxeris*, *nē timueritis* (ag1903 §450). The other two ways are *nōlī* + infinitive (lesson 9) and *cavē* + present subjunctive.
- **Wishes about the past:** *utinam* + pluperfect subjunctive, for what did not happen: *utinam mānsissēs* (ag1903 §441; lesson 19).
- **Unfulfilled duty in the past:** the pluperfect, like the imperfect of lesson 17: *nē poposcissēs* "you should not have asked" (ag1903 §439b).
- **Possibility, softened statement:** the perfect subjunctive, "someone may say", "I would venture to say" (ag1903 §447; lesson 19).
- **Conditions contrary to past fact:** the pluperfect in both halves, "if he had come, he would have seen" (Dido's *tetigissent*; part 6).
- **Subordinate clauses in the past:** *cum* "when, since" + pluperfect subjunctive is one of the commonest constructions in Latin narrative (part 6). Reported questions and other clauses use the perfect and pluperfect for earlier events, following the sequence of tenses (part 6).

## Watch out

**Do not read *nē fēceris* as past.** It looks like "you may have done", but in a prohibition it means "don't do it", now or later (ag1903 §450).

**Do not use the present subjunctive with *nē* for a second-person prohibition in prose.** *\*Nē faciās* for "don't do it" belongs mainly to early Latin and poetry; the regular classical prose forms are *nē fēceris*, *nōlī facere* and *cavē faciās* (ag1903 §§439a, 450).

**Do not confuse the perfect subjunctive with the future perfect.** *Amāverit*, *amāverimus*, *amāverint* are written alike in both. The first person singular differs (*amāverim* subjunctive, *amāverō* future perfect), and Wiktionary marks a long *ī* in the subjunctive's *-erīs*, *-erīmus*, *-erītis*. Otherwise the construction decides: *nē* or a subordinating word points to the subjunctive.

**Do not build the pluperfect subjunctive on the present stem.** *\*Amāssem* is a real contracted form of *amāvissem*, but *\*regissem* or *\*capissem* are wrong: the perfect stems are *rēx-* and *cēp-*: *rēxissem*, *cēpissem*.

**Do not confuse *fuissem* and *essem*.** *Essem* is the imperfect subjunctive, "I would be"; *fuissem* is the pluperfect, "I would have been".

**Do not use *sum* in the indicative in the perfect passive subjunctive.** *\*Amātus est* in a subjunctive context is wrong; the subjunctive is *amātus sit* (perfect) or *amātus esset* (pluperfect).

**Do not translate the pluperfect subjunctive with "had" every time.** In a wish it is "if only ... had"; in a condition "would have"; after *cum* it is often a plain past or "since he had". The construction decides (part 6).

## Connections

Spanish and French build their perfect and pluperfect subjunctives with a helping verb: Spanish *que haya amado*, *si hubiera amado*; French *qu'il ait aimé*. Latin needs one word, *amāverit*, *amāvisset*. Dido's *tetigissent* would be Spanish *si hubieran tocado*, "if they had touched".

## Exercises

1. Give the perfect and pluperfect subjunctive, third person plural, of *mittō*, *videō*, *faciō* and *veniō*.
2. Give the perfect and pluperfect subjunctive passive, third person singular, of *capiō*, with a feminine subject.
3. Say "don't be afraid, boys", with *nē* and the perfect subjunctive.
4. Say "if only he had seen the city!".
5. Translate *Nē hoc fēceris*, and give the other two classical ways to say it.
6. *Cēperit*: give two possible meanings, and say what would decide.
7. Correct *\*Utinam venīret*, meant as "if only he had come".
8. Why does the pluperfect subjunctive look like *essem*? Give Allen and Greenough's explanation.
9. Translate *Utinam ille numquam nātus esset!*, and explain *nātus esset*.

## Worked answers

1. *Mīserint*, *mīsissent*; *vīderint*, *vīdissent*; *fēcerint*, *fēcissent*; *vēnerint*, *vēnissent*. Each is the perfect stem from the third principal part with *-erint* or *-issent* (ag1903 §169d-e).
2. *Capta sit* (perfect) and *capta esset* (pluperfect): the participle *capta*, feminine, with the present or imperfect subjunctive of *sum*.
3. *Nē timueritis, puerī.* *Timeō, timuī*: perfect stem *timu-*, perfect subjunctive second person plural *-eritis*; *nē* forbids (ag1903 §450).
4. *Utinam urbem vīdisset!* *Videō, vīdī*: pluperfect subjunctive *vīdisset*, for a past wish that was not fulfilled.
5. "Don't do this." The other ways: *Nōlī hoc facere* (the most courteous) and *Cavē hoc faciās* (ag1903 §450).
6. "He may have taken" (perfect subjunctive) or "he will have taken" (future perfect). The construction decides: with *nē*, *utinam* or a subordinating word expecting the subjunctive, it is subjunctive; in a plain statement about the future, it is future perfect.
7. *Utinam vēnisset!* A wish about the past needs the pluperfect subjunctive. *Utinam venīret* is "if only he were coming", a wish about the present (lesson 19).
8. Allen and Greenough suggest it was formed on the analogy of the pluperfect indicative and "influenced by *essem*", as *eram* is to *essem* (ag1903 §169e, note).
9. "If only he had never been born!" *Nātus esset* is the pluperfect subjunctive of the deponent *nāscor*: participle + *esset*, with an active meaning, "had been born" in English idiom.
