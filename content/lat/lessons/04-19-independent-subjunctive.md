## The idea

You can now build every subjunctive form. This lesson shows what the subjunctive does when it stands on its own, as the verb of a main clause. Allen and Greenough sort these uses into two families (ag1903 §§436, 445):

1. **The subjunctive of will and wish.** The speaker wants something: "let us go" (a proposal or command), "may he live" (a wish), "grant that it is so" (a concession), and the question "what am I to do?", which asks what one should will (ag1903 §§439-444).
2. **The subjunctive of possibility**, called the **potential**: the speaker presents something as merely conceivable: "someone might say", "you would have thought" (ag1903 §§445-447).

Allen and Greenough note that "no satisfactory connection between the Potential and the Hortatory and optative subjunctive has been traced" (ag1903 §445). The two families may have different roots in the parent language, which would fit what you saw in lesson 17: the Latin subjunctive is a merger of an old subjunctive and an old optative. Whether the two uses map neatly onto the two old moods is not settled, and this guide does not claim that they do.

One practical difference follows: the negative. With will and wish the negative is *nē* (*nē veniat* "let him not come"); with possibility and doubtful questions it is *nōn* (*nōn crēdās* "you would not believe") (ag1903 §§439, 441, 444, 446).

Each use has its tense rules, and they are logical: the present looks forward or to now, the imperfect and pluperfect to what is unreal or past.

## How it is built

**1. Proposals and commands (hortatory and jussive).** Present subjunctive. Negative *nē*.

- First person plural, "let us": *eāmus* "let's go".
- Third person, "let him, let them": *veniat*, *nē veniant* (ag1903 §439).
- Second person only for "you" in general ("one should ..."), and in prohibitions (*nē fēceris*, lesson 18) (ag1903 §439a).

**2. Wishes (optative).** Negative *nē*. Often introduced by *utinam* "if only" (ag1903 §§441-442).

- Present: a wish that may come true: *(utinam) valeās* "may you be well".
- Imperfect: a wish about the present that is not true: *utinam adessēs* "if only you were here".
- Pluperfect: a wish about the past that did not come true: *utinam adfuissēs* "if only you had been here".
- *Velim* "I should like" and *vellem* "I could wish" with a subjunctive or infinitive also express wishes (ag1903 §442b).

**3. Concessions.** Present for now, perfect for the past: *sit fūr* "suppose he is a thief", "granted he is a thief"; negative *nē* (ag1903 §440).

**4. Doubtful questions (deliberative).** In questions, often with a question word (part 6). Present for the present, imperfect for the past. Negative *nōn* (ag1903 §444).

- *Quid faciam?* "What am I to do?"
- *Quid facerem?* "What was I to do?"

**5. Possibility (potential).** Negative *nōn* (ag1903 §§446-447).

- Present or perfect for the present or near future, especially "I would say", "someone may say": *dīxerim* "I would say", *dīcat aliquis* "someone may say".
- Imperfect, second person singular "you" in general, for the past: *crēderēs* "you would have thought"; *vidērēs* "you could have seen".
- With *forsitan* "perhaps", the subjunctive is regular: *forsitan quaerātis* "perhaps you may ask" (ag1903 §447a). *Fortāsse* "perhaps" normally takes the indicative (ag1903 §447b).

**Choosing.** Ask what the speaker is doing.

1. Urging, ordering, proposing? Hortatory, present, *nē*.
2. Wishing? Optative: present if possible, imperfect or pluperfect with *utinam* if impossible.
3. Granting a point for argument's sake? Concessive.
4. Asking what to do? Deliberative.
5. Saying what could be or could have been? Potential, *nōn*.

Say "may you be happy". A possible wish: present. *Sīs laetus.* Say "if only you had been happy". An impossible past wish: pluperfect with *utinam*. *Utinam laetus fuissēs.*

## Where it applies

- **Hortatory and jussive:** proposals in the first person plural (*eāmus*, *amēmus*, *vīvāmus*), orders in the third person (*veniat*), general advice in the second person singular (ag1903 §439).
- **Unfulfilled obligation:** imperfect or pluperfect, "he should have": *potius docēret* (lesson 17; ag1903 §439b).
- **Concession:** *sit fūr, at bonus imperātor est*, "granted he is a thief, he is still a good general" (Cicero, quoted at ag1903 §440).
- **Wishes:** present (*dī tē servent*), imperfect and pluperfect with *utinam* (*utinam adessēs*, *utinam vīdissēs*); *velim*, *vellem*, *nōllem*, *māllem* (ag1903 §§441-442).
- **Doubtful questions:** *quid faciam?*, *quid facerem?*; also indignant questions: *ego nōn venīrem?* "was I not to come?" (ag1903 §444).
- **Modest statements:** *dīxerim* "I would say", *velim* "I should like" (ag1903 §447).
- **"You would think", "you could have seen":** *crēdās*, *crēderēs*, *vidērēs* (ag1903 §447).
- **With *forsitan*:** subjunctive (ag1903 §447a).
- **The same forms in subordinate clauses** have other jobs: purpose, result, *cum*, conditions (part 6). A conditional sentence ("if he came, he would see") is the potential with an "if" clause added (ag1903 §447, note).

## Watch out

**Do not use *nōn* with a wish or a command.** *\*Nōn veniat* for "let him not come" and *\*utinam nōn* for "if only not" are not the regular forms; the negative is *nē*: *nē veniat*, *utinam nē* (ag1903 §§439, 441-442; *utinam nōn* occurs occasionally).

**Do not use *nē* with a potential or a doubtful question.** *\*Nē crēdās* for "you would not believe" reads as a command, "do not believe!", not as a possibility. The potential takes *nōn*.

**Do not use the present for an impossible wish.** *\*Utinam adsīs* for "if only you were here (but you aren't)" turns it into a possible wish, "I hope you will be here". Impossible present wishes need the imperfect: *utinam adessēs*.

**Do not use the imperfect for an impossible past wish.** *\*Utinam vidērēs* is about now; the past needs the pluperfect: *utinam vīdissēs*.

**Do not use the indicative for "let us".** *\*Īmus!* is "we go"; "let's go" is *eāmus*.

**Do not translate the potential with English "can" every time.** *Crēderēs* is "you would have thought", not "you could believe". Choose "would", "might", "could" or "may" by the context (ag1903 §445).

**Do not read every main-clause subjunctive as a wish.** *Quid faciam?* is a question about what to do; *dīxerim* is a modest assertion; *crēderēs* a possibility. The person, tense, negative and the presence of *utinam* or a question word tell you which.

**Spanish *ojalá* is a good guide.** Spanish *¡ojalá vengas!* (possible) and *¡ojalá vinieras!* (impossible now) match Latin *utinam veniās* and *utinam venīrēs*. Spanish *cantemos* "let's sing" also matches Latin *cantēmus*.

## Connections

Spanish and French use their subjunctive in main clauses mostly after a particle: Spanish *¡que te vaya bien!*, French *qu'il vienne !*, "let him come". Latin needs no particle. Japanese and Korean have dedicated endings for "let's" (Japanese *-ō*, Korean *-ja*) and for wishes and guesses (Japanese *-darō* "probably", Korean *-gesseo-* "will, would"), dividing up the same territory as the Latin subjunctive in a different way.

## Exercises

1. Say "let us praise the gods" and "let the soldiers not flee".
2. Say "if only my brother were here!" and "if only my brother had been here!".
3. Translate *Utinam nē bellum gessissēmus!*
4. Translate *Quid dīcerem?* and say what kind of subjunctive it is and why it is imperfect.
5. Translate *Vidērēs urbem ārdentem* (*ārdentem* "burning" is a present participle, part 5).
6. Correct *\*Nōn timeāmus* meant as "let's not be afraid".
7. Correct *\*Utinam pater meus vīvit* meant as "if only my father were alive".
8. Name the use in each: *Sīs laetus*; *Sit sānē miser* (*sānē* "certainly, granted"); *Dīxerim*; *Nē veniat*.
9. Why does the potential take *nōn* and the wish take *nē*? Say what Allen and Greenough say and what they leave open.

## Worked answers

1. *Deōs laudēmus*: hortatory, first person plural, first conjugation *ē*. *Nē mīlitēs fugiant*: jussive, third person, negative *nē* (ag1903 §439).
2. *Utinam frāter meus adesset!*: imperfect, a wish about the present that is not true. *Utinam frāter meus adfuisset!*: pluperfect, a past wish (ag1903 §442).
3. "If only we had not waged the war!" Pluperfect subjunctive with *utinam nē*: an unfulfilled wish about the past.
4. "What was I to say?" A deliberative question; the imperfect puts the doubt in the past, as *quid facerem?* does (ag1903 §444, note).
5. "You could have seen the city burning." Potential of the past: imperfect, second person singular, "you" in general (ag1903 §447).
6. *Nē timeāmus.* A proposal takes *nē*, not *nōn* (ag1903 §439).
7. *Utinam pater meus vīveret!* An impossible present wish needs the imperfect subjunctive, not the indicative.
8. *Sīs laetus* "may you be happy": wish (optative). *Sit sānē miser* "granted, let him be wretched": concession (*sānē* "certainly, granted"). *Dīxerim* "I would say": potential, a modest assertion. *Nē veniat* "let him not come": jussive, with *nē* (ag1903 §439).
9. The wish and the command express will, whose negative is *nē*; the potential and the doubtful question present something as possible or in doubt, and take *nōn* (ag1903 §§439, 441, 444, 446). Allen and Greenough say that no satisfactory connection between the potential and the hortatory and optative uses has been traced (ag1903 §445); the history behind the two negatives is left open.
