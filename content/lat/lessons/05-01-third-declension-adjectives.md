## The idea

The adjectives you know so far, *bonus, -a, -um* and *miser, -a, -um*, borrow their endings from the first and second declensions (part 2, lesson 7). Latin has a second large class of adjectives that borrow theirs from the third declension. *Fortis* "brave" is one: *vir fortis*, *mīlitem fortem*, *mīlitum fortium*.

Nothing about agreement changes. The adjective still matches its noun in gender, number and case (ag1903 §286). What changes is the set of endings it uses to do it. A third-declension adjective goes with a noun of any declension: *nauta fortis* "a brave sailor", *fēmina fortis* "a brave woman", *in bellō ācrī* "in a fierce war". Agreement is not rhyme, as part 2 said. Here the endings will often not rhyme at all.

The third declension gives these adjectives fewer gender forms. In that declension, masculine and feminine nouns already decline alike: *rēx* (m.) and *lēx* (f.) have the same endings. An adjective built on the same endings therefore has one form for masculine and feminine together. Neuters differ from them only where neuter nouns differ, in the nominative and accusative (part 3, lesson 7). So most third-declension adjectives have two sets of forms, not three: *omnis* for masculine and feminine, *omne* for neuter.

These adjectives are mostly i-stems. You met the i-stems in part 3, lesson 8, with their four signs: ablative *-ī*, neuter plural *-ia*, genitive plural *-ium*, accusative plural *-īs* beside *-ēs*. Allen and Greenough call adjectives of this class "true i-stems" that keep all four (ag1903 §114a). Part 3 quoted their remark that nouns tended to lose their i-forms while adjectives gained them (ag1903 §73). The result is that the ablative singular of an adjective often differs from that of a noun in the same declension: *cum mīlite fortī* "with a brave soldier", noun *-e*, adjective *-ī*.

## How it is built

Grammars sort these adjectives by how many different forms they have in the nominative singular. They call these forms "terminations" (ag1903 §114).

**Two forms: *omnis, omne*.** This is the commonest type.

1. The masculine and feminine nominative ends in *-is*: *omnis*, *fortis*, *gravis*.
2. The neuter nominative and accusative singular end in *-e*: *omne*, *forte*.
3. Take the stem by removing *-is*: *omn-*, *fort-*.
4. Add the i-stem endings: accusative *omnem*, genitive *omnis*, dative and ablative *omnī*; plural *omnēs*, neuter *omnia*, genitive *omnium*, dative and ablative *omnibus*.

**Three forms: *ācer, ācris, ācre*.** A small group adds a separate masculine nominative in *-er* (ag1903 §115).

1. Masculine nominative *ācer*, feminine *ācris*, neuter *ācre*.
2. Every other form is built on the feminine, exactly like *omnis*: *ācrem*, *ācris*, *ācrī*, *ācrēs*, *ācria*, *ācrium*.
3. As with *miser* and *pulcher* in part 2, the feminine shows whether the *e* stays: *ācer, ācris* drops it; *celer, celeris* "swift" keeps it.

The separate masculine matters only in the nominative and vocative singular. Allen and Greenough describe it as a comparatively late formation: early Latin and the poets sometimes use *ācris* or *ācer* for both genders (ag1903 §115 note 1). They do not explain why the *-er* form arose, and this guide does not guess.

**One form: *fēlīx*, genitive *fēlīcis*.** Here the nominative singular is the same for all three genders (ag1903 §117).

1. The dictionary gives the genitive, because the nominative hides the stem: *fēlīx, fēlīcis* "lucky", stem *fēlīc-*; *ingēns, ingentis* "huge", stem *ingent-*.
2. Add the endings to the stem: *fēlīcem*, *fēlīcis*, *fēlīcī*; plural *fēlīcēs*, *fēlīcium*, *fēlīcibus*.
3. The neuter differs only where neuters always differ: its accusative singular is the same as its nominative, *fēlīx*, and its plural ends in *-ia*, *fēlīcia*.

These are consonant stems at heart, like *rēx*, but they have taken over the i-forms: usually ablative *-ī* (often also *-e*), neuter plural *-ia*, genitive plural *-ium* (ag1903 §117). A few keep the pure consonant pattern: *vetus, veteris* "old", *dīves, dīvitis* "rich" and *pauper, pauperis* "poor" have ablative *-e* and genitive plural *-um*: *vetere*, *veterum* (ag1903 §121). *Vetus* is an old *s*-stem whose *s* became *r* between vowels, as in *corpus, corporis* (ag1903 §119 note; part 3, lesson 7).

**Agreement step by step.** Say "the brave soldiers were carrying everything".

1. Subject *mīlitēs*, masculine nominative plural. *Fortis* in the masculine nominative plural: *fortēs*. *Mīlitēs fortēs*.
2. "Everything" is "all things": *omnia*, neuter accusative plural, used as a noun.
3. Verb: imperfect of *ferō*, *ferēbant*. *Mīlitēs fortēs omnia ferēbant*.

Say "in every war". *Bellum* is neuter, second declension; *in* "in" takes the ablative. *Omnis* in the neuter ablative singular: *omnī*. *In omnī bellō*. The two endings differ, and both are right.

## Where it applies

- **With nouns of every declension.** *Nauta fortis*, *bellum ācre*, *rēx fēlīx*, *manus fortis*, *diēs fēlīx*. The adjective takes its own ending for the noun's gender, number and case (ag1903 §286).
- **"All", "every", "whole".** *Omnis* in the plural means "all": *omnēs cīvēs*. In the singular it usually means "every": *omnis homō* "every man". With a singular noun naming one thing it can mean "as a whole": *Gallia est omnis dīvīsa* "Gaul as a whole is divided", Caesar's first sentence (part 3, lesson 8). *Tōtus* "whole" comes in lesson 2.
- **Standing as nouns.** The masculine plural names people, the neuter plural things: *omnēs* "everyone", *omnia* "everything", *fortēs* "the brave", *dīvitēs* "the rich". You met *Fortīs fortūna adiuvat* in the part 2 review. Lesson 2 of this part develops this.
- **As predicate adjectives after *sum*** and verbs like *fīō*, in the nominative: *Mīles fortis est*; *Iter breve fit*.
- **Where English uses an adverb.** Latin often puts an adjective, agreeing with the subject, where English says how someone acts: *Trīstis at ille* "but he, sadly". Allen and Greenough note that an adjective agreeing with the subject or object often has the force of an adverb (ag1903 §290). Lesson 3 gives the true adverbs.
- **With nouns of different genders.** An attributive adjective agrees with the nearest noun: *fīliī et fīliae fēlīcēs*, "lucky sons and daughters", takes its gender from *fīliae*, though here the form would be the same for either. A predicate adjective is masculine plural if the nouns name living beings, neuter plural if they name things (ag1903 §287): *Pater et māter laetī sunt*; *Mūrus et turris alta sunt*.
- **With present participles.** Participles in *-ns*, such as *amāns* "loving", decline like *ingēns* (ag1903 §117b). They come in lesson 7 of this part.
- **Comparatives.** Forms like *fortior* "braver" decline in the third declension too, but as consonant stems. Lesson 4.

## Watch out

**Do not give the adjective the noun's ending.** *\*Nauta fortus* and *\*fēmina forta* force a first- or second-declension ending onto *fortis*. A third-declension adjective keeps its own endings: *nauta fortis*, *fēmina fortis*.

**Do not use the ablative *-e* of nouns.** *\*Cum mīlite forte* follows *mīlite*. The adjective takes *-ī*: *cum mīlite fortī*, *in omnī bellō* (ag1903 §114a). Poets sometimes have *-e*; prose rarely does (ag1903 §116 note).

**Do not give *vetus*, *dīves* and *pauper* the i-forms.** *\*Veteria*, *\*in urbe veterī*, *\*pauperium*: these three keep the consonant pattern, *vetera*, *in urbe vetere*, *pauperum* (ag1903 §121). (Wiktionary allows *dīvitī* beside *dīvite*.)

**Do not use *ācer* for the feminine or neuter.** *\*Fēmina ācer*, *\*bellum ācer*: the *-er* form is masculine only. *Fēmina ācris*, *bellum ācre*. In early Latin and in poetry you may meet one form for both genders (ag1903 §115 note 1).

**Do not use the neuter *-e* in the plural.** *\*Omne* is neuter singular only. "All things" is *omnia*; "all the gifts" *omnia dōna*.

**Do not make a one-form neuter accusative in *-em*.** *\*Animal ingentem videō* treats the neuter like a masculine. Neuters have the same form in the nominative and accusative: *animal ingēns videō*.

**Do not read the accusative plural *-īs* as a dative.** *Omnīs* and *fortīs* are accusative plurals, the i-stem alternative to *-ēs*. The dative and ablative plural of these adjectives is always *-ibus*: *omnibus*, *fortibus*.

**Do not take the genitive *omnis* for the nominative.** *Omnis* is also the genitive singular: *mors omnis hominis* "the death of every man". The noun beside it decides.

**Do not stretch *omnis* to mean "each one separately".** *Omnis* is "every", "all". For "each" taken one at a time, Latin has *quisque* (lesson 10 of this part).

## Connections

French and Spanish inherited this split. Latin *fortis* gives French *fort*, *forte* and Spanish *fuerte*, and *dulcis* gives *doux*, *douce* and *dulce* (Wiktionary). Spanish *fuerte* and *dulce* still have one form for masculine and feminine. French went the other way and made a feminine on the model of *bon*, *bonne*.

## Exercises

1. Make *fortis* agree with *puella*, *servus*, *bellum* and *manus* in the nominative singular, and give each pair in the ablative singular.
2. Decline *ācer* with *equus* in the singular.
3. Give the accusative singular and genitive plural of *audāx* with *mīles*, and of *ingēns* with *saxum*.
4. Translate *Omnēs mīlitēs parēs nōn sunt*.
5. Say "the old friends were living in a rich city".
6. Say "every journey is short to happy men".
7. Correct *\*In urbe ingente mīlitēs fortī sunt*.
8. Take apart *Gravia et turpia ferunt* and translate it.

## Worked answers

1. *Puella fortis*, *servus fortis*, *bellum forte*, *manus fortis* (*manus* is feminine). Ablatives: *puellā fortī*, *servō fortī*, *bellō fortī*, *manū fortī*. The adjective has one ablative, *-ī*, for every gender.
2. *Equus ācer*, vocative *eque ācer*, accusative *equum ācrem*, genitive *equī ācris*, dative *equō ācrī*, ablative *equō ācrī*. Only the nominative and vocative show the masculine *ācer*; the rest is built on *ācr-*.
3. *Mīlitem audācem*, *mīlitum audācium*. *Saxum ingēns* (neuter: accusative = nominative), *saxōrum ingentium*. The genitive plural *-ium* is an i-form these consonant stems have taken over.
4. *Omn-ēs mīlit-ēs*: all the soldiers, nominative plural. *Par-ēs*: equal, a predicate adjective. "Not all soldiers are equal." *Pār, paris* takes *-ēs* like any third-declension plural. Its vowel is long only in the one-syllable nominative *pār*; the other forms have short *a*: *parem*, *parēs* (Wiktionary). *Pārēs* with a long *ā* would be "you obey", from *pāreō*.
5. "Old friends": *amīcī veterēs*, masculine nominative plural. "In a rich city": *in urbe dīvite*, with the consonant-stem ablative *-e*. "Were living": imperfect *habitābant*. *Amīcī veterēs in urbe dīvite habitābant.*
6. *Iter*, neuter, so *omne iter*; predicate *breve*. "To happy men": dative plural of *fēlīx* used as a noun, *fēlīcibus*. *Omne iter fēlīcibus breve est.*
7. *Ingēns* takes the adjective ablative *-ī*: *in urbe ingentī*. *Fortis* must agree with *mīlitēs*, nominative plural: *fortēs*. *In urbe ingentī mīlitēs fortēs sunt*, "in the huge city the soldiers are brave". (*Ingente* also occurs, ag1903 §117; *ingentī* is the regular form.)
8. *Grav-ia*: heavy things, neuter accusative plural used as a noun. *Turp-ia*: shameful things, the same. *Fer-unt*: they bear. "They endure hard and shameful things." The neuter plural *-ia* marks both as "things".
