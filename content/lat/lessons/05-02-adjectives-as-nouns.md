## The idea

This lesson has two halves. Both are about adjectives that behave a little like something else.

**Adjectives as nouns.** English needs a noun to lean on: "the brave ones", "good people", "many things". Latin does not. An adjective's ending already says gender and number, and gender tells you what kind of thing is meant. So the adjective can stand alone. Allen and Greenough give the rule: the masculine usually means men or people of that kind, the feminine women, the neuter things (ag1903 §288). *Bonī* is "good men, good people"; *bona* is "good things", and so "goods, property"; *omnia* is "all things, everything". A grammar calls this the **substantive** use of an adjective: using it as a noun. You have met it already in *omnia vincit Amor* and *Fortīs fortūna adiuvat*.

The plural is the normal case. Allen and Greenough note that the singular is comparatively rare except in the neuter and in words that have become nouns in practice (ag1903 §288 note). *Sapiēns* "a wise man" and *amīcus* "a friend" are such words.

**The ūnus group.** Nine common adjectives decline like *bonus* except in three places: the genitive singular ends in *-īus*, the dative singular in *-ī*, for all genders, and *alius* has a neuter in *-d*, *aliud* (ag1903 §113). The nine are *ūnus* "one", *sōlus* "alone", *tōtus* "whole", *ūllus* "any", *nūllus* "no", *alius* "other", *alter* "the other of two", *uter* "which of two", *neuter* "neither".

Why should nine adjectives look different? Because these endings belong to pronouns. You met them in part 3: *illīus*, *illī*, *illud*; *ipsīus*, *ipsī*. Allen and Greenough say the genitive *-īus*, the dative *-ī* and the neuter *-d* of the ūnus group are "pronominal in origin" (ag1903 §113b). The nine words do a pronoun's work: they pick out or count things rather than describe them. Grammars therefore call them **pronominal adjectives**: adjectives that take some endings from the pronouns.

## How it is built

**Reading an adjective as a noun.**

1. Find an adjective with no noun to agree with: *Fortēs laudāmus*.
2. Read its gender and number. *Fortēs* here is masculine (or feminine) accusative plural.
3. Supply the noun the gender suggests: masculine plural, "people"; neuter plural, "things". "We praise brave people", "we praise the brave".
4. If the sentence or context names a group, take the adjective from that group: in an account of a battle, *nostrī* means "our men", *multī* "many of them".

**Declining the ūnus group.**

1. Start from *bonus*: *ūnus, ūna, ūnum*; *ūnum, ūnam*; ablative *ūnō, ūnā*. The plural is all regular: *ūnī, ūnae, ūna* (ag1903 §113a).
2. Replace the genitive singular of all three genders with *-īus*: *ūnīus*, *tōtīus*, *nūllīus*.
3. Replace the dative singular of all three genders with *-ī*: *ūnī*, *tōtī*, *nūllī*.
4. For *alius* only, make the neuter nominative and accusative *aliud*.
5. Words in *-er* keep or drop their *e* as the feminine shows: *alter, altera*, genitive *alterīus*; *uter, utra*, genitive *utrīus*; *neuter, neutra*, genitive *neutrīus*.

Allen and Greenough print the genitive with a long *ī* and note that poets may shorten it, often in *alterius* and regularly in *utriusque* (ag1903 §113c). Wiktionary marks the vowel as either long or short. This guide prints *-īus*.

*Alius* has its own gap. Its genitive *alīus* is rare. Latin uses *alterīus* instead, or, for "belonging to another", the adjective *aliēnus* (ag1903 §113d).

**The words of two.** *Alter*, *uter* and *neuter* all deal with pairs. Allen and Greenough call *alter* a comparative form, with the same suffix as Greek *-τερος* (ag1903 §132 note). Wiktionary traces *uter* and *alter* to Proto-Italic *\*kʷoteros* and *\*aliteros*, both with this *-ter-*. *Neuter* is *ne* "not" + *uter*: "not either". *Uterque* is *uter* + *-que*, "each of two" (Wiktionary).

Say "the courage of the whole army".

1. "The whole army": *exercitus tōtus*. "Of": genitive singular.
2. *Exercitus* is fourth declension: *exercitūs*. *Tōtus* takes the pronominal genitive: *tōtīus*.
3. *Virtūs tōtīus exercitūs*.

## Where it applies

**Adjectives as nouns** (ag1903 §§288-289):

- **Masculine plural for people**: *bonī* "good men", *multī* "many people", *omnēs* "everyone", *nostrī* "our men", *Rōmānī* "the Romans", *mortālēs* "mortals", *paucī* "a few".
- **Masculine singular for one person**, mostly in words that have become nouns: *sapiēns* "a wise man", *amīcus* "a friend", *familiāris* "a close friend", *propinquus* "a relative" (ag1903 §288a). Such words can take their own adjectives: *amīcus meus fidēlis*.
- **Feminine for women**: *Rōmānae* "Roman women".
- **Neuter singular for an act or a quality**: *bonum* "a good thing", *malum* "an evil", *honestum* "what is honourable" (ag1903 §289a).
- **Neuter plural for things in general**: *omnia* "everything", *multa* "many things", *bona* "property", *vēra* "true things, the truth" (ag1903 §289b). Allen and Greenough's *Omnēs fortia laudant* is "everyone praises brave deeds", that is, bravery.
- **A neuter predicate with a noun of another gender**: *Trīste lupus stabulīs*, "the wolf is a grim thing for the sheepfolds" (Vergil, Eclogue 3.80, quoted in ag1903 §289c).
- **Words now used only as nouns**: *superī* "the gods above", *maiōrēs* "ancestors", *posterī* "descendants", *līberī* "children" (from *līber* "free": Wiktionary), *prīvātus* "a private citizen".
- **Participles**, which are adjectives, do the same: *victī* "the defeated" (Vergil above). Lesson 7 adds *amantēs* "lovers".

**The ūnus group** (ag1903 §§113, 134a, 314-315):

- ***ūnus*** "one", also "alone, only": *ūnus ex omnibus* "one out of all". Its plural *ūnī* goes with plural-only nouns: *ūna castra* "one camp" (ag1903 §134a). Counting comes in lesson 5.
- ***sōlus*** "alone, only": *Sōlus in silvā erat*.
- ***tōtus*** "whole, entire": *tōta urbs* "the whole city".
- ***nūllus*** "no, not any": *nūllum perīculum* "no danger". As a noun it supplies the genitive and ablative of *nēmō*: *nūllīus* "of no one", *nūllō* "by no one" (ag1903 §314a).
- ***ūllus*** "any", mostly after a negative: *sine ūllō perīculō* "without any danger" (ag1903 §312). Lesson 10 explains.
- ***alius*** "other, another" of any number; *aliī … aliī* "some … others"; *alius aliud* "different people, different things" (ag1903 §315).
- ***alter*** "the other of two", "the second": *alter … alter* "the one … the other".
- ***uter*** "which of the two?", in questions (part 6); ***neuter*** "neither"; ***uterque*** "each of two, both". In *uterque* only the first part declines: *utrīusque*, *utrīque*, *utrumque*. The plural *utrīque* means "both sides".
- ***cēterī*** and ***reliquī*** "the rest, the others" (ag1903 §315) are ordinary *bonus* adjectives, not pronominal.
- ***plērīque*** "most, the majority": the plural of *plērusque*, with *-que* fixed on.

## Watch out

**Do not give the ūnus group the *bonus* genitive and dative.** *\*Tōtī urbis*, *\*ūnō puerō dedit* (as dative): *tōtīus urbis*, *ūnī puerō dedit*. The regular forms are sometimes found, such as *aliae* (genitive and dative feminine) and *aliō* (dative masculine) (ag1903 §113 note), but they are not the norm.

**Do not make *aliud* masculine, or *alium* neuter.** *\*Aliud amīcum*, *\*alium cōnsilium*: *alium amīcum*, *aliud cōnsilium*.

**Do not use *alius* for the other of two.** Spanish *otro* and French *autre* cover both. *\*Alius cōnsul* for "the other consul" is wrong when there are two. *Alter cōnsul*.

**Do not use *alīus* as the everyday genitive of *alius*.** Use *alterīus*, or *aliēnus*: *aliēna pecūnia* "another man's money" (ag1903 §113d).

**Do not use *nūllus* alone for "no one" in the nominative.** *\*Nūllus vēnit* is rare in classical prose; "no one came" is *Nēmō vēnit*. But in the genitive and ablative it is *nūllīus* and *nūllō*, not *nēminis* and *nēmine* (ag1903 §314a).

**Do not use *ūllus* in a plain positive statement.** *\*Ūllum perīculum videō* for "I see some danger" is wrong: *ūllus* means "any" where the sense is negative. Use *perīculum videō*, or wait for *aliquis* (lesson 10).

**Do not give *uterque* a plural verb by English analogy.** "Both brothers came" is *Uterque frāter vēnit*, singular. *\*Uterque frāter vēnērunt* is rare. For two groups, use the plural *utrīque*.

**Do not read *bonī* as "goods".** *Bonī* is masculine, "good men"; "goods, property" is the neuter *bona*.

**Do not use the singular for a class of people.** French *le pauvre* and Spanish *el pobre* can mean "the poor" as a class. Latin uses the plural: *pauperēs* (ag1903 §288 note).

**Do not leave an adjective alone where its gender is hidden.** *Omnium* can be masculine or neuter: "of all men" or "of all things". Allen and Greenough say to add a noun when that is unclear: *potentia omnium rērum* "power over everything" (ag1903 §288b). The same holds for every genitive, dative and ablative plural.

## Connections

French *nul* continues *nūllus* (Spanish *nulo* was borrowed from the Latin later), and French *nul* still means "no one" or "worthless". Spanish *otro* and French *autre* come from *alter*, not *alius* (Wiktionary): the word for "the other of two" took over the whole meaning.

## Exercises

1. Translate *Multī veniunt, paucī manent*.
2. Give the genitive and dative singular of *nūlla causa* and *alter frāter*.
3. Say "the hope of the whole city".
4. Say "one brother is brave, the other is wretched".
5. Correct *\*Alius cōnsul in urbe erat, alius in castrīs* (there were two consuls).
6. Translate *Aliī aliud dīcunt*.
7. Translate *Fortēs bona sua līberīs dant* and say how you know who gets what.
8. What is *ūnī* in *ūnī puellae librum dedit*, and why is it not nominative plural?

## Worked answers

1. "Many come, few stay." *Multī* and *paucī* are masculine plurals standing alone, so they mean people (ag1903 §288).
2. *Nūllīus causae*, *nūllī causae*; *alterīus frātris*, *alterī frātrī*. The pronominal adjective takes *-īus* and *-ī*; the noun keeps its own endings.
3. *Spēs tōtīus urbis*. *Tōtus* agrees with *urbs*, genitive singular: *tōtīus*, not *\*tōtae*.
4. *Alter frāter fortis est, alter miser*. Two brothers, so *alter … alter*. *Fortis* is the masculine of *fortis, forte* (lesson 1).
5. *Alter cōnsul in urbe erat, alter in castrīs.* Of two, the word is *alter* (ag1903 §315).
6. "Different people say different things." *Alius* repeated in another case gives two statements in one (ag1903 §315c).
7. "Brave men give their property to their children." *Fortēs*: masculine nominative plural standing alone, so the subject, people. *Bona*: neuter accusative plural, "goods". *Sua*: their own, agreeing with *bona*. *Līberīs*: dative plural of *līberī*, the receivers.
8. *Ūnī* is the dative singular feminine, agreeing with *puellae*: "he gave a book to one girl". The verb *dedit* is singular, so neither word can be its subject; and *puellae* with *librum* and *dedit* is the person given to. The ūnus group has dative *-ī* in every gender.
