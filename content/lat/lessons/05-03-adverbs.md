## The idea

An **adverb** is a word that tells you how, when, where or how much something happens: "bravely", "often", "there", "very". You already know many Latin ones: *saepe*, *semper*, *nunc*, *ibi*, *bene*, *nōn*. The name says what they are for. Allen and Greenough derive it from *ad* "to" and *verbum* "verb": an adverb is attached to a verb (ag1903 §320). It can also modify an adjective, *valdē fortis* "very brave", or another adverb, *nimis graviter* "too severely" (ag1903 §§320-321).

An adverb never agrees with anything. It has one form. That is the point of it: an adjective changes to match a noun; an adverb describes the action and has nothing to match.

Where do adverbs come from? Allen and Greenough call them "petrified case forms" (ag1903 §320). Most Latin adverbs began as a case of an adjective or noun, frozen in one form when the language stopped feeling it as a case. You can still see the cases in many of them:

- the regular ending *-ē*, as in *lātē* "widely", is a relic of an old ablative in *-ēd* (ag1903 §214a). Wiktionary traces *bene* "well" to Archaic Latin *\*duenēd*, from *duenos*, the older form of *bonus*.
- *multum* "much" and *facile* "easily" are neuter accusatives (ag1903 §214d);
- *vulgō* "commonly", *forte* "by chance" and *adeō* "to such a degree" are ablatives (ag1903 §§214e, 215);
- *magnopere* "greatly" is two words grown together, *magnō opere* "with great effort" (Wiktionary).

The third-declension ending *-ter*, as in *fortiter* "bravely", is harder. Allen and Greenough suggest it may be the same suffix as the *-ter-* of *uter* and *alter* (lesson 2), but they say "perhaps" (ag1903 §214b note). Its origin is not settled.

## How it is built

**From a first- or second-declension adjective: stem + *-ē*** (ag1903 §214a).

1. Take the adjective: *lātus, -a, -um* "wide".
2. Find the stem by removing the ending of the feminine: *lāt-*. The feminine matters for adjectives in *-er*: *miser, misera* gives *miser-*; *pulcher, pulchra* gives *pulchr-*.
3. Add *-ē*: *lātē* "widely", *miserē* "wretchedly", *pulchrē* "beautifully", *vērē* "truly", *longē* "far".

**From a third-declension adjective: stem + *-iter*, or *-ter* after *nt*** (ag1903 §214b).

1. Take the adjective and find the stem from the genitive: *fortis, fortis* gives *fort-*; *ācer, ācris* gives *ācr-*; *fēlīx, fēlīcis* gives *fēlīc-*.
2. Add *-iter*: *fortiter*, *ācriter* "keenly", *fēlīciter* "luckily", *celeriter* "quickly", *breviter* "briefly".
3. If the stem ends in *nt*, add only *-er*: *sapiēns, sapientis* gives *sapienter* "wisely"; *potēns* gives *potenter*. The *e* is short (Wiktionary): the long *ē* of *sapiēns* stands only before *ns* (part 1).

**Irregular and fixed forms.** A few common adjectives make their adverbs another way. Learn these as words.

1. *bonus* gives *bene* "well"; *malus* gives *male* "badly". Allen and Greenough note that the *-ē* of adverbs is long but that these two have a short *e* (ag1903 §604e).
2. *magnus* gives *magnopere* "greatly"; *multus* gives *multum* "much"; *parvus* gives *parum* "too little".
3. *facilis* "easy" gives *facile* "easily", its neuter accusative (ag1903 §214d).
4. *alius* gives *aliter* "otherwise", from an old stem *ali-* (ag1903 §214b).
5. *rārus* "rare" gives *rārō* "rarely", an ablative.

Say "the soldiers fought bravely".

1. The verb: "fought", a finished action, perfect *pugnāvērunt*.
2. "Bravely" describes how they fought: an adverb, not an adjective.
3. *Fortis* is third declension: *fort-* + *-iter*, *fortiter*.
4. An adverb usually stands before its verb: *Mīlitēs fortiter pugnāvērunt*.

## Where it applies

- **Modifying a verb**, the core use: *celeriter currit*, *fortiter pugnat* (ag1903 §320).
- **Modifying an adjective**, to show degree: *valdē fortis* "very brave", *admodum facilis* "quite easy" (ag1903 §320).
- **Modifying another adverb**: *nimis graviter* "too severely", *valdē bene* "very well" (ag1903 §320).
- **Modifying a noun used as an adjective**, rarely: *admodum puer* "quite a boy, very young" (ag1903 §321c).
- **Modifying a participle used as a noun**: *bene facta* "good deeds", literally "things well done". Allen and Greenough note that *dictum* and *factum* used as nouns take adverbs rather than adjectives (ag1903 §321b).
- **After *sum*, a few adverbs do the work of adjectives**: *Haec palam sunt*, "these things are public knowledge" (ag1903 §321d). This is limited to a few words such as *palam* and *obviam*.
- **Whole-sentence adverbs.** Some comment on the whole statement: *scīlicet* "of course", from *scī licet* "you may know" (ag1903 §216); *fortāsse* "perhaps". These often sit second, like *autem*.
- **Neuter accusatives of degree**: *multum* "much", *paulum* "a little", *tantum* "so much; only", *nihil* "not at all" (ag1903 §214d).
- **Adverbs of place**: *ibi*, *hīc*, *illīc* "there", *procul* "far off". Many come from pronoun stems (part 3).
- **Adverbs of time and frequency**: *semper*, *saepe*, *interdum* "sometimes", *rārō*, *plērumque* "generally", *prōtinus* "at once", *dēmum* "at last".
- **Adverbs that join**, such as *tamen*, *deinde*, *itaque*. Lesson 6 treats them.
- **The adverb's job done by an adjective.** Latin often uses an adjective agreeing with the subject where English uses an adverb: *Laetī audiunt* "they hear gladly" (ag1903 §290; lesson 1). Both are correct. The adjective describes the person; the adverb describes the action.

## Watch out

**Do not make the adverb agree.** *\*Mīlitēs fortēs pugnāvērunt* is a correct sentence, but it means "the brave soldiers fought" or "the soldiers fought as brave men". For "bravely", use the adverb, which never changes: *fortiter*.

**Do not add *-ē* to a third-declension adjective.** *\*Fortē* and *\*celerē* treat *fortis* and *celer* like *lātus*. Third-declension adjectives take *-iter*: *fortiter*, *celeriter*. (*Forte* with short *e* is another word, "by chance".)

**Do not add *-iter* after *nt*.** *\*Sapientiter*, *\*potentiter*: after *nt* the ending is *-er*. *Sapienter*, *potenter*.

**Do not build *\*bonē* or *\*malē*.** The adverbs of *bonus* and *malus* are *bene* and *male*, with short *e*. The ablative *bonō*, *malō* are not adverbs.

**Do not build *\*magnē*.** "Greatly" is *magnopere* or *valdē*; "much" with verbs is *multum*.

**Do not confuse *facile* and *facilis*.** *Facilis* is the adjective, "easy"; its neuter *facile* doubles as the adverb, "easily". In *Iter facile est* it is the neuter adjective; in *facile vincunt* it is the adverb. Ask whether there is a neuter singular noun for it to agree with.

**Do not confuse *sōlum* and *sōlus*.** *Sōlus* agrees with a noun, "alone": *Sōlus vēnit* "he came alone". *Sōlum* is the adverb "only": *nōn sōlum … sed etiam* "not only … but also".

**Do not take *adeō* for the verb.** *Adeō* "I approach" (part 4) and *adeō* "to such a degree" are spelled alike. Wiktionary gives them separate origins: *ad* + *eō* "go", and *ad* + *eō* "to that point". Context decides: *adeō fortis* can only be "so brave".

**Do not put an adverb after *sum* where English uses an adjective.** *\*Iter celeriter est*: "the journey is quick" needs the adjective, *Iter celere est*. Only the few words like *palam* work after *sum* (ag1903 §321d).

**Do not copy Spanish *-mente*.** *\*Fortī mente* is real Latin, but it means "with a brave mind". The Romance adverb grew from this ablative later. Classical Latin uses *fortiter*.

## Connections

Spanish *-mente* and French *-ment* come from Latin *mente*, the ablative of *mēns* "mind" (Wiktionary). *Clārā mente* "with a clear mind" became *claramente* "clearly". That is why the adjective is feminine in both languages: *mēns* is feminine. Latin itself still used *clārē*.

## Exercises

1. Form adverbs from *dūrus*, *līber*, *pulcher*, *ācer*, *potēns* and *bonus*.
2. Translate *Mīles fortis fortiter pugnat*, and say what each of the two forms describes.
3. Say "the girls sing beautifully but rarely".
4. Say "this is very easy, and you will do it easily".
5. Correct *\*Dux sapientiter et celerē dīxit*.
6. Is *facile* adjective or adverb in *Opus facile est* and in *Opus facile cōnfēcit*?
7. Translate *Nōn sōlum puerī sed etiam puellae ibi erant*.
8. Translate *Sōlus adeō miser erat ut …* up to the dots, and say which word is an adverb.

## Worked answers

1. *Dūrē* (stem *dūr-* + *-ē*; Allen and Greenough note *dūriter* too, ag1903 §214c), *līberē* (the feminine *lībera* keeps the *e*), *pulchrē* (the feminine *pulchra* drops it), *ācriter* (stem *ācr-* + *-iter*), *potenter* (*nt*-stem + *-er*), *bene* (irregular).
2. "The brave soldier fights bravely." *Fortis* agrees with *mīles*: it describes the man. *Fortiter* describes the fighting and agrees with nothing.
3. *Puellae pulchrē sed rārō cantant.* *Pulchrē*: stem *pulchr-* + *-ē*. *Rārō*: the fixed ablative of *rārus*.
4. *Hoc valdē facile est, et facile faciēs.* The first *facile* is the neuter adjective agreeing with *hoc*; the second is the adverb with *faciēs* (future, part 4).
5. *Dux sapienter et celeriter dīxit.* After *nt*, only *-er*; *celer* is third declension, so *-iter*.
6. In *Opus facile est*, an adjective: it agrees with the neuter *opus* after *est*, "the work is easy". In *Opus facile cōnfēcit*, an adverb: "he finished the work easily". It could in theory still agree with *opus*, "he finished the easy work"; the word order and sense decide.
7. "Not only the boys but also the girls were there." *Nōn sōlum … sed etiam*: *sōlum* is the adverb "only".
8. "He alone was so wretched that …". *Sōlus* is the adjective agreeing with the subject; *adeō* is the adverb, modifying *miser*. (The *ut* clause that would follow is a result clause, part 6.)
