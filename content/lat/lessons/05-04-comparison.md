## The idea

English has three steps for a quality: "high", "higher", "highest". Latin has the same three, which grammars call **degrees**: the **positive** (the plain adjective, *altus*), the **comparative** (*altior* "higher") and the **superlative** (*altissimus* "highest") (ag1903 §123). Like English, Latin makes the last two with suffixes. Unlike English, it then declines them, because they are adjectives and must agree with their noun.

Allen and Greenough point out that the comparative and superlative are really new adjectives built on the old one, not extra endings of it (ag1903 §124 footnote). That is why each belongs to a declension of its own. The comparative is a third-declension consonant stem: *altior*, *altiōris*. The superlative is a *bonus* adjective: *altissimus, -a, -um*.

The comparative has a history you can see in its forms. Its suffix was once *-iōs-* (ag1903 §124 footnote). Allen and Greenough explain that the *s* became *r* in most forms, and the vowel varied by position: *altior* in the masculine and feminine nominative, *altiōr-* everywhere else, but *altius* in the neuter nominative and accusative singular, which kept the *s* (ag1903 §120b). This is rhotacism again, as in *corpus, corporis* and *honor, honōris* (part 3). The neuter *-ius* is also the comparative adverb: *altius* means "higher" (of a thing) and "more highly" (ag1903 §218).

The superlative suffix *-issimus* is, in Allen and Greenough's words, "a double form of uncertain origin" (ag1903 §124 footnote). Do not look for a neat story.

The irregular comparisons are a different matter. *Bonus, melior, optimus* "good, better, best" has three unrelated stems, as English "good, better" does. Allen and Greenough say these irregularities come from the use of different stems (ag1903 §129 note).

## How it is built

**Regular comparison** (ag1903 §124).

1. Take the stem of the positive by removing the genitive ending: *alt-ī* gives *alt-*; *fort-is* gives *fort-*; *fēlīc-is* gives *fēlīc-*.
2. Comparative: add *-ior* (masculine and feminine), *-ius* (neuter): *altior, altius*; *fortior, fortius*; *fēlīcior, fēlīcius*.
3. Decline it like a consonant stem on *-iōr-*: *altiōrem*, *altiōris*, *altiōrī*, *altiōre*; plural *altiōrēs*, neuter *altiōra*, *altiōrum*, *altiōribus*. The ablative is normally *-e* and the genitive plural *-um*: comparatives do not take the i-forms (ag1903 §117).
4. Superlative: add *-issimus, -a, -um*: *altissimus*, *fortissimus*, *fēlīcissimus*.

**Two special groups.**

1. Adjectives in *-er* add *-rimus* to the masculine nominative: *ācer, ācerrimus*; *miser, miserrimus*; *pulcher, pulcherrimus*. The comparative is regular: *ācrior*, *miserior* (ag1903 §125). *Vetus* has *veterrimus* (ag1903 §125a).
2. Six adjectives in *-lis* add *-limus* to the stem: *facilis, facillimus*; *difficilis, difficillimus*; *similis, simillimus*; *dissimilis, dissimillimus*; and two rarer ones, *gracilis* "slender" and *humilis* "low" (ag1903 §126). Other adjectives in *-lis* are regular: *ūtilissimus*, *nōbilissimus*.

Allen and Greenough explain *-rimus* and *-limus* as *-simus* assimilated to the consonant before it (ag1903 §124 footnote).

**Irregular comparison** (ag1903 §129). Learn these six:

1. *bonus, melior, optimus* "good, better, best";
2. *malus, peior, pessimus* "bad, worse, worst";
3. *magnus, maior, maximus* "great, greater, greatest";
4. *parvus, minor, minimus* "small, smaller, smallest";
5. *multus, plūs, plūrimus* "much, more, most"; plural *multī, plūrēs, plūrimī* "many, more, most";
6. *superus, superior, summus* (or *suprēmus*) "upper, higher, highest"; *īnferus, īnferior, īnfimus* "lower …, lowest" (ag1903 §130b).

*Plūs* is special. In the singular it is a neuter noun, "more", followed by a genitive: *plūs pecūniae* "more money" (ag1903 §120c). In the plural it is an adjective: *plūrēs mīlitēs* "more soldiers".

**Saying "than".** There are two ways (ag1903 §§406-407).

1. ***Quam* + the same case.** Put the two things compared in the same case: *Mārcus altior est quam Quīntus*. This works everywhere.
2. **The ablative alone.** If the first thing compared is nominative or accusative, you may put the second in the ablative and drop *quam*: *Mārcus altior est Quīntō*. This is the **ablative of comparison**: the ablative's old meaning "from" (part 3), here "taller, measured from Quintus".

Say "gold is more valuable than silver". Comparative of *cārus*: *cārior*, neuter *cārius* to agree with *aurum*. "Than silver": *quam argentum*, same case, or *argentō*, ablative. *Aurum cārius est quam argentum*, or *Aurum argentō cārius est*.

**How much more?** The difference goes in the ablative: *multō altior* "much higher", literally "higher by much"; *paulō altior* "a little higher" (ag1903 §414; lesson 8).

**Adverbs.** The comparative adverb is the neuter comparative: *fortius* "more bravely". The superlative adverb is the superlative + *-ē*: *fortissimē* "most bravely" (ag1903 §218). So *bene, melius, optimē*; *male, peius, pessimē*; *multum, plūs, plūrimum*; *magnopere, magis, maximē*.

## Where it applies

- **Comparing two things**: *altior quam collis*, *colle altior* (ag1903 §§406-407).
- **"Rather" or "too", with nothing compared.** A comparative alone often means "rather" or "too": *brevior* "rather short", *audācior* "too bold" (ag1903 §291a).
- **"Very", with nothing compared.** A superlative alone often means "very": *mōns altissimus* "a very high mountain" (ag1903 §291b). Context decides between "very" and "the most".
- **"Of a group"**: the superlative with a genitive, *fortissimī omnium* "the bravest of all" (Caesar above), or with *ex* and the ablative, *fortissimus ex mīlitibus*.
- **"As … as possible"**: *quam* + superlative: *quam plūrimī* "as many as possible", *quam celerrimē* "as fast as possible" (ag1903 §291c).
- **Two qualities of one thing**: both comparative, *longior quam lātior* "longer than it is wide" (Livy, quoted in ag1903 §292).
- **With *magis* and *maximē***: some adjectives, especially those in *-eus* and *-ius*, compare with *magis* "more" and *maximē* "most": *magis idōneus* "more suitable" (ag1903 §128). *Magis … quam* also means "rather than": *clārī magis quam honestī* "more famous than honourable" (Sallust, quoted in ag1903 §292a).
- **Position and order**: *summus*, *īnfimus*, *medius*, *extrēmus*, *ultimus* often mean a part of the thing: *summus mōns* "the top of the mountain", *in extrēmā viā* "at the end of the road" (ag1903 §293).
- **Fixed ablatives after a comparative**: *opīniōne celerius* "faster than expected", *spē* "than hoped", *aequō* "than is fair" (ag1903 §406a).
- **Comparatives without a positive**: *prior, prīmus* "earlier, first", *propior, proximus* "nearer, nearest", *ulterior, ultimus* "farther, farthest" (ag1903 §130a).
- **As nouns**: *maiōrēs* "ancestors" (lesson 2) is a comparative, literally "the greater (by birth)" (ag1903 §291 note 3).

## Watch out

**Do not use *magis* with an adjective that has its own comparative.** *\*Magis altus* for "higher" copies English "more high" or Spanish *más alto* and French *plus haut*. Latin says *altior*. *Magis* is for the few adjectives like *idōneus* (ag1903 §128) and for "rather than".

**Do not change the case after *quam*.** *\*Mārcus altior est quam Quīntum* puts the second term in the accusative for no reason. With *quam*, both terms are in the same case: *quam Quīntus*.

**Do not keep *quam* with the ablative.** *\*Altior est quam colle*: choose one construction. *Quam collis* or *colle*.

**Do not use the ablative when the first term is not nominative or accusative.** *\*Mihi gravius est illō* for "harder for me than for him": the first term is dative, so *quam* is required, *quam illī* (ag1903 §407a).

**Do not give comparatives the i-forms of lesson 1.** *\*Altiōria*, *\*altiōrium*: the comparative is a consonant stem, *altiōra*, *altiōrum*, and its ablative is normally *altiōre* (ag1903 §117). Wiktionary also lists *altiōrī* as an ablative, so that form is not wrong, only less usual.

**Do not decline *plūs* as an adjective in the singular.** *\*Plūs pecūnia* for "more money": in the singular *plūs* is a noun with a genitive, *plūs pecūniae* (ag1903 §120c).

**Do not read the neuter comparative as a masculine.** *Altius* is neuter, or an adverb; it is not "a higher man", which is *altior*. The *-us* here comes from the old *-ios*, not from *bonus*.

**Do not build *\*facilissimus* or *\*ācrissimus*.** The six *-lis* adjectives take *-limus*: *facillimus*. The *-er* adjectives take *-rimus*: *ācerrimus*.

**Do not take every superlative as "the most".** *Vir fortissimus* in Cicero is often "a very brave man", a compliment, not a ranking (ag1903 §291b note).

**Do not read *maior* and *minor* as "older" and "younger" without checking.** "Older brother" is fully *frāter maior nātū*, "greater by birth"; Allen and Greenough note that *nātū* is often left out (ag1903 §131c), so *frāter maior* can mean "older" or "bigger", and context decides. They explain *maiōrēs* "ancestors" as short for *maiōrēs nātū* (ag1903 §291 note 3). *Nātū* "by birth" is an ablative of respect (ag1903 §131c note), glossed here.

## Connections

French and Spanish kept only a few of these one-word forms: Latin *melior* gives French *meilleur* and Spanish *mejor*, *peior* gives *pire* and *peor*, *minor* gives *moindre* and *menor*, *maior* gives Spanish *mayor* (French *majeur* is a later borrowing) (Wiktionary). For everything else they replaced the suffix with *plus* and *más*, from Latin *plūs* and *magis*.

## Exercises

1. Give the comparative and superlative of *longus*, *trīstis*, *ācer*, *facilis* and *bonus*.
2. Decline *fortior* in the singular, masculine and neuter.
3. Say "Rome is larger than Carthage" in two ways (*Carthāgō, Carthāginis* f.).
4. Say "my brother fought more bravely than the others".
5. Translate *Nihil est dulcius lībertāte* and name the construction.
6. Say "as quickly as possible".
7. Correct *\*Haec via magis longa est quam illam*.
8. Translate *Ex summō colle hostēs vīdimus; plūrimī erant*.
9. Why does *Hoc mihi gravius est quam illī* need *quam*?

## Worked answers

1. *Longior, longissimus*; *trīstior, trīstissimus*; *ācrior, ācerrimus* (*-er* adjectives add *-rimus* to the nominative); *facilior, facillimus* (one of the six in *-lis*); *melior, optimus* (irregular).
2. Masculine: *fortior*, *fortiōrem*, *fortiōris*, *fortiōrī*, *fortiōre*. Neuter: *fortius*, *fortius*, *fortiōris*, *fortiōrī*, *fortiōre*. The neuter keeps the old *s* as *-ius* only in the nominative and accusative (ag1903 §120b).
3. *Rōma maior est quam Carthāgō*, with both in the nominative; or *Rōma Carthāgine maior est*, with the ablative of comparison. *Maior* is irregular: *magnus, maior, maximus*.
4. *Frāter meus fortius pugnāvit quam cēterī.* The adverb is the neuter comparative *fortius*. *Cēterī* is nominative like *frāter* (lesson 2).
5. "Nothing is sweeter than freedom." *Dulcius*, neuter comparative agreeing with *nihil*; *lībertāte*, ablative of comparison. After a negative like *nihil*, the ablative is the usual construction (ag1903 §407b).
6. *Quam celerrimē*: *quam* + the superlative adverb. *Celer* is an *-er* adjective, so *celerrimus*, adverb *celerrimē*.
7. *Haec via longior est quam illa* (or *illā*, without *quam*). *Longus* has its own comparative, so not *magis longa*; and after *quam* the second term takes the case of the first, nominative *illa*.
8. "From the top of the hill we saw the enemy; there were very many of them." *Summō colle*: the top of the hill (ag1903 §293). *Plūrimī*: superlative of *multī* used as "very many".
9. The first term, *mihi*, is a dative. The ablative of comparison can replace *quam* only when the first term is nominative or accusative (ag1903 §407a), so *quam illī*, with *illī* also dative.
