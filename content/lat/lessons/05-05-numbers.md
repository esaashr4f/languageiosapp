## The idea

Latin numbers come in families that answer different questions (ag1903 §132).

- **Cardinals** answer "how many?": *ūnus* "one", *duo* "two", *trēs* "three".
- **Ordinals** answer "which in order?": *prīmus* "first", *secundus* "second", *tertius* "third".
- **Distributives** answer "how many each?" or "how many at a time?": *singulī* "one each", *bīnī* "two each".
- **Numeral adverbs** answer "how many times?": *semel* "once", *bis* "twice", *ter* "three times".

You have met some already: *ūnus* (lesson 2), *prīmus*, *secundus*, *tertius*, *semel*, *ter*, and *trēs* in Caesar's *partēs trēs*.

Most cardinals do not decline at all. Only *ūnus*, *duo*, *trēs*, the hundreds from two hundred up, and the plural *mīlia* "thousands" change their endings (ag1903 §134). From four to a hundred a number is a fixed word: *quattuor mīlitēs*, *quattuor mīlitum*, *cum quattuor mīlitibus*. The noun carries the case.

The small numbers decline because they are old adjectives. *Duo* even keeps a trace of a lost grammatical number. Allen and Greenough explain that its ending *-o* is a remnant of the **dual**, a set of forms for exactly two things that Latin lost but related languages kept (ag1903 §134 footnote). *Ambō* "both" has the same ending.

The ordinals are adjectives like *bonus*. Allen and Greenough explain most of them as built with the suffixes of the superlative: *decimus* "tenth" is formed like *īnfimus* and can be seen as "the last of a series of ten"; *prīmus* is a superlative of a stem akin to *prō* "before" (ag1903 §132 footnote). *Secundus* is different: it is a participle of *sequor*, "the following one" (ag1903 §132 footnote). That is why it also means "favourable", as in *ventus secundus*, a following wind.

## How it is built

**One, two, three.**

1. *Ūnus, ūna, ūnum* declines like the ūnus group of lesson 2: genitive *ūnīus*, dative *ūnī*.
2. *Duo* "two": masculine *duo*, feminine *duae*, neuter *duo*. Accusative *duōs, duās, duo*; genitive *duōrum, duārum*; dative and ablative *duōbus, duābus*.
3. *Trēs* "three": masculine and feminine *trēs*, neuter *tria*, genitive *trium*, dative and ablative *tribus*. It is an ordinary third-declension i-stem plural.

**Four to a hundred.** These do not change: *quattuor*, *quīnque*, *sex*, *septem*, *octō*, *novem*, *decem*; *ūndecim* "eleven" (*ūnus* + *decem*), *duodecim* "twelve"; *vīgintī* "twenty"; *centum* "a hundred". Allen and Greenough derive the *-gintā* of thirty and above from a word for ten (ag1903 §132 footnote).

**Compound numbers.**

1. Below a hundred, put the smaller number first with *et*, or the larger first without it: *ūnus et vīgintī* or *vīgintī ūnus* "twenty-one" (ag1903 §135a).
2. Eighteen and nineteen, and the same in each later ten, are usually said by subtraction: *duodēvīgintī* "two from twenty", *ūndēvīgintī* "one from twenty" (ag1903 §133 note 2).
3. Above a hundred, the largest comes first, as in English: *centum vīgintī* "a hundred and twenty".

**The hundreds and the thousands.**

1. Two hundred to nine hundred are plural adjectives like *bonī*: *ducentī, -ae, -a*, *trecentī*, *quīngentī* (ag1903 §134c).
2. *Mīlle* "a thousand" is an indeclinable adjective in the singular: *mīlle mīlitēs*, *cum mīlle mīlitibus* (ag1903 §134d).
3. The plural *mīlia* is a neuter noun, declined like *maria*: *mīlia*, *mīlium*, *mīlibus*. As a noun it takes the genitive of what is counted: *duo mīlia mīlitum* "two thousand soldiers", literally "two thousands of soldiers" (ag1903 §135c).

**Ordinals.** *Prīmus*, *secundus* (or *alter*), *tertius*, *quārtus*, *quīntus*, *sextus*, *septimus*, *octāvus*, *nōnus*, *decimus*; *vīcēsimus* "twentieth", *centēsimus* "hundredth". All decline like *bonus* and agree with their noun.

Say "with three thousand soldiers".

1. "Thousands" is plural: *mīlia*. Three agrees with it: neuter *tria*.
2. "With" a group of people takes *cum* + ablative: *cum tribus mīlibus*.
3. What is counted goes in the genitive: *mīlitum*.
4. *Cum tribus mīlibus mīlitum* (ag1903 §135c).

**Roman numerals.** The signs are I, V, X, L, C, D, M; Allen and Greenough list both IIII and IV, VIIII and IX (ag1903 §133). Read the sign, then say the word in the case the sentence needs: *XX nāvēs* is *vīgintī nāvēs*.

## Where it applies

- **Counting with a noun**: the cardinal agrees if it can (*ūnus*, *duo*, *trēs*, the hundreds), otherwise stays fixed (ag1903 §134).
- **Thousands**: *mīlle* as a fixed adjective, *mīlia* as a noun with a genitive (ag1903 §§134d, 135c). Allen and Greenough note that *mīlle* is sometimes a noun too: *mīlle hominum* (ag1903 §134d note).
- **Distances**: *mīlia passuum* "miles", in the accusative of extent: *tria mīlia passuum* "three miles" (ag1903 §135c).
- **Order and dates**: ordinals for hours, days, years, legions: *tertiā hōrā*, *legiō decima*.
- **Fractions**: an ordinal agreeing with *pars*: *tertia pars* "a third" (ag1903 §135e).
- **"Each"**: distributives, *bīna dōna* "two presents each", *singula singulīs* "one apiece" (ag1903 §137a).
- **Plural-only nouns**: distributives instead of cardinals, *bīna castra* "two camps", *trīna castra* "three camps" (ag1903 §137b).
- **Multiplication**: *bis bīna* "twice two" (ag1903 §137c).
- **"How many times"**: *semel*, *bis*, *ter*, *quater*, then *-iēns*: *quīnquiēns* "five times", *deciēns* "ten times" (ag1903 §138).
- **"Same" and "only"**: *ūnus* can mean "one and the same" or "alone", and its plural is used that way too (ag1903 §134a).
- **"Both"**: *ambō, ambae, ambō*, declined like *duo* (ag1903 §134b note). *Uterque* (lesson 2) treats the two separately; *ambō* takes them together.
- **"Second"**: *secundus*, or *alter* when there are only two (ag1903 §133).

## Watch out

**Do not decline the fixed numbers.** *\*Quattuōrum*, *\*cum septibus*: from *quattuor* to *centum* the number never changes. *Cum septem mīlitibus*.

**Do not make *mīlle* plural by adding *-s*.** *\*Duo mīlle*, *\*duo mīllēs*: the plural is *mīlia*, a noun. *Duo mīlia*.

**Do not make *mīlia* agree with the noun.** *\*Tria mīlia mīlitēs*: *mīlia* is a noun, so what is counted goes in the genitive, *tria mīlia mīlitum*. With the singular *mīlle*, it agrees: *mīlle mīlitēs*.

**Do not use a masculine number with *mīlia*.** *\*Trēs mīlia*, *\*duōs mīlia*: *mīlia* is neuter, so *tria mīlia*, *duo mīlia*.

**Do not use *duo* with a plural-only noun.** *\*Duo castra* means "two forts", not "two camps" (ag1903 §137b footnote). Use *bīna castra*.

**Do not read *duodēvīgintī* as "twelve and twenty".** *Duo-dē-vīgintī* is "two from twenty", eighteen.

**Do not give *secundus* only its number sense.** In *secunda fortūna* "good fortune" and *ventus secundus* "a favourable wind", it means "following, favourable", from *sequor*.

**Do not use *prīmus* for "at first" or "firstly".** *Prīmus vēnit* means "he was the first to come" (ag1903 §290). "First" as a step in a sequence is the adverb *prīmum*; "at first", as opposed to later, is *prīmō*.

**Do not say *\*ūnus diēs* for "one day, some day".** *Ūnus diēs* is "a single day". "One day" in a story is *quōdam diē* (lesson 10).

## Connections

French *deux*, *trois* and Spanish *dos*, *tres* go back to the Latin accusatives *duōs* and *trēs* (Wiktionary). Spanish *mil* and French *mille* continue *mīlle*, and the English "mile" is the Roman *mīlia passuum*.

## Exercises

1. Say "two legions" in the nominative, accusative and ablative.
2. Say "with three ships" and "of three ships".
3. Translate *Quīnque mīlia hostium ad flūmen vēnērunt*.
4. Say "four thousand citizens" as subject.
5. Read *XVIII annōs nātus erat* aloud as words (*annōs nātus* "years old", literally "born … years").
6. Correct *\*Duo mīlle mīlitēs et tria castra*.
7. Translate *Decimā legiōne fortior nūlla erat*, and explain *decimā* and *fortior*.
8. Say "the second day" and "the second of the two consuls".

## Worked answers

1. *Legiō* is feminine, so *duae legiōnēs*, *duās legiōnēs*, *duābus legiōnibus*.
2. *Cum tribus nāvibus*, *trium nāvium*. *Trēs* is a third-declension plural: ablative *tribus*, genitive *trium*.
3. "Five thousand of the enemy came to the river." *Quīnque* is fixed; *mīlia* is the subject, with the genitive *hostium*. The verb is plural to agree with *mīlia*.
4. *Quattuor mīlia cīvium*. *Mīlia* is a noun, so the citizens go in the genitive, *cīvium*.
5. *Duodēvīgintī annōs nātus erat*, "he was eighteen years old". XVIII is eighteen, said as "two from twenty". *Annōs* is accusative of extent (part 3).
6. *Duo mīlia mīlitum et bīna castra*: the plural of *mīlle* is the noun *mīlia*, with the genitive; and a plural-only noun takes the distributive (ag1903 §137b).
7. "No legion was braver than the tenth." *Decimā legiōne*: the ordinal agrees with *legiōne*, and the pair is an ablative of comparison, usual after a negative (lesson 4). *Fortior*: comparative agreeing with *nūlla* (*legiō* understood), feminine nominative singular.
8. *Secundus diēs* (or *alter diēs*). Of two consuls: *alter cōnsul* (lesson 2).
