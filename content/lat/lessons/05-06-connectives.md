## The idea

You met the commonest linking words in part 2, lesson 8, mainly for their place in the sentence. This lesson is about what each one means, and why Latin has several where English has one.

A **conjunction** is a word that joins words, phrases or sentences: "and", "or", "but", "for". This lesson covers **coordinating** conjunctions, which join things of equal rank: two nouns, two adjectives, two main clauses. Conjunctions that attach a dependent clause, such as "if" and "because", are part 6 (ag1903 §223).

Allen and Greenough make two points that explain the whole system. First, Latin conjunctions are "more numerous and more accurately distinguished" than English ones (ag1903 §224). Latin has three common words for "and", three for "or", and several each for "but", "for" and "therefore", and each has its own shade. Second, conjunctions are related to adverbs. Many are frozen cases or phrases (ag1903 §222): *vērō* "but, indeed" is an old ablative of *vērus* "true"; *nihilōminus* "nevertheless" is *nihilō minus* "less by nothing" (ag1903 §216); *quārē* "for which reason" is *quā rē* "by which thing" (Wiktionary). That is why the line between "linking adverb" (*tamen*, *deinde*) and "conjunction" (*sed*) is blurred.

One rule holds for all coordinating conjunctions: they join like with like. What stands after them is normally in the same case or mood as what stands before (ag1903 §323). *Senātuī et populō*: two datives. *Venit et videt*: two present verbs.

## How it is built

**"And"** (ag1903 §324a-b).

1. *Et* joins two things as separate items: *puerī et puellae*.
2. *-que* joins them more closely, as one whole. Allen and Greenough's example is *ferrō ignīque* "with fire and sword", the combined means of destruction (ag1903 §324a). *Senātus populusque Rōmānus* is the state as one body.
3. *Atque*, before consonants often *ac*, adds with emphasis: "and also", "and indeed". Allen and Greenough warn that the shade depends on the speaker's feeling and is often untranslatable (ag1903 §324b).
4. "Both … and": *et … et*. "And not": *neque* or *nec* (part 2). "Neither … nor": *neque … neque*.

**"Or"** (ag1903 §324e).

1. *Aut* excludes: one or the other, not both. *Aut bibat aut abeat*, "let him drink or leave".
2. *Vel* and the enclitic *-ve* offer a choice: either will do. Allen and Greenough explain *vel* as an old imperative of *volō* "want", so roughly "take your pick" (ag1903 §324e); Wiktionary instead derives it from an old form meaning "you wish".
3. *Sīve* (or *seu*) is "or if", "whether … or": *sīve … sīve*. Wiktionary derives it from *sī* "if" + *-ve*. It is used especially with two names for the same thing (ag1903 §324f).

**"But"** (ag1903 §324d).

1. *Sed* "but" sets something against what precedes, especially after a negative: "not this, but that". *Vērum* and *vērō* do the same with more weight.
2. *At* brings in a new point or an objection with emphasis.
3. *Autem* is the weakest. Allen and Greenough say it often marks only a transition, "now", "moreover", with hardly any contrast (ag1903 §324d).
4. *Tamen* "nevertheless" contrasts with what was expected; *attamen* and *sed tamen* strengthen it; *nihilōminus* is "none the less".

**"For"** (ag1903 §324h).

1. *Nam* (and *namque*) gives a real reason for what was just said.
2. *Enim* adds a less important explanation in passing.
3. *Etenim* gives a reason that needs no proof: "for, of course".

**"Therefore"** (ag1903 §324i).

1. *Ergō* draws a conclusion that has been proved, though often with weaker force.
2. *Igitur* moves an argument on to its next stage: "so, then".
3. *Itaque* "and so" draws a consequence from the facts.
4. *Proinde* "accordingly", often with a command; *idcircō* "for this reason", pointing to a reason given in a clause.

**Where they stand.** *Autem*, *enim* and *vērō* never come first; they stand second. *Igitur* usually does too, and *tamen* often (ag1903 §324j). *Nam*, *sed*, *at*, *itaque* come first. *-que* and *-ve* hang on the second of the two things joined.

Say "the enemy could not withstand the attack, and turned and fled". Two main clauses: *hostēs impetum ferre nōn potuērunt* and *terga vertērunt* "they turned their backs". The second follows from the first, so *ac* "and so": *Hostēs impetum ferre nōn potuērunt ac terga vertērunt*, which is how Allen and Greenough quote Caesar (Gallic War 4.35; ag1903 §324d note, where they render *ac* as "but").

## Where it applies

- **Joining like with like**: two nouns in the same case, two verbs in the same mood (ag1903 §323): *senātuī et populō*, *venit et videt*.
- **Two adjectives with one noun** are joined by a conjunction, unlike English: *multae et gravēs causae* "many serious reasons" (ag1903 §323d; lesson 2).
- **Lists**: Latin puts the conjunction between every item, *aut … aut … aut*, or between none (ag1903 §323c). *-que* may stand on the last item alone: *vōce vultū mōtūque* "with voice, face and gesture" (Cicero, quoted in ag1903 §323c).
- **No conjunction at all**, called **asyndeton** (Greek "unbound"): items or clauses set side by side for speed or force, as in *Vēnī, vīdī, vīcī* (ag1903 §323b).
- **Pairs**: *et … et* "both … and", *aut … aut*, *vel … vel* "either … or", *sīve … sīve* "whether … or", *neque … neque* "neither … nor", *modo … modo*, *nunc … nunc* "now … now", *simul … simul* "at once … and", *partim … partim* "partly … partly" (ag1903 §§323e-f, 324).
- **"And" where English says "but"**: after a negative, *et*, *-que* and *atque* may continue the thought where English would use "but" (ag1903 §324d note). Caesar's *nōn potuērunt ac terga vertērunt* above.
- ***Atque* after words of likeness**: *aequē ac tū* "as much as you", *simul atque* "as soon as" (ag1903 §324c).
- ***Vel* as "even"**: *vel minimus* "the very least" (ag1903 §324g).
- **Two conjunctions together** for emphasis: *at vērō* "but surely", *itaque ergō*, *namque*, *etenim* (ag1903 §324k).
- **Correcting**: *immō* "no, rather", "on the contrary", corrects what was said: *Immō vērō* "no, indeed".
- **Opening a fresh step**: *cēterum* "but, for the rest", *quārē* and *quam ob rem* "for which reason, therefore", which begin a sentence and point back to the one before.

## Watch out

**Do not use *aut* when either choice is acceptable.** "You may take the sword or the spear" offers a free choice: *vel*. *Aut* means one excludes the other (ag1903 §324e). Spanish *o* and French *ou* do not make the distinction.

**Do not put *autem*, *enim* or *vērō* first.** *\*Enim pluit*: these words stand second, *Pluit enim* (ag1903 §324j). Put *nam* first instead if you want a word at the front: *Nam pluit*.

**Do not attach *-que* to the first item.** *\*Puerīque puellae*: *-que* follows the second, *puerī puellaeque*. With a preposition, it goes on the noun, not the preposition: *in agrīs silvīsque*.

**Do not write *et nōn* for "and not".** *Neque* or *nec* (part 2, lesson 6). "And no one", "and nothing", "and never" are likewise *nec quisquam*, *nec quicquam*, *nec umquam* (lesson 10).

**Do not use *sed* for a mere "now" or "however".** *Sed* is a real contrast. For a mild turn in a story, Latin uses *autem*.

**Do not join two adjectives without a conjunction by English habit.** *\*Multae gravēs causae* sounds abrupt in prose: *multae et gravēs causae* (ag1903 §323d).

**Do not change case across *et*.** *\*Mārcō et Quīntum pecūniam dedit*: the two receivers must both be dative, *Mārcō et Quīntō*.

**Do not confuse *quārē* "for which reason, therefore" with the question "why?".** Both exist, and the question use comes in part 6. At the start of a statement after a reason, *quārē* means "therefore".

## Connections

Spanish and French kept *et* (*y* comes from it in Spanish; French *et*), *aut* (*o*, *ou*) and *nec* (*ni*), but lost *-que* and *atque* (Wiktionary). Japanese also has more than one "and" between nouns: *to* names the complete set, *ya* gives examples from a longer list.

## Exercises

1. Join *puerī* and *puellae* in three ways, and say how they differ.
2. Say "either Marcus or Quintus will come" (only one can) and "take the book or the letter" (either will do).
3. Translate *Nōn urbem sed castra petimus*.
4. Put *enim* into *Mīlitēs fessī sunt* so that it means "for the soldiers are tired".
5. Say "the general was brave; nevertheless he was afraid".
6. Correct *\*Autem dux vēnit* and *\*Pecūniam Mārcō et Quīntum dedit*.
7. Translate *Hostēs fugiunt; itaque castra capimus*.
8. Explain the difference between *nam* and *enim* in *Domī manē; nam pluit* and *Domī manē; pluit enim*.
9. What does the lack of conjunctions add in *Vēnī, vīdī, vīcī*?

## Worked answers

1. *Puerī et puellae*: two groups named separately. *Puerī puellaeque*: one combined group, "boys and girls alike". *Puerī atque puellae*: "the boys, and the girls too", with some weight on the second. Allen and Greenough stress that these shades are often untranslatable (ag1903 §324a-b).
2. *Aut Mārcus aut Quīntus veniet*: *aut* excludes. *Cape vel librum vel epistulam*: *vel*, a free choice (ag1903 §324e). The verb *veniet* is singular, because only one comes.
3. "We are making not for the city but for the camp." *Sed* after a negative (ag1903 §324d).
4. *Mīlitēs enim fessī sunt.* *Enim* stands second (ag1903 §324j).
5. *Dux fortis erat; tamen timēbat.* Or *nihilōminus timēbat*. The imperfect for a state of mind (part 4, lesson 7).
6. *Dux autem vēnit*: *autem* cannot come first. *Pecūniam Mārcō et Quīntō dedit*: *et* joins like with like, so both are dative (ag1903 §323).
7. "The enemy are fleeing; and so we are taking the camp." *Itaque* first, drawing the consequence (ag1903 §324i).
8. Both give a reason and both are correct. *Nam* stands first and presents the reason formally; *enim* stands second and adds it more lightly, in passing (ag1903 §324h).
9. Speed and finality: three actions, each complete, with nothing between them. The perfect *vēnī, vīdī, vīcī* also makes each a single finished event (part 4, lesson 7). In Latin the three words also share their sound, *v-* and *-ī*.
