## The idea

You met the perfect participle in part 4: *captus* "taken", *amātus* "loved". A participle is an adjective made from a verb. Latin has two more.

- The **present participle** *amāns* "loving" describes someone in the middle of an action. It is active.
- The **future participle** *amātūrus* "about to love, going to love" describes someone whose action is still to come. It is also active.

So a regular verb has three participles: *amāns* (present, active), *amātus* (perfect, passive), *amātūrus* (future, active). There is no present passive participle ("being loved") and no perfect active one ("having loved") (ag1903 §158c note). Deponents fill one gap: their perfect participle is active, *secūtus* "having followed" (part 4, lesson 13).

Allen and Greenough sum up what a participle is: it has "all the functions of an adjective with some of the functions of a verb" (ag1903 §488 note). As an adjective it agrees with a noun in gender, number and case. As a verb it has tense and can take an object: *puer epistulam legēns* "the boy reading a letter".

Its tense is relative. Allen and Greenough explain that a participle's time is measured from the main verb of its clause, not from now (ag1903 §489). The present participle means "at the same time as the main verb"; the perfect "before it"; the future "after it". So *legēns* can be "reading" in a past story: *puerum legentem vīdī* "I saw the boy reading".

Latin uses participles far more than English does. Where English says "when he saw", "because she was afraid", "although they were fighting", Latin often says one word: "seeing", "fearing", "fighting" (ag1903 §496). Reading Latin means turning these back into clauses.

## How it is built

**The present participle** is built on the present stem with *-ns*, genitive *-ntis*. It declines like *ingēns* (lesson 1; ag1903 §117b).

1. First conjugation: *amā-* + *-ns*: *amāns, amantis*.
2. Second: *monē-* + *-ns*: *monēns, monentis*.
3. Third: stem + *-ēns*: *regēns, regentis*; *dūcēns*.
4. Third in *-iō* and fourth: *-iēns*: *capiēns, capientis*; *audiēns, audientis*; *veniēns*.
5. *Eō* "go": *iēns*, genitive *euntis* (ag1903 §203).
6. Deponents have it too, active in meaning: *sequēns* "following", *loquēns* "speaking".

The vowel is long before *ns* and short before *nt*: *amāns*, *amantis* (Wiktionary). *Sum* has no present participle (Wiktionary gives none).

The ablative singular has two forms. Allen and Greenough explain the choice: a participle used as a participle, or as a noun, has *-e*; one used as an adjective has *-ī* (ag1903 §121a). *Ab amante* "by a lover"; *ab amantī muliere* "by a loving woman".

**The future participle** is built on the supine stem with *-ūrus, -a, -um*, like *bonus* (ag1903 §158b).

1. Take the fourth principal part: *amātum*, *rēctum*, *captum*, *audītum*.
2. Remove *-um* and add *-ūrus*: *amātūrus*, *rēctūrus*, *captūrus*, *audītūrus*.
3. Deponents use the participle stem: *sequor, secūtus* gives *secūtūrus*; *cōnfiteor, cōnfessus* gives *cōnfessūrus*.
4. A few are irregular, and the dictionary gives them: *sum* has *futūrus* "about to be"; *eō* has *itūrus*; *morior* has *moritūrus*.

**With *sum***, the future participle says "about to", "going to", "intending to": *Urbs est cāsūra* "the city is about to fall"; *mānsūrus eram* "I was going to stay" (ag1903 §158b note). Allen and Greenough call this the active periphrastic conjugation (ag1903 §498a). *Periphrastic* means "said with a phrase": a tense made of two words.

**Turning a participle into a clause.** Say "the soldiers, seeing the enemy, ran away".

1. The main verb is "ran away": *fūgērunt*. Subject *mīlitēs*.
2. "Seeing" happens at the same time: present participle of *videō*, *vidēns*.
3. It describes *mīlitēs*: masculine nominative plural, *videntēs*.
4. Its object "the enemy": *hostēs*. *Mīlitēs hostēs videntēs fūgērunt.*

To read it, go the other way and choose the English link the context suggests: "when they saw the enemy", or "because they saw the enemy".

## Where it applies

Allen and Greenough list the ways a participle can stand for an English clause (ag1903 §496). Each is a reading, not a different form; context decides.

- **Time**: *Hostēs videntēs fūgērunt* "when they saw the enemy, they fled".
- **Cause**: *impetum timentēs recessērunt* "because they feared the attack".
- **Concession**: *dōna ferentīs* "even though they bring gifts".
- **Condition**: "if …". Allen and Greenough's example uses a perfect participle: *damnātum poenam sequī oportēbat* "if he was condemned, the punishment had to follow" (Caesar, Gallic War 1.4, quoted in ag1903 §496).
- **Description or manner**: *puer legēns* "the boy, reading".
- **Plain adjective**: *aqua currēns* "running water". Many participles have become adjectives: *sapiēns* "wise", *potēns* "powerful", *praesēns* "present", *absēns* "absent", *frequēns* "crowded, frequent", *recēns* "fresh" (ag1903 §494a). Such adjectives can be compared: *sapientior*, *sapientissimus*.
- **Noun**: *amantēs* "lovers", *cantantibus* "for singers", *moritūrī* "those about to die" (ag1903 §494a).
- **An action begun in the past and still going on**: *quaerentī mihi iam diū* "to me, who had long been searching" (Cicero, quoted in ag1903 §490).
- **An attempted action**: *agrum dīvidentī* "trying to divide the land" (ag1903 §490).
- **The future participle with *sum***: "about to", "going to", "intending to" in every tense: *captūrus est, erat, erit* (ag1903 §498a).
- **The future participle alone**, in poets and later prose, for purpose or intention: *sī peritūrus abīs* "if you are going away to die" (Vergil, Aeneid 2.675; ag1903 §499). In Cicero and Caesar it usually needs *sum*; *futūrus* and *ventūrus* are the exceptions (ag1903 §498).
- **Deponents' perfect participles in a present sense**: *ratus* "thinking", *veritus* "fearing", *ausus* "daring" (ag1903 §491).
- **The ablative absolute**, a participle with a noun in the ablative, standing apart from the sentence: part 6.

## Watch out

**Do not look for a present passive participle.** *\*Amātēns* or *\*amandus* for "being loved" does not exist in this sense. Latin uses a clause: *dum amātur* "while he is being loved" (ag1903 §492).

**Do not look for a perfect active participle.** "Having seen the city, he left" cannot be *\*vīsēns* or *\*vīsus* (which means "having been seen"). Latin says "the city having been seen", an ablative absolute (part 6), or uses a deponent, whose perfect participle is active: *hostēs secūtus* "having followed the enemy" (ag1903 §493).

**Do not read a present participle as "past" or "future".** In *Puellam cantantem audīvimus*, she sang at the time we heard, in the past. "Present" means "at the same time as the main verb" (ag1903 §489).

**Do not use the participle for an English "-ing" noun.** "Reading is pleasant" is not *\*Legēns iūcundum est*. A participle describes a person or thing. "Reading" as an activity is the infinitive *legere* (part 4) or the gerund (part 7).

**Do not forget the participle agrees.** *\*Hostēs venientem vīdērunt* for "they saw the enemy coming": *venientēs*, accusative plural, to match *hostēs*.

**Do not use the future participle alone as a verb.** *\*Rōmam itūrus* for "I shall go to Rome" lacks the verb. Either *Rōmam ībō* or *Rōmam itūrus sum*.

**Do not build the future participle on the present stem.** *\*Amāntūrus*, *\*capiētūrus*: it comes from the supine stem, *amātūrus*, *captūrus*.

**Do not keep the long vowel before *nt*.** *\*Amāntem*: the vowel is long only before *ns*: *amāns*, *amantem*.

**Do not translate every participle with "-ing".** *Dōna ferentīs* is best "even when they bring gifts"; *impetum timentēs* is "because they feared the attack". Choose the English link the sense needs.

## Connections

Spanish and French kept the present participle mostly as adjectives and nouns: *presente*, *présent*; *estudiante*, *étudiant* "student" (one who is studying); *amante*, *amant* "lover" (Wiktionary). English took many of the same words: "present", "absent", "agent". The future participle survives in *futūrus*, the source of "future", *futur*, *futuro*.

## Exercises

1. Form the present and future participles of *vocō*, *videō*, *dūcō*, *veniō* and *loquor*.
2. Decline *audiēns* in the singular.
3. Translate *Puerōs in agrīs currentēs vīdī*.
4. Say "the citizens, fearing war, demanded peace" with a participle.
5. Say "we were going to come to Rome" with a future participle.
6. Translate *Hostēs fortiter pugnantēs tamen victī sunt*, and say which English link fits the participle.
7. Correct *\*Mīlitēs urbem vīsēns discessērunt* ("having seen the city, the soldiers left") as far as you can with what you know.
8. Translate *Ā mātre absente nihil cognōvit* and explain *absente*.

## Worked answers

1. *Vocāns, vocātūrus*; *vidēns, vīsūrus*; *dūcēns, ductūrus*; *veniēns, ventūrus*; *loquēns, locūtūrus*. The present is built on the present stem, the future on the supine (or deponent participle) stem.
2. *Audiēns*, *audientem*, *audientis*, *audientī*, *audiente* or *audientī*. Long *ē* before *ns*, short before *nt*.
3. "I saw the boys running in the fields." *Currentēs* agrees with *puerōs*, accusative plural, and happens at the time of *vīdī*.
4. *Cīvēs bellum timentēs pācem poposcērunt.* *Timentēs* agrees with *cīvēs*, nominative plural; *poposcērunt* is the perfect of *poscō*.
5. *Rōmam ventūrī erāmus.* Future participle of *veniō*, masculine nominative plural, with the imperfect of *sum* (ag1903 §158b note).
6. "The enemy, although they fought bravely, were nevertheless defeated." *Tamen* shows the participle is concessive: "although" (ag1903 §496).
7. Latin has no perfect active participle (ag1903 §493). With what you know, use a clause or a different verb: *Mīlitēs urbem vīdērunt et discessērunt* "the soldiers saw the city and left". Part 6 gives *urbe vīsā* "the city having been seen".
8. "He learned nothing from his mother while she was absent", or "in his mother's absence". *Absente* is ablative of *absēns*, agreeing with *mātre*; the *-e* ending is the participle form (ag1903 §121a).
