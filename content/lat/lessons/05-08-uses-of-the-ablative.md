## The idea

Part 3 (lesson 4) introduced the ablative as three old cases merged into one: a "from" case, a "with" case and an "in" case (ag1903 §398). You have since used it for the agent (part 4), for "than" (lesson 4 of this part) and for time and place (part 3, lesson 13). This lesson puts the uses side by side and gives you a way to tell which one you are looking at.

Begin with a warning from Allen and Greenough themselves. The grammar's labels are ours, not the Romans'. Of means, manner and accompaniment they write that "no sharp line can be drawn between them, and indeed the Romans themselves can hardly have thought of any distinction" (ag1903 §408). Of the ablative as a whole: "As the Romans had no such categories as we make, it is impossible to classify all uses of the Ablative" (ag1903 §418a note). In *vincit vīribus* "he wins by strength" or "he wins in strength", you cannot tell means from respect, and you do not need to.

So the labels are a tool for translating, not a test of right and wrong. What matters is that you can ask the right question of an ablative and give the English that fits. Each use answers a different English question: "with what?", "how?", "why?", "from what?", "in what respect?", "what kind of?", "than what?", "by how much?".

The three sources still explain the groups. The "from" ablative gives separation, source, cause, the agent and comparison: comparison measures one thing from another. The "with" ablative, the old instrumental, gives means, manner, accompaniment, description, respect and price: Allen and Greenough derive the ablative of quality from accompaniment and manner (ag1903 §415 footnote), and treat respect as originally instrumental too (ag1903 §418a note). The "in" ablative gives place and time.

## How it is built

When you meet an ablative, ask these questions in order. Stop at the first that fits.

1. **Is there a preposition?** Then the preposition decides: *ē silvā* "out of the forest", *dē mūrō* "down from the wall", *sine metū* "without fear". With *cum*, a person means company (*cum amīcīs*), an abstract noun means manner (*cum cūrā*).
2. **Is there a comparative nearby?** An ablative of a person or thing is "than": *Mārcus Quīntō altior* (ablative of comparison, ag1903 §406). An ablative of amount is "by how much": *multō altior* "much taller", *paulō post* "a little later", *tribus annīs ante* "three years before" (ablative of degree of difference, ag1903 §414).
3. **Is the verb or adjective one of the fixed groups?** Several take the ablative where English has a direct object or "of":
   - *ūtor* "use", *fruor* "enjoy", *fungor* "perform", *potior* "take possession of", *vēscor* "feed on" (ag1903 §410). Allen and Greenough explain this as an old ablative of means with verbs that were really middle: *ūtor gladiō* was "I serve myself by means of a sword" (ag1903 §410 note).
   - verbs of lacking and freeing: *careō*, *egeō* "need, lack", *līberō*, *prīvō* "deprive" (ag1903 §401);
   - *dignus* "worthy of", *indignus* "unworthy of" (ag1903 §418b); *frētus* "relying on", *contentus* "content with" (ag1903 §431a);
   - *opus est* "there is need of": *Pecūniā opus est* "money is needed" (ag1903 §411);
   - verbs of filling, and *plēnus* "full of" (ag1903 §409a).
4. **Does it say what something costs?** *Magnō emit* "he bought it at a high price"; *vīgintī dēnāriīs* "for twenty denarii" (*dēnārius* is a silver coin) (ablative of price, ag1903 §416).
5. **Is it a noun with an adjective, describing a person or thing?** Then it is the ablative of description, also called quality: *vir magnā virtūte* "a man of great courage" (ag1903 §415). It works like an adjective: it attaches to a noun, not to the verb.
6. **Does it say in what respect something is true?** Typically with adjectives or verbs of excelling and differing: *virtūte praecēdunt* "they excel in courage"; *nōn rē sed nōmine* "not in fact but in name" (ablative of respect, or specification, ag1903 §418).
7. **Does it give a reason, often a feeling?** *Gaudiō lacrimat* "he weeps for joy" (*lacrimō* "weep", from *lacrima*; ablative of cause, ag1903 §404). Allen and Greenough note that the motive inside the person is usually an ablative, the outside cause often *ob* or *propter* with the accusative (ag1903 §404b).
8. **Does it say how something was done, with an adjective?** *Magnā cūrā* or *magnā cum cūrā* "with great care". Without an adjective, manner needs *cum*: *cum cūrā*. Certain nouns work alone as adverbs: *silentiō* "in silence", *iūre* "rightly" (ablative of manner, ag1903 §412).
9. **Otherwise it is the thing used**: *gladiō pugnat*, *saxīs oppugnant* (ablative of means, ag1903 §409).

Then check the result against sense. If two labels both give good English, both are right (ag1903 §418a note).

## Where it applies

**The "from" ablative**

- **Separation**, with verbs of removing, freeing, lacking: *metū līberāre* "to free from fear"; *egeō cōnsiliō* "I need advice" (ag1903 §401). With real motion, a preposition: *ab urbe discēdere* (ag1903 §402).
- **Source and origin**, usually with a preposition: *ex aurō* "made of gold". After *nātus* "born" the plain ablative: *Iove nātus* "son of Jupiter" (Cicero, quoted in ag1903 §403a).
- **Cause**: feelings and motives, *gaudiō*, *metū*, *īrā*; also *causā* "for the sake of" with a genitive in front: *amīcitiae causā* (ag1903 §404c).
- **Agent**: *ā Caesare* with a passive verb (part 4).
- **Comparison**: *vītā cārior* (ag1903 §406). Also fixed phrases, *opīniōne celerius* "faster than expected" (ag1903 §406a).

**The "with" ablative**

- **Means**: *gladiō*, *vī*; with *ūtor*, *fruor*, *fungor*, *potior*, *vēscor* (ag1903 §§409-410); with *compleō*, *plēnus* (ag1903 §409a).
- **Accompaniment**, with *cum* for people: *cum amīcīs*. In military phrases *cum* may be left out: *omnibus cōpiīs* "with all his forces" (ag1903 §413a).
- **Manner**: *cum cūrā*, *magnā cūrā*; *iūre*, *silentiō*, *mōre* (ag1903 §412b).
- **Description**: *vir summō ingeniō*; physical features especially: *capillō prōmissō* "with long hair" (Caesar, Gallic War 5.14, quoted in ag1903 §415a). The genitive can also describe (lesson 9).
- **Respect**: *virtūte praecēdunt*; *maior nātū* "older"; *meā sententiā* "in my opinion" (ag1903 §418).
- **Worthiness**: *dignus laude*, *indignus honōre* (ag1903 §418b).
- **Price**: *magnō*, *parvō*, *vīgintī dēnāriīs* (ag1903 §416). But "of great value" in general is a genitive, *magnī*: lesson 9 (ag1903 §417).
- **Degree of difference**: *multō*, *paulō*, *nihilō*, *tantō … quantō* "the more … the more" (ag1903 §414).
- **Need**: *opus est* + ablative (ag1903 §411).

**The "in" ablative**: place where, time when, time within which (part 3, lesson 13).

**The ablative absolute** combines a noun and participle in the ablative as a background clause: part 6.

## Watch out

**Do not use *ā/ab* for a thing.** *\*Ab gladiō interfectus est*: *ab* marks a person as agent. A thing is the plain ablative of means: *gladiō interfectus est* (ag1903 §405).

**Do not use *cum* for the tool.** *\*Cum gladiō pugnat*, by analogy with English "with" or Spanish *con* and French *avec*, says "he fights in company with a sword". The tool takes no preposition: *gladiō pugnat*. (*Cum tēlō esse* "to go armed" is accompaniment, ag1903 §413.)

**Do not leave out *cum* with manner when there is no adjective.** *\*Cūrā labōrat* is ambiguous; *cum cūrā labōrat* "he works with care". With an adjective, *magnā cūrā* is enough (ag1903 §412).

**Do not leave out *cum* with people.** *\*Amīcīs vēnit* would be means, "by his friends' help". "With his friends" is *cum amīcīs*. Only some military phrases drop it (ag1903 §413a).

**Do not give *ūtor* an accusative.** *\*Gladium ūtitur*, after English "use" or Spanish *usar*: *ūtor* takes the ablative, *gladiō ūtitur*. The same for *fruor*, *fungor*, *potior*, *vēscor* (ag1903 §410).

**Do not put the description in the genitive by English "of" without checking.** "A man of great courage" can be *vir magnā virtūte* or *vir magnae virtūtis*; physical features prefer the ablative (ag1903 §415a). But the description must have an adjective: *\*vir virtūte* alone is not Latin for "a brave man". Use *vir fortis*.

**Do not mistake respect for means.** *Pede claudus* is "lame in the foot", not "lame by means of a foot" (ag1903 §418). Ask whether the noun is a tool or a part or aspect.

**Do not use the genitive for price.** "He sold it for a hundred denarii" is *centum dēnāriīs vēndidit*, ablative. A genitive of value, *magnī*, *parvī*, appears with verbs of valuing (lesson 9).

**Do not translate every ablative "by" or "with".** Choose the English the question gives: "for joy", "in courage", "than my life", "much", "of great influence".

## Connections

Arabic marks the state in which someone acts with an accusative, the *ḥāl*, and the instrument with the preposition *bi-* "with, by"; Latin uses the ablative for both. Spanish *con* and French *avec* cover tool, manner and company alike, so a Spanish or French speaker must remember that Latin keeps *cum* for company and manner only.

## Exercises

1. Name the use of each ablative: *metū fūgērunt*; *ā patre laudātus est*; *nāve vectus est*; *cum frātre vēnit*; *pede claudus* (*claudus* "lame").
2. Say "the city was taken by Caesar with great speed".
3. Say "he was a man of great authority but unworthy of honour".
4. Translate *Gallī capillō longō erant* and name the use.
5. Correct *\*Cum gladiō et ab saxīs pugnāvērunt*.
6. Say "we need money" with *opus est* and with *egeō*.
7. Translate *Multō plūrēs mīlitēs paucīs post diēbus vēnērunt* and explain both ablatives.
8. Say "he sold the horse for a small price and bought a book".
9. Why is *vincit vīribus* hard to label, and does it matter?

## Worked answers

1. *Metū*: cause, "they fled out of fear" (ag1903 §404). *Ā patre*: agent, a person with a passive (ag1903 §405). *Nāve*: means, "carried by ship" (ag1903 §409). *Cum frātre*: accompaniment (ag1903 §413). *Pede*: respect, "lame in the foot" (ag1903 §418).
2. *Urbs ā Caesare magnā celeritāte capta est.* Agent *ā Caesare*; manner with an adjective, so no *cum* needed; perfect passive agreeing with *urbs* (part 4).
3. *Vir magnā auctōritāte erat, sed honōre indignus.* Description with an adjective (ag1903 §415); *indignus* takes the ablative (ag1903 §418b).
4. "The Gauls had long hair", literally "were of long hair". Description, a physical feature, in the ablative (ag1903 §415a).
5. *Gladiīs et saxīs pugnāvērunt.* Tools take the plain ablative of means; *cum* would mean company, *ab* an agent (ag1903 §§405, 409).
6. *Pecūniā nōbīs opus est* (ag1903 §411), with *nōbīs* the dative of the person in need; or *Pecūniā egēmus* (ag1903 §401).
7. "Many more soldiers came a few days later." *Multō*: degree of difference with *plūrēs*, "more by much". *Paucīs diēbus* with *post*: degree of difference again, "later by a few days" (ag1903 §414).
8. *Equum parvō vēndidit et librum ēmit.* *Parvō*: ablative of price (ag1903 §416). *Ēmit*: perfect of *emō*, with long *ē* (Wiktionary).
9. *Vīribus* can be the means of winning or the respect in which he wins (ag1903 §418a). Both give good English, "wins by strength". The labels are ours; it does not matter.
