## The idea

Part 3 gave the core of each case. The genitive ties a noun to another noun: "of". The dative names the person concerned: "to, for". This lesson takes the uses that look strange from English and shows that each grows from that core.

**The genitive** is at heart a case of belonging. Allen and Greenough show how even the "objective" genitive grows from it. In *odium Caesaris* "hatred of Caesar", the hatred "belongs" to Caesar in a passive sense, as the thing he receives; so the genitive can name either the one who hates or the one who is hated. They add that the line between the two "is very unstable and is often lost sight of": *amor patris* can be "a father's love" or "love for a father" (ag1903 §348 note). Context decides.

The **partitive genitive**, or genitive of the whole, names the whole that a part is taken from: *pars mīlitum* "part of the soldiers", *nēmō eōrum* "not one of them", *satis pecūniae* "enough (of) money" (ag1903 §346). English "of" does the same in "some of the money".

**The dative** names the person or thing an action is aimed at or matters to. Some verbs that English treats as transitive take the dative in Latin. Allen and Greenough explain why: these verbs "retain an original intransitive meaning". *Invideō* "envy" is literally "look askance at" someone; *serviō* is "be a slave to"; *suādeō*, the verb inside *persuādeō*, is "make a thing pleasant (sweet) to" (ag1903 §367). Read them with "to" and the dative is natural: *tibi invideō* "I look askance at you"; *dominō servit* "he is a slave to his master".

The **dative of purpose** says what something serves as: *auxiliō mittere* "send as help". Allen and Greenough describe it as a use "once apparently general" that survives in only a few constructions (ag1903 §382). It usually comes with a second dative of the person: *nostrīs auxiliō* "as help for our men". This is the **double dative**.

## How it is built

**Reading a genitive.** Ask what the two nouns have to do with each other.

1. Does the second noun own or produce the first? Possessive or subjective: *fāma Caesaris*.
2. Is the first noun a feeling or action, and the second its target? Objective: *amor patriae* "love for the homeland", *metus mortis* "fear of death" (ag1903 §348).
3. Is the first word a part, an amount, a number, a superlative or a neuter pronoun? Partitive: *multī mīlitum*, *nihil novī* "nothing new", *fortissimus omnium* (ag1903 §346).
4. Is it a noun with an adjective, describing? Quality: *vir magnī animī* (ag1903 §345).
5. Is it an adjective of value with a verb of valuing? Value: *magnī aestimō* "I value highly" (ag1903 §417).

**Genitive with verbs and adjectives.** Learn these groups.

1. Remembering and forgetting: *meminī*, *oblīvīscor*, *reminīscor* take the genitive of a person or thing one is mindful of, the accusative of facts one recalls (ag1903 §350).
2. Pity: *misereor* "pity" takes the genitive (ag1903 §354a).
3. Accusing, condemning, acquitting: the charge is genitive: *fūrtī damnātus* "convicted of theft"; *caedis absolūtus* "acquitted of murder" (ag1903 §352). Allen and Greenough trace this to the genitive of value: one was condemned "to pay" a sum, in a society where offences were settled by fines (ag1903 §352 note).
4. Adjectives of desire, knowledge, memory, fullness, sharing and their opposites: *cupidus glōriae*, *perītus bellī* "skilled in war", *imperītus* "unskilled in", *memor* "mindful of", *immemor* "forgetful of", *plēnus* "full of" (ag1903 §349a).

**Dative with verbs.**

1. Verbs of favouring, helping, pleasing, trusting, believing, persuading, commanding, obeying, serving, resisting, envying, pardoning and sparing take the dative (ag1903 §367): *crēdō*, *faveō*, *ignōscō*, *imperō*, *invideō*, *noceō*, *parcō*, *pāreō*, *persuādeō*, *placeō*, *resistō*, *serviō*, *studeō*.
2. Many compounds of *ad*, *ante*, *con*, *in*, *inter*, *ob*, *post*, *prae*, *prō*, *sub*, *super* take a dative in their new meaning: *praesum* "be in charge of", *succurrō* "run to help", *occurrō* "meet" (ag1903 §370). A transitive one has both: *legiōnī lēgātum praeficit* "he puts a lieutenant in command of the legion".
3. *Nūbō* "marry", said of a woman, takes the dative of the husband (Wiktionary).

**The double dative.**

1. Take the abstract noun that says what the thing serves as: *auxilium* "help", *praesidium* "protection", *ūsus* "use", *salūs* "safety", *odium* "hatred", *cūra* "concern".
2. Put it in the dative singular: *auxiliō*, *ūsuī*, *odiō*.
3. Add the person concerned in the dative: *nostrīs*.
4. The verb is usually *sum*, or *mittō*, *relinquō*, *veniō*: *Magnō ūsuī nostrīs fuit* "it was of great use to our men" (ag1903 §382).

## Where it applies

**Genitive**

- **Partitive** after: nouns of part and quantity, *pars*, *nēmō*; numerals, comparatives, superlatives and the ūnus group, *ūnus tribūnōrum*, *maior frātrum* "the elder of the brothers", *alter cōnsulum*; neuter pronouns and words of amount, *nihil*, *quid*, *multum*, *plūs*, *tantum*; adverbs, *satis*, *parum*, *ubi gentium* "where in the world" (ag1903 §346a).
- **Objective** after nouns of feeling and action, *amor*, *metus*, *cūra*, *spēs*, *memoria*, and after adjectives (ag1903 §§348-349).
- **Quality**, always with an adjective: *vir magnī animī*; in classical prose the genitive of quality is much less common than the ablative and practically confined to measure and number, *fossa trium pedum* "a ditch three feet deep", and to nouns with *magnus*, *maximus*, *summus* or *tantus*, as in *vir magnī animī* (ag1903 §345 note and b).
- **Value**: *magnī*, *parvī*, *plūris*, *minōris*, *tantī*, *nihilī* with *aestimō*, *faciō*, *putō*, *sum*: *magnī aestimō* "I value highly", *Est mihi tantī* "it is worth it to me" (ag1903 §417).
- **Charge**, with verbs of accusing and condemning (ag1903 §352).
- **With *meminī*, *oblīvīscor*, *misereor*** (ag1903 §§350, 354).
- **With *potior*** in the phrase *rērum potīrī* "to take control of affairs" (ag1903 §410a).
- **With *similis***: Cicero uses the genitive of persons, *patris similis*, and either case of things (ag1903 §385c).
- **With *causā*** "for the sake of", placed before it: *glōriae causā* (ag1903 §404c).

**Dative**

- **Indirect object** with verbs of giving, telling and showing (part 3).
- **Special verbs** of §367, read with "to": *tibi parcō* "I am sparing to you".
- **Compound verbs** of §370: *praesum*, *praeficiō*, *succurrō*, *occurrō*, *adsum*; *bellum īnferre* + dative "make war on".
- **Possession** with *sum*: *Mihi est liber* (part 3).
- **Reference**, the person in whose eyes or for whose sake: *tibi arās* (ag1903 §376).
- **The "ethical" dative**: a personal pronoun showing interest, *Quid mihi Celsus agit?* "what, pray, is Celsus up to?" (Horace, quoted in ag1903 §380).
- **Purpose**, double dative: *auxiliō*, *praesidiō*, *subsidiō*, *ūsuī*, *salūtī*, *odiō*, *cūrae*. In military language also with concrete nouns: *locum castrīs dēligit* "he chooses a site for a camp" (ag1903 §382).
- **With adjectives** of fitness, nearness, likeness, friendship, pleasing: *aptus*, *proximus*, *similis*, *pār*, *fidēlis*, *ūtilis*, *grātus*, *amīcus*, *inimīcus* (ag1903 §384).
- **With the passive of a dative verb**, impersonally: *mihi parcitur* (part 4, lesson 11).

## Watch out

**Do not give *iuvō*, *laedō*, *iubeō*, *dēlectō* the dative.** They mean "help", "harm", "order", "please", like dative verbs, but take the accusative (ag1903 §367a): *amīcum iuvō*, *mīlitēs iubet*. Contrast *amīcō succurrō*, *mīlitibus imperat*.

**Do not use the partitive genitive with a cardinal by default.** *Ūnus mīlitum* exists, but *ūnus ex mīlitibus* is the regular prose form (ag1903 §346c).

**Do not use a third-declension adjective as a partitive genitive.** Allen and Greenough note *nihil novī* but *nihil memorābile*, not *\*nihil memorābilis* (ag1903 §346a note 2). Write *nihil grave*, *nihil ūtile*.

**Do not read *amor patris* only one way.** It can be the father's love or love for the father (ag1903 §348 note). Let the context choose.

**Do not use *auxilium* in the accusative for "as help".** *\*Auxilium nostrīs mīsit* says he sent "help" as an object, which is possible; but "sent the cavalry as help" is *equitēs auxiliō mīsit*, with the dative of purpose. The purpose noun is regularly abstract and singular (ag1903 §382 note 1).

**Do not use the ablative for value.** "I value you highly" is *tē magnī aestimō*, genitive. The ablative is for an actual price paid (lesson 8).

**Do not give *nūbō* an accusative.** *\*Mārcum nūpsit*: *Mārcō nūpsit* "she married Marcus". A man "leads a wife home": *uxōrem dūcit*.

## Connections

Spanish and French lost the dative as a case but kept its logic in the pronouns: Spanish *le* and French *lui* stand for the person a thing is given to, said to or done for, as *mihi* and *tibi* did. Spanish *obedecer a alguien* and French *obéir à quelqu'un* keep the "to" that Latin *pāreō* took with the dative.

## Exercises

1. Translate *Nēmō nostrum perīculī immemor est*.
2. Say "a large part of the citizens" and "enough money".
3. Say "the judge acquitted the defendant of murder" (*caedēs*).
4. Translate *Caesar duās legiōnēs castrīs praesidiō relīquit*.
5. Say "I pardon you and spare your brother".
6. Correct *\*Mīlitibus iussit* and *\*Amīcum succurrimus*.
7. Translate *Tē magnī aestimō* and say why *magnī* is genitive.
8. Is *amor patriae* subjective or objective? And *amor patris*?
9. Say "this was a great help to the allies" with a double dative.

## Worked answers

1. "None of us is forgetful of the danger." *Nostrum*: partitive genitive of *nōs* (part 3). *Perīculī*: genitive with *immemor* (ag1903 §349a).
2. *Magna pars cīvium*; *satis pecūniae*. Both partitive: after a noun of part, and after an adverb of quantity (ag1903 §346a).
3. *Iūdex reum caedis absolvit.* The charge goes in the genitive (ag1903 §352).
4. "Caesar left two legions as a guard for the camp." *Castrīs*: the thing protected; *praesidiō*: the purpose, double dative (ag1903 §382).
5. *Tibi ignōscō et frātrī tuō parcō.* Both verbs take the dative (ag1903 §367).
6. *Mīlitēs iussit*: *iubeō* takes the accusative (ag1903 §367a). *Amīcō succurrimus*: *succurrō* is a compound of *sub* that takes the dative (ag1903 §370).
7. "I value you highly." Indefinite value is a genitive of quality with verbs of valuing (ag1903 §417). A real price would be ablative.
8. *Amor patriae* is objective: the homeland cannot love; it is loved. *Amor patris* can be either: "a father's love" (subjective) or "love for a father" (objective) (ag1903 §348 note).
9. *Hoc sociīs magnō auxiliō fuit.* *Auxiliō*: purpose; *sociīs*: the persons concerned; *magnō* agrees with *auxiliō* (ag1903 §382).
