## The idea

A **relative clause** is a clause that describes a noun, the way an adjective does: "the soldier *whom you saw*", "the city *which they built*". It opens with a **relative pronoun**, Latin *quī, quae, quod* "who, which, that". The noun it describes is its **antecedent**, "the word that goes before" (ag1903 §304).

The relative pronoun has two jobs at once (ag1903 §303). It is a pronoun inside its own clause, with a case like any noun: subject, object, "to whom", "with which". And it links its clause to the antecedent. Every rule in this lesson follows from that double job.

1. The link is shown by **gender and number**: the relative copies them from its antecedent, so you can see which noun it describes.
2. The job inside the clause is shown by **case**: the relative takes whatever case its own clause needs, whatever the case of the antecedent (ag1903 §305).

Where does *quī* come from? It shares its stem with the question word *quis* (ag1903 §150), and you have already seen that the question sense is thought to be the original one. How a question word became a linking word, Allen and Greenough say, happened "at such an early period that the steps of the process cannot now be traced" (ag1903 §303 note). So the forms are explained; the history of the use is not.

## How it is built

**The forms** are those of the question adjective *quī* (lesson 1 of this part), with one difference in the plural: the relative never has *quis*. Masculine *quī*, *quem*, *cuius*, *cui*, *quō*; feminine *quae*, *quam*, *cuius*, *cui*, *quā*; neuter *quod*, *quod*, *cuius*, *cui*, *quō*. Plural *quī*, *quae*, *quae*; *quōs*, *quās*, *quae*; *quōrum*, *quārum*, *quōrum*; *quibus* (ag1903 §147). The table at the end gives them all.

**Building a relative clause.** Say "the soldier whom you saw is my brother".

1. Main clause: *mīles frāter meus est*.
2. The relative describes *mīles*: masculine singular.
3. Inside its clause, "you saw him": the soldier is the object, so accusative: *quem*.
4. The relative clause usually stands right after its antecedent: *Mīles quem vīdistī frāter meus est.*

Say "the women to whom I gave the money were poor".

1. Main clause: *fēminae pauperēs erant*.
2. Antecedent *fēminae*: feminine plural.
3. "I gave the money to them": dative: *quibus*.
4. *Fēminae quibus pecūniam dedī pauperēs erant.*

Say "the city from which they came is large".

1. Antecedent *urbs*: feminine singular.
2. "They came from it": *ex* + ablative: *ex quā*. A relative adverb can replace the phrase: *unde* "from where" (ag1903 §308g).
3. *Urbs ex quā* (or *unde*) *vēnērunt magna est.*

**The verb agrees with the antecedent in person.** If the antecedent is "you", the verb inside is second person: *Pater noster, quī es in caelīs* "our Father, who are in heaven" (ag1903 §316a).

***cum* is joined to the end**, as with the personal pronouns: *quōcum* "with whom", *quibuscum* (ag1903 §150d).

**The antecedent can be left out**, especially when it means "the person" or "the thing": *quī nōn est mēcum* "(he) who is not with me" (ag1903 §307c). A demonstrative often marks the gap: *is quī*, *ea quae*, *id quod*.

**The relative clause can come first.** In formal prose Latin often puts the relative clause before the main clause, with the antecedent noun inside it and a demonstrative (*is*, *hic*, *ille*) pointing back to it from the main clause (ag1903 §§307b, 308d). Caesar: *Quae pars cīvitātis … calamitātem populō Rōmānō intulerat, ea prīnceps poenās persolvit* "the part of the state which had brought disaster on the Roman people was the first to pay the penalty" (Gallic War 1.12, quoted in ag1903 §308d).

**The connecting relative.** A relative often opens a new sentence and refers to something in the last one. English uses "this" or "and this" instead (ag1903 §308f): *Quā dē causā* "for this reason", literally "for which reason". Translate it with a demonstrative.

**Correlatives.** Many relative words answer a demonstrative partner (ag1903 §308h): *tālis … quālis* "such … as", *tantus … quantus* "as great … as", *tot … quot* "as many … as", *īdem quī* "the same as". English often says plain "as": *tot mala quot sīdera* "as many troubles as stars".

**The general relatives.** *Quīcumque* and *quisquis* mean "whoever, whatever" (ag1903 §151). *Quīcumque* declines its first part and keeps *-cumque*; of *quisquis* only *quisquis*, *quidquid* (or *quicquid*) and *quōquō* are common (ag1903 §151a).

**Mood.** These relative clauses state facts, so they are indicative. With the subjunctive they express purpose (lesson 7) or kind (lesson 15).

## Where it applies

- **Describing a noun**, the basic use: *mīles quem vīdistī*.
- **Any case inside the clause**: subject *quī*, object *quem*, possessor *cuius* (*vir cuius fīlium vīdī* "the man whose son I saw"), receiver *cui*, ablative with or without a preposition (*gladius quō pugnābat* "the sword he was fighting with"; *amīcus quōcum vēnit*).
- **A whole idea as antecedent**: *id quod* or *quae rēs* "a thing which", referring to a whole clause (ag1903 §307d).
- **No antecedent**: *quī* "the man who, whoever"; *quae* "the things which" (ag1903 §307c).
- **Relative clause first**, antecedent inside it, demonstrative in the main clause (ag1903 §§307b, 308d).
- **Connecting a new sentence**: *quā dē causā*, *quae ubi audīvit* "and when he heard this" (ag1903 §308f; *ubi* "when" is lesson 3).
- **In place of an English noun or participle**: *Caesar quī Galliam vīcit* "Caesar, the conqueror of Gaul"; *lēgēs quae nunc sunt* "the existing laws"; *quī legit* "a reader" (ag1903 §308c).
- **Relative adverbs** for place, especially after a town in the locative: *Athēnīs, ubi habitābat* "at Athens, where he lived"; *unde*, *quō* (ag1903 §308g).
- **Correlatives**: *tālis … quālis*, *tantus … quantus*, *tot … quot*, *īdem quī*: *īdem quod semper* "the same as always" (ag1903 §308h).
- **Whoever, whatever**: *quisquis*, *quīcumque*; *ubicumque* "wherever" (ag1903 §151).
- **Purpose and kind**: with the subjunctive, lessons 7 and 15.

## Watch out

**Do not copy the case of the antecedent.** *\*Mīlitem quem vīdī vēnit* puts the antecedent in the accusative to match *quem*. Each word takes the case of its own clause: *Mīles quem vīdī vēnit*. And *\*Mīles quī vīdistī* gives the relative the antecedent's case: the soldier is the object of *vīdistī*, so *quem*. (Allen and Greenough note that poets and comedy sometimes "attract" one into the other's case, ag1903 §306a; do not imitate it in prose.)

**Do not take the gender from English "which" or "who".** English "which" is for things, "who" for people; Latin goes by the antecedent's grammatical gender. "The city which" is *urbs quae*, feminine, not *\*urbs quod*. "The book which" is *liber quem*.

**Do not leave the relative out.** English says "the book you gave me". Latin never drops the relative (ag1903 §308a): *\*liber mihi dedistī* is two unconnected statements. Say *liber quem mihi dedistī*.

**Do not use the question pronoun as a relative.** *\*Liber quid legō* and *\*vir quis venit* use the question forms. The relative is *quod*, *quī*: *liber quem legō*, *vir quī venit*. (The two sets share most forms, so this error shows only in *quis* and *quid*.)

**Do not use an invariable "that" as in Spanish *que*.** Spanish *el libro que leo* has one word whatever the job. Latin declines: *liber quem legō*, *liber quī mihi placet*, *liber dē quō dīxī*.

**Do not join a preposition to a locative with a relative pronoun.** After *Rōmae* "at Rome", *\*Rōmae, in quā habitō* mixes constructions. Use the relative adverb: *Rōmae, ubi habitō* (ag1903 §308g).

**Do not translate a connecting relative as "who" or "which" after a comma.** *Quā dē causā*, opening a sentence, is "for this reason", not "for which reason". *Quae ubi audīvit* is "when he heard this". The Latin sentence is independent; make the English one independent too.

**Do not let a predicate noun confuse you.** The relative often agrees with a noun in its own clause that it equals: *Thēbae, quod caput est* "Thebes, which is the capital", neuter because of *caput* (Livy, quoted in ag1903 §306). It happens with "is", "is called" and the like.

**Do not read every *quod* as "which".** It may be the relative, the question adjective (lesson 1), or the conjunction "because" (next lesson). A relative *quod* has a neuter antecedent and a gap in its clause; "because" *quod* has neither.

## Connections

French *qui* continues Latin *quī* (Wiktionary). Spanish *que* comes from *quid* and took over the jobs of *quod* and *quia* as well, which is why it no longer changes for case. Japanese and Korean have no relative pronoun at all: the describing clause stands directly before its noun, *anata ga mita heishi* "the soldier you saw", Korean *dangsini bon gunin*. Latin's relative clause normally follows its noun, but the "relative clause first" order of formal Latin (*quae pars …, ea*) puts the description first, as Japanese does.

## Exercises

1. Say "the girl who is singing is my sister" and "the girl whom you hear is my sister".
2. Say "the men whose sons we saw were Romans".
3. Say "the friend with whom I came is a poet" in two ways (with *cum* and with *quōcum*).
4. Join into one sentence with a relative: *Caesar pontem rescindit. Pōns erat ad Genavam.*
5. In *Gallia est omnis dīvīsa in partēs trēs, quārum ūnam incolunt Belgae*, why is *quārum* feminine, plural and genitive?
6. Translate *Quī legit, discit* and say what is missing.
7. Translate *Quae cīvitās lēgēs nōn habet, ea lībera nōn est* and explain the word order.
8. Correct *\*Urbs quod vīdī magna erat* and *\*Liber dedistī mihi bonus est*.
9. Translate *Tot sunt sententiae quot hominēs.*
10. Translate *Quā dē causā Caesar statim profectus est* and explain *quā*.

## Worked answers

1. *Puella quae cantat soror mea est*: feminine singular from *puella*, nominative because it is the subject of *cantat*. *Puella quam audīs soror mea est*: accusative, the object of *audīs* (ag1903 §305).
2. *Virī quōrum fīliōs vīdimus Rōmānī erant.* Masculine plural from *virī*; genitive because "whose" is possession.
3. *Amīcus cum quō vēnī poēta est*, or, more usually, *amīcus quōcum vēnī* (ag1903 §150d). Masculine singular, ablative after *cum*.
4. *Caesar pontem quī erat ad Genavam rescindit.* The second *pōns* becomes the relative: masculine singular from *pontem*, nominative because it is the subject of *erat*.
5. Feminine plural because its antecedent is *partēs*; genitive because it means "of which", a partitive with *ūnam* (part 5, lesson 9).
6. "Whoever reads learns." The antecedent *is* is left out (ag1903 §307c).
7. "A state which has no laws is not free." The relative clause comes first and contains the antecedent *cīvitās*; *ea* in the main clause points back to it (ag1903 §§307b, 308d).
8. *Urbs quam vīdī magna erat*: gender from *urbs* (feminine), case from *vīdī* (accusative). *Liber quem mihi dedistī bonus est*: Latin cannot leave out the relative (ag1903 §308a).
9. "There are as many opinions as there are people." *Tot … quot* is a correlative pair; neither word changes form.
10. "For this reason Caesar set out at once." *Quā* is a connecting relative referring back to the previous sentence; *causā* is its antecedent noun placed inside the phrase (ag1903 §308f).
