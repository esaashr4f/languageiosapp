## The idea

A **temporal clause** says when the main action happens: "*when he arrived*, they fled". A **causal clause** says why: "they fled *because they were afraid*". Both are **subordinate clauses**: clauses that cannot stand alone but hang on a main clause. The word that opens one, "when", "because", is a **subordinating conjunction**.

This lesson teaches the conjunctions that state real, observed times and reasons, and so take the indicative. Allen and Greenough say these particles are "almost all of relative origin" and are "construed like other relative clauses" (ag1903 §541). You already know how a relative clause works: it states a fact about its antecedent. A temporal clause is a relative clause about a time.

You can see the relative stem inside many of them.

- *Postquam* "after" is *post* "later" + *quam* "than": "later than". *Priusquam* and *antequam* "before" are *prius*, *ante* "earlier" + *quam*: "earlier than" (ag1903 §550).
- *Quod* "because" is the neuter relative used as an adverb, a colourless "that" which took on the sense "because" early (ag1903 §539).
- *Quia* "because" is perhaps an old neuter plural of the same stem (ag1903 §539).
- *Quoniam* "since" is *quom iam* "when now", *quom* being the old spelling of *cum* "when"; the causal sense grew out of the time sense (ag1903 §539).
- *Ubi* "where" also means "when", as English "where" once could.

*Dum* is different: it was an adverb meaning "for a while", which survives in *nōndum* "not yet" (ag1903 §552). The origin of *dōnec* "until" is unknown (ag1903 §552).

Why the indicative? These clauses report what did or does happen, on the speaker's own authority. The same conjunctions take the subjunctive when the clause is only expected, intended, or someone else's claim. Those uses come later in this part; here everything is fact.

## How it is built

**"When, after, as soon as": *ubi*, *ut*, *postquam*, *simul ac*.** These take the indicative, usually the perfect or the historic present (ag1903 §543). *Simul ac* (also *simul atque*, or *simul* alone) is "at the same time as".

Say "when Caesar had seen this, he sent the cavalry".

1. Main clause: *equitēs mīsit*.
2. "When he had seen": English uses the pluperfect, but Latin views the single completed event and uses the perfect: *ubi* (or *postquam*) *hoc vīdit* (ag1903 §543).
3. Put the clause first, as Latin usually does with background: *Caesar, ubi hoc vīdit, equitēs mīsit.*

The pluperfect appears with these words only for a state already reached, and the imperfect for a situation that lasted (ag1903 §543a).

**"While": *dum* with the present.** In a past story *dum* "while" regularly takes the present indicative, even though English uses the past: *dum haec geruntur* "while this was going on" (ag1903 §556). Allen and Greenough call this a special use of the historic present.

**"As long as": *dum*, *dōnec*, *quoad*, *quam diū*.** With the indicative in any tense, each tense its natural one: *dōnec grātus eram tibi* "as long as I was dear to you" (ag1903 §555). *Dōnec* in this sense is poetic or late (§555 note 1).

**"Until": *dōnec*, *quoad*, *dum* with the perfect indicative** for something that really happened: *dōnec rediit, silentium fuit* "there was silence until he returned" (Livy, quoted in ag1903 §554). With the subjunctive, "until" means "waiting for something to happen": *exspectāvit dum nāvēs convenīrent* "he waited for the ships to gather" (ag1903 §553; the subjunctive use rests on purpose, lesson 7).

**"Before": *antequam*, *priusquam*.** With the perfect indicative for a past fact, often after a negative main clause: *neque prius fugere dēstitērunt quam ad flūmen pervēnērunt* "they did not stop running until they reached the river" (ag1903 §551a). For the future, the present or future perfect indicative: *priusquam respondeō, … dīcam* "before I reply, I shall say" (§551c). The two halves are often split: *prius … quam*, *ante … quam*. With the subjunctive, "before" means "before it could happen" (§551b): that too waits for lesson 7.

**"Because": *quod*, *quia*.** With the indicative when the speaker vouches for the reason (ag1903 §540). A word in the main clause often prepares it: *proptereā quod*, *eō quod* "for this reason, that"; *ideō quia*. Say "the soldiers are afraid because the enemy are near":

1. Main clause: *mīlitēs timent*.
2. The reason is a fact the speaker states: *quod* or *quia* + indicative: *quod hostēs prope sunt*.
3. *Mīlitēs timent, quod hostēs prope sunt.*

**"Since, seeing that": *quoniam*, *quandō*.** Always the indicative in classical Latin; they give a reason that is obvious or agreed (ag1903 §540a). Causal *quandō* is mostly archaic or late (§540a note).

## Where it applies

- **Single past events, "when, after, as soon as"**: *ubi*, *ut*, *postquam* (also *posteāquam*), *simul ac*, with the perfect or historic present (ag1903 §543). *Ut prīmum* "as soon as" is the same (§543).
- **A past state** with these words: imperfect or pluperfect (ag1903 §543a).
- **A time interval**: *post diem quīntum quam* "on the fifth day after" (Sallust, quoted in ag1903 §543a), with *post* and *quam* apart.
- **"While"**: *dum* + present, also in the past (ag1903 §556); a past tense with *dum* stresses "so long as" by contrast (§556a).
- **"As long as"**: *dum*, *quoad*, *quam diū*, poetic *dōnec* (ag1903 §555).
- **"Until", as a fact**: *dōnec*, *quoad*, rarely *dum*, with the perfect (ag1903 §554).
- **"Before", as a fact**: *antequam*, *priusquam*, with the perfect (past) or present and future perfect (future) (ag1903 §551).
- **"Whenever"**: *ubi*, *cum*, *ut* can mean "whenever" and then follow the rules of general conditions (ag1903 §542; lesson 13).
- **Reasons the speaker vouches for**: *quod*, *quia*, prepared by *proptereā*, *eō*, *ideō*, *idcircō* (ag1903 §540).
- **"Since" for an agreed reason**: *quoniam*, early *quandō* (ag1903 §540a).
- **"Not because …, but because"**: *nōn quod* + subjunctive for the rejected reason (ag1903 §540 note 3), lesson 15.
- **A reason given as someone else's**: *quod* + subjunctive, "because, as he said" (ag1903 §540): lesson 6.

## Watch out

**Do not use the pluperfect after *postquam* by English habit.** "After he had seen this" tempts *\*postquam hoc vīderat*. For a single event Latin uses the perfect: *postquam hoc vīdit* (ag1903 §543). The pluperfect is for a state reached, and in the *post … quam* form with an interval.

**Do not put *dum* "while" in the imperfect.** *\*Dum haec gerēbantur, nūntiātum est* is not the classical pattern. Use the present: *dum haec geruntur* (ag1903 §556). Read *dum* + present in a story as English "while … was".

**Do not use the subjunctive by analogy with Spanish *antes de que* or French *avant que*.** Both languages always use the subjunctive after "before". Latin uses the indicative for a fact: *priusquam respondeō* "before I reply"; *nec prius dēstitērunt quam pervēnērunt*. The subjunctive is for what was prevented or expected (ag1903 §551b).

**Do not use *cum* in this lesson's sense yet.** *Cum* "when, since" has its own rules of mood (lesson 12). *Ubi*, *postquam* and *simul ac* never need the subjunctive for a plain past event.

**Do not read *quod* as "which" when it means "because".** In *Belgae fortissimī sunt, proptereā quod … absunt* there is no neuter antecedent and no gap in the clause: nothing is missing from *ā cultū … longissimē absunt*. A relative *quod* fills a gap; "because" *quod* does not.

**Do not use *quia* or *quoniam* for "since" in the sense of time.** English "since you came" is a time, so *postquam* or *posteāquam*: *posteāquam forum attigistī, nihil fēcistī* "since you came into public life, you have done nothing" (Cicero, quoted in ag1903 §543). *Quoniam* "since" is causal only.

**Do not translate *dum* always as "while".** With the indicative it can be "while" or "as long as"; with the subjunctive "until" (ag1903 §553) or "provided that" (lesson 14). Look at the mood.

**Do not leave out the *quam*.** *\*Prius dēstitērunt ad flūmen pervēnērunt* has no conjunction. *Prius* belongs to the main clause and *quam* introduces the clause (ag1903 §550): *prius … quam pervēnērunt*.

**Do not use *ubi* "when" for "where" or the reverse without checking.** The two meanings share one word; the clause tells you. *Ubi habitat* asks "where?"; *ubi vēnit, …* followed by a main clause is "when".

## Connections

Spanish *mientras* and French *pendant que* take the indicative, as Latin *dum* does, but Spanish *antes de que* and French *avant que* always take the subjunctive, where Latin decides by fact or expectation. French *quand* comes from *quandō* (Wiktionary), which in Latin was mainly "when?" and only rarely causal. Japanese marks time and cause with endings on the clause's last verb (*-te kara* "after", *-kara* "because"), and like Latin puts the background clause first.

## Exercises

1. Say "when the consul arrived, the citizens were glad" with *ubi* and with *postquam*.
2. Say "while the soldiers were fighting, the women fled" and explain the tense of the *dum* clause.
3. Say "the boy is afraid because it is dark" (*tenebrae sunt*), first with *quod*, then with *proptereā quod*.
4. Translate *Quoniam nox est, domum redeāmus* and explain the mood of each verb.
5. Translate *Nec prius dēstitit quam urbem cēpit.*
6. Correct *\*Postquam hostēs vīderant, fūgērunt* (a single event) and *\*Dum haec gerēbantur, Caesar vēnit*.
7. In *Ubi dē eius adventū Helvētiī certiōrēs factī sunt, lēgātōs ad eum mittunt*, explain the two tenses and *certiōrēs factī sunt*.
8. Translate *Dum vīvimus, discimus* and say which sense of *dum* it has.
9. Is *quod* a relative or a conjunction in (a) *Templum quod vīdī magnum est*; (b) *Laetus sum quod vēnistī*? Explain.

## Worked answers

1. *Ubi cōnsul vēnit, cīvēs laetī erant* and *Postquam cōnsul vēnit, cīvēs laetī erant*. Perfect for the single event of arriving (ag1903 §543).
2. *Dum mīlitēs pugnant, fēminae fūgērunt.* *Dum* "while" takes the present even in a past story (ag1903 §556).
3. *Puer timet quod tenebrae sunt*; *Puer proptereā timet quod tenebrae sunt.* The speaker vouches for the reason, so the indicative (ag1903 §540); *proptereā* announces it.
4. "Since it is night, let us go back home." *Est*: indicative after *quoniam*, an agreed fact (ag1903 §540a). *Redeāmus*: present subjunctive of proposal (part 4, lesson 19).
5. "And he did not stop until he had taken the city." *Prius … quam* with the perfect indicative after a negative main clause (ag1903 §551a).
6. *Postquam hostēs vīdērunt, fūgērunt*: perfect for a single event (ag1903 §543). *Dum haec geruntur, Caesar vēnit*: *dum* "while" takes the present (§556).
7. *Factī sunt*: perfect passive in the *ubi* clause for the single event (ag1903 §543). *Mittunt*: historic present, Caesar's vivid narrative tense (part 4). *Certiōrēs factī sunt* "were made more certain", Caesar's way of saying "were informed".
8. "As long as we live, we learn." *Dum* with the indicative in the sense "as long as" (ag1903 §555): the two actions run together.
9. (a) Relative: *quod* is neuter singular from *templum* and fills the gap as the object of *vīdī* (lesson 2). (b) Conjunction "because": nothing is missing from *vēnistī*, and there is no neuter antecedent (ag1903 §540).
