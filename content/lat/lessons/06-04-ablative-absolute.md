## The idea

Lesson 3 gave you clauses of time and cause: "when the city had been taken", "because the letter had arrived". Latin has a shorter way to say the same thing. Put the noun and a participle agreeing with it in the ablative, and leave out the conjunction:

- *urbe captā*, "the city having been taken": "when the city had been taken", "after taking the city";
- *litterīs acceptīs*, "the letter having been received": "on receiving the letter".

This is the **ablative absolute**. Allen and Greenough define it: "A noun or pronoun, with a participle in agreement, may be put in the ablative to define the time or circumstances of an action" (ag1903 §419).

"Absolute" is Latin *absolūtus* "loosened, free". The phrase is not grammatically tied to any word in the main clause: it is not the subject, not the object, not governed by a preposition. It stands on its own and sets the scene, as an adverb would (ag1903 §419 note). English has a rare equivalent, the "nominative absolute": "*the work done*, we went home"; "*weather permitting*". Latin uses it constantly, Caesar on almost every page.

Why the ablative? Allen and Greenough call the origin "perhaps instrumental", that is, the "with" ablative ("with the city taken"), but note that some explain it from the locative "in" ablative, and that the ablatives of time and place must have contributed (ag1903 §419 footnote). The exact origin is not settled. In use it covers both ideas: the circumstance "with which" and the time "at which" something happened.

Why is it so common? Part 5 (lesson 7) showed that Latin has no perfect active participle: there is no word for "having taken". English "having taken the city, Caesar left" has to be turned round: "the city having been taken, Caesar left", *urbe captā Caesar discessit*. The ablative absolute fills the gap.

## How it is built

**Turning a clause into an ablative absolute.** Say "when the city had been taken, the soldiers left".

1. The background clause: "the city had been taken". Its subject is *urbs*.
2. Its verb becomes a participle. The taking came before the leaving, so the perfect participle: *captus*.
3. Put the noun in the ablative and make the participle agree: *urbe captā*, feminine ablative singular.
4. Drop the conjunction and add the main clause: *Urbe captā, mīlitēs discessērunt.*

**The participle's tense is relative**, as in lesson 7 of part 5 (ag1903 §489).

- Perfect participle: the action came before the main verb. *Urbe captā* "after the city had been taken".
- Present participle: at the same time. *Patre spectante* "while the father was watching".
- The future participle is rare in this construction and is not used in this guide's examples.

**Active English, passive Latin.** Because the commonest participle is the passive perfect, an ablative absolute often translates best as an active clause with the main subject as doer: *Caesar, litterīs acceptīs, nūntium mittit* "Caesar, having received the letter, sends a messenger" (ag1903 §420 note). Latin does not say who received the letter; the context makes it Caesar.

**No participle: two nouns, or a noun and adjective.** *Sum* has no present participle (ag1903 §170b), so "Cicero being consul" is just the two nouns: *Cicerōne cōnsule* "in Cicero's consulship" (ag1903 §419a). This is how Romans dated years: by the names of the two consuls in the ablative (ag1903 §419a). An adjective works the same way: *mē invītō* "against my will" (*invītus* "unwilling"), *exiguā parte aestātis reliquā* "when only a small part of the summer was left" (Caesar, quoted in ag1903 §419a).

**What English link to supply.** The ablative absolute says only that the two things go together. You choose the link from the sense (ag1903 §420): "when", "after", "because", "although", "if", or "with" and "without". Words in the main clause sometimes point the way: *tamen* "nevertheless" shows "although".

**Several at once.** One sentence can hold two or three ablative absolutes in a row, each a step in the story: *nōndum hieme cōnfectā, proximīs quattuor coāctīs legiōnibus* "with winter not yet over, and having gathered the four nearest legions".

**Deponents.** A deponent's perfect participle is active, so it can agree directly with the main subject and needs no ablative absolute: *Caesar profectus* "Caesar, having set out" (part 4, lesson 13). An intransitive deponent can still form one: *Caesare profectō* "after Caesar had set out", when Caesar is not the subject of the main clause.

## Where it applies

Allen and Greenough list the clauses an ablative absolute can replace (ag1903 §420):

- **Time, "when, after"**: *urbe captā*; *patre interfectō* = *cum pater interfectus esset* "when his father had been killed".
- **Time, "while"**, with a present participle: *patre spectante*.
- **Cause, "because, since"**: *dēspērātā pāce* "despairing of peace" (Curtius, quoted in ag1903 §420).
- **Concession, "although"**, often with *tamen* in the main clause: *turribus excitātīs, tamen …*; *iānuīs clausīs*.
- **Condition, "if"**: *hostibus victīs, pāx erit*; *cōnsule Milōne* "if Milo were consul" (Cicero, quoted in ag1903 §420).
- **Attendant circumstance, "with", "without"**: *nec imperante nec sciente nec praesente dominō*.
- **Dates**: *Cicerōne cōnsule*, the two consuls of a year (ag1903 §419a).
- **"Under the leadership of", "against the will of"**: *Caesare duce*, *mē invītō*.
- **A series of preliminary steps**, one after another before the main verb: *nōndum hieme cōnfectā, … coāctīs legiōnibus*.
- **A participle alone**, a few fixed phrases: *cōnsultō* "on purpose", *serēnō* "under a clear sky" (ag1903 §419c).
- **A connecting relative inside it**: *quibus rēbus cognitīs* "on learning this", one of Caesar's commonest openings.

## Watch out

**Do not use the ablative absolute for the subject of the main clause.** *\*Caesare veniente, Caesar hostēs vīdit* says the same person twice in two unconnected forms. When the participle describes the main subject, it agrees with it in the nominative: *Caesar veniēns hostēs vīdit* (part 5, lesson 7). Allen and Greenough: the noun in an ablative absolute "very seldom" refers to anything else in the same clause (ag1903 §419 note).

**Do not use it for the object either.** "Having caught the spy, they killed him": *\*explōrātōre captō, eum interfēcērunt* is avoided. Make the participle agree with the object: *explōrātōrem captum interfēcērunt* "they killed the spy they had caught", literally "they killed the caught spy". (*Explōrātor* "spy, scout".)

**Do not try to say "having taken" with an active participle.** *\*Caesar urbem capiēns discessit* means "Caesar left while taking the city": the present participle is simultaneous. Latin has no perfect active participle (part 5, lesson 7). Say *Urbe captā Caesar discessit*, or use a clause: *Caesar, postquam urbem cēpit, discessit*.

**Do not supply a present participle of *sum*.** *\*Cicerōne ente cōnsule* invents a form; Spanish and French have *siendo*, *étant*, Latin does not (ag1903 §170b). Two nouns are enough: *Cicerōne cōnsule*.

**Do not read the ablative absolute as means or agent.** *Litterīs acceptīs* is not "by means of the letters received" and *Caesare duce* is not "by Caesar". The phrase is a background clause; translate it as one.

**Do not leave the participle in the wrong gender or number.** It agrees with its noun: *urbe captā*, *oppidō captō*, *urbibus captīs*. *\*Urbe captō* fails to agree.

**Do not translate it word for word.** "The city having been taken" is clumsy English. Choose the link and voice the context gives: "when he had taken the city", "after the capture of the city", "once the city was taken" (ag1903 §420 note).

**Do not over-use it in your own Latin.** Where the participle can agree with a word in the main clause, Latin prefers that: *Caesar hostēs victōs persecūtus est*, not *hostibus victīs eōs persecūtus est*.

## Connections

Spanish and French have a close cousin: the absolute participle, *terminada la guerra* "the war (being) over", *la guerre finie*, and the gerund *siendo cónsul Cicerón*. They descend from this Latin habit, though Latin can drop the "being" that Spanish *siendo* must spell out. Japanese has no absolute phrase; it uses a clause with *-te* or *-ta ato* "after", which is how you will usually translate an ablative absolute into English as well.

## Exercises

1. Turn into ablative absolutes: "the letter having been read"; "the soldiers having been called together"; "the king watching"; "Caesar being leader".
2. Say "when the bridge had been broken down, the Helvetii could not cross" (*trānseō*).
3. Say "after hearing these things, the consul left the city" with an ablative absolute.
4. Translate *Signō datō, mīlitēs impetum fēcērunt* (*signum* "signal").
5. Translate *Mē invītō nihil fiet* and explain *mē invītō*.
6. Translate *Omnibus rēbus parātīs, tamen nōn profectus est* and name the link.
7. Correct *\*Caesare urbem capiente, Caesar discessit* meant as "having taken the city, Caesar left".
8. Correct *\*Cicerōne ente cōnsule* and *\*Urbe captō*.
9. In *Quibus rēbus cognitīs Caesar apud mīlitēs cōntiōnātur*, explain every word of *quibus rēbus cognitīs*.

## Worked answers

1. *Epistulā lēctā*; *mīlitibus convocātīs*; *rēge spectante*; *Caesare duce*. Perfect participles for actions before, present for the same time, no participle for "being" (ag1903 §§419, 419a).
2. *Ponte rescissō, Helvētiī trānsīre nōn poterant.* *Pōns* is masculine, so *rescissō* (participle of *rescindō*, lesson 2). The link here is "because" or "since".
3. *Hīs rēbus audītīs, cōnsul urbem relīquit.* The ablative absolute stands first as background; "after hearing" becomes "these things having been heard" (ag1903 §420 note).
4. "When the signal had been given, the soldiers attacked." *Datō* is the perfect participle of *dō*, neuter to agree with *signō*.
5. "Nothing will be done against my will." *Mē invītō*: a pronoun and an adjective, no participle, "I being unwilling" (ag1903 §419a). *Fiet*: future of *fīō* (part 4, lesson 15).
6. "Although everything was ready, he still did not set out." *Tamen* in the main clause shows the link is "although" (ag1903 §420).
7. *Urbe captā, Caesar discessit.* The ablative absolute must not refer to the main subject, and the present participle would mean "while taking". Latin has no perfect active participle, so the city becomes the subject of the phrase (ag1903 §419 note).
8. *Cicerōne cōnsule*: *sum* has no present participle (ag1903 §170b). *Urbe captā*: the participle agrees with the feminine *urbe*.
9. *Quibus*: relative pronoun, ablative plural feminine, used as a connecting relative "these" (lesson 2). *Rēbus*: ablative plural of *rēs*. *Cognitīs*: perfect participle of *cognōscō*, agreeing with *rēbus*. Together: "these things having been learned", "when he learned this".
