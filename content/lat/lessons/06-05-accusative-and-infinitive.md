## The idea

English reports a statement with a "that" clause: "he says *that the city is large*". Spanish and French do the same: *dice que la ciudad es grande*, *il dit que la ville est grande*. Latin, in its classical form, has no "that" here. It turns the reported subject into an accusative and the reported verb into an infinitive:

- direct: *Urbs magna est.* "The city is large."
- reported: *Dīcit urbem magnam esse.* "He says the city is large", literally "he says the city to be large".

This is **indirect statement**, also called reported speech or, with the Latin term, *ōrātiō oblīqua* "slanting speech" (ag1903 §578). Its core is the **accusative and infinitive**: a subject in the accusative with an infinitive for its verb. English keeps a trace of it: "I believe *him to be* honest", "they reported *the city to be* safe".

Where does it come from? Allen and Greenough explain it from the plain object (ag1903 §577). In *videō hominem venīre*, "I see the man coming", *hominem* was once only the object of *videō*, and *venīre* completed the sense. In time the object "came to be regarded as, and in fact to all intents became, the subject of the infinitive". They add that the construction is "a comparatively late form of speech", developed only in Latin and Greek, perhaps separately in each (§577).

It is used much more widely than English "that". Allen and Greenough list the verbs that take it (ag1903 §579 footnote): of **knowing** (*sciō*, *cognōscō*), **thinking** (*putō*, *exīstimō*, *arbitror*), **telling** (*dīcō*, *nūntiō*, *polliceor*, *prōmittō*), and **perceiving** (*sentiō*, *comperiō*, *videō*, *audiō*). Also verbs of **hoping, promising, threatening and swearing**, where English uses a plain infinitive: "he promises to come" is *prōmittit sē ventūrum esse*, "he promises himself to be about to come" (ag1903 §580c).

To report statements about the past and the future, Latin needs infinitives for those times. Part 4 taught two infinitives, the present active *amāre* and the present passive *amārī*. This lesson completes the set of six.

## How it is built

**The six infinitives** (ag1903 §§164, 184; Wiktionary). Build each from a principal part you already know.

1. Present active: the second principal part, *amāre*, *regere*.
2. Present passive: *amārī*, *monērī*, *audīrī*; third conjugation *-ī*: *regī*, *capī*.
3. Perfect active: the perfect stem + *-isse*: *amāv-isse*, *rēx-isse*, *cēp-isse*.
4. Perfect passive: the perfect participle + *esse*: *amātum esse*, *captum esse*.
5. Future active: the future participle + *esse*: *amātūrum esse*, *captūrum esse*.
6. Future passive: the form in *-um* of the fourth principal part, unchanged, + *īrī*: *amātum īrī*, *captum īrī*. *Īrī* is the passive infinitive of *eō* "go": "to be gone to love". The *-um* form is the **supine**, a verbal noun taught in part 7; here it never changes (Wiktionary).

The two participles inside the perfect passive and future active agree with the subject accusative: *urbem captam esse*, *urbēs captās esse*; *eam ventūram esse*. *Sum* has a one-word future infinitive, *fore*, beside *futūrum esse* (Wiktionary). Deponents: present *sequī*, perfect *secūtum esse*, future *secūtūrum esse*.

**The tense of the infinitive is relative.** It is measured from the verb of saying, not from now (ag1903 §584):

- present infinitive: at the same time as the saying;
- perfect: before it;
- future: after it.

So *dīcit eum venīre* "he says he is coming", *dīxit eum venīre* "he said he was coming": the same infinitive, because each time the coming is simultaneous with the saying. The perfect infinitive covers every past of the direct speech: imperfect, perfect and pluperfect (ag1903 §584a).

**Building a reported statement.** Say "Marcus said that the enemy had fled".

1. What was said: *hostēs fūgērunt*.
2. Its subject becomes accusative: *hostēs* (the same form).
3. The fleeing came before the saying: perfect infinitive, *fūgisse*.
4. *Mārcus dīxit hostēs fūgisse.*

Say "the soldiers hope that they will win".

1. Direct: *vincēmus*. The subject is "we", the soldiers themselves.
2. A subject referring to the main subject is the reflexive *sē* (part 3, lesson 10): *sē*.
3. Future: *victūrōs esse*, masculine accusative plural to agree with *sē*.
4. *Mīlitēs spērant sē victūrōs esse.*

**The subject is always expressed**, even where the direct statement had none (ag1903 §581): *ōrātor sum* becomes *dīcit sē esse ōrātōrem*. *Esse* is often left out of the compound infinitives: *spērō mē līberātum* for *līberātum esse*.

**"Say that … not" is *negō*.** Latin prefers *negat eum venīre* "he says he is not coming", "he denies that he is coming", to *dīcit eum nōn venīre* (ag1903 §580b).

**Clauses inside the reported statement** go into the subjunctive (ag1903 §580). That rule needs the sequence of tenses, the next lesson.

## Where it applies

- **Saying, telling, reporting**: *dīcō*, *nūntiō*, *respondeō*, *scrībō*, *certiōrem faciō* (ag1903 §579).
- **Denying**: *negō* for "say that … not" (ag1903 §580b).
- **Thinking, believing, judging**: *putō*, *exīstimō*, *arbitror*, *crēdō*, *cēnseō*, *iūdicō*.
- **Knowing, learning, remembering**: *sciō*, *nesciō*, *cognōscō*, *reperiō*, *intellegō*, *meminī*. With *meminī* the present infinitive reports something you witnessed: *tē meminī haec dīcere* "I remember you saying this" (ag1903 §584a note).
- **Perceiving**: *videō*, *audiō*, *sentiō*.
- **Hoping, promising, threatening, swearing**: *spērō*, *prōmittō*, *polliceor*, *minor*, *iūrō*, with the future infinitive (ag1903 §580c). *Polliceor* and *prōmittō* sometimes take a plain infinitive: *pollicentur obsidēs dare* "they promise to give hostages" (Caesar, quoted in ag1903 §580c note).
- **Feelings**: *gaudeō*, *doleō*, *mīror* "I am glad, sorry, surprised that": *gaudeō tē valēre* "I am glad you are well".
- **Impersonal expressions**: *cōnstat* "it is agreed", *appāret* "it is clear", with an accusative and infinitive as their subject.
- **No verb of saying at all**, when the context implies one: Livy and Caesar run on in the accusative and infinitive for whole speeches (ag1903 §580a).
- **Not after *inquam***: *inquit* "he says" introduces the direct words (ag1903 §579 note).
- **Verbs that both report and command**: *persuādeō*, *moneō* with the accusative and infinitive report a fact; with *ut* they give an order (lesson 10; ag1903 §580d).

## Watch out

**Do not add a "that" word.** *\*Dīcit quod urbs magna est*, by analogy with Spanish *que* or French *que*, is not classical Latin. It turns up in late Latin, like the Vulgate's *videō quia prophēta es*, but Cicero and Caesar say *dīcit urbem magnam esse*.

**Do not leave the subject in the nominative.** *\*Dīcit urbs magna esse* keeps the direct form. The reported subject is accusative, and so is any adjective or noun agreeing with it: *urbem magnam*.

**Do not drop the subject because Latin usually does.** *Veniō* needs no "I", but its report does: *\*dīcit venīre* is unclear; say *dīcit sē venīre* (ag1903 §581).

**Do not use *eum* for the speaker himself.** *Caesar dīxit eum ventūrum esse* means someone else will come. For the main subject use *sē*: *Caesar dīxit sē ventūrum esse*.

**Do not shift the tense of the infinitive by English habit.** "He said he *was* coming" is *dīxit sē venīre*, present infinitive, because the coming and the saying are simultaneous. *\*Dīxit sē vēnisse* means "he said he had come" (ag1903 §584).

**Do not use the present infinitive after "hope" and "promise" for a future event.** English "I hope to win" leads to *\*spērō vincere*. Latin: *spērō mē victūrum esse* (ag1903 §580c).

**Do not forget agreement inside the compound infinitives.** *\*Dīcit urbem captum esse* fails: *urbem captam esse*. The future passive is the one exception: *urbem captum īrī*, with the *-um* form unchanged.

**Do not say *dīcit … nōn* where *negō* is natural.** *Dīcit sē nōn venīre* is possible, but *negat sē venīre* is the usual Latin (ag1903 §580b). And "he says nobody is coming" is *negat quemquam venīre*.

**Do not confuse the perfect infinitive active with the pluperfect subjunctive.** *Amāvisse* "to have loved" and *amāvisset* "he would have loved" share the *-isse-*. The ending tells you: *-isse* alone is the infinitive.

## Connections

Spanish and French report with *que* + a finite verb, the type that replaced the accusative and infinitive in late Latin, as in the Vulgate's *videō quia prophēta es*. Classical Latin's accusative and infinitive has parallels in English "I believe him to be honest" and in Spanish *lo vi venir*, French *je l'ai vu venir* "I saw him come", after verbs of seeing. Japanese and Korean report with a quoting particle (*to*, *-go*) after a full clause, closer to the Spanish type.

## Exercises

1. Give all six infinitives of *capiō* and of *audiō*.
2. Report each statement after *dīcit*: *Puer currit*; *Puer cucurrit*; *Puer curret*.
3. Report the same three after *dīxit*, and explain why the infinitives do not change.
4. Say "Caesar said that he would come" and "Caesar said that he (another man) would come".
5. Say "I hope that the city will be taken" and "I hope that the cities will be taken".
6. Say "the messenger reported that the enemy had been defeated".
7. Translate *Negat sē quemquam vīdisse.*
8. Correct *\*Dīcit quod Mārcus venit* and *\*Spērō vincere* (meant as "I hope I shall win").
9. In *Vōs autem quem mē esse dīcitis?*, why is *quem* accusative?
10. Translate *Tōtīus Galliae sēsē potīrī posse spērant* and explain *sēsē* and *posse*.

## Worked answers

1. *Capere, capī, cēpisse, captum esse, captūrum esse, captum īrī*. *Audīre, audīrī, audīvisse, audītum esse, audītūrum esse, audītum īrī* (Wiktionary).
2. *Dīcit puerum currere* (same time), *puerum cucurrisse* (before), *puerum cursūrum esse* (after) (ag1903 §584).
3. *Dīxit puerum currere / cucurrisse / cursūrum esse*. The infinitive's tense is measured from the verb of saying, not from now, so it is the same after *dīcit* and *dīxit* (ag1903 §584). The English changes ("was running", "had run", "would run"); the Latin does not.
4. *Caesar dīxit sē ventūrum esse*: *sē* for the main subject. *Caesar dīxit eum ventūrum esse*: *eum* for someone else (part 3, lesson 11).
5. *Spērō urbem captum īrī* and *Spērō urbēs captum īrī*. The *-um* form of the future passive infinitive never agrees, so the two differ only in the subject (ag1903 §580c).
6. *Nūntius nūntiāvit hostēs victōs esse.* Perfect passive infinitive, *victōs* agreeing with *hostēs* (masculine accusative plural).
7. "He says he has not seen anyone", "he denies seeing anyone". *Negō* for a negative report (ag1903 §580b); "anyone" after a negative is *quisquam* (part 5, lesson 10).
8. *Dīcit Mārcum venīre*: classical Latin uses the accusative and infinitive, not a "that" clause. *Spērō mē victūrum esse*: verbs of hoping take the future infinitive with an expressed subject (ag1903 §§580c, 581).
9. It is the predicate of *esse*, and a predicate agrees in case with the subject it describes, here the accusative *mē*. "You say that I am whom?"
10. "They hope they can get control of the whole of Gaul." *Sēsē*: reflexive, the subject of the infinitive, referring to the main subject (ag1903 §581). *Posse*: present infinitive, here with a future sense, "will be able" (ag1903 §584b). *Potīrī* takes the genitive *Galliae*.
