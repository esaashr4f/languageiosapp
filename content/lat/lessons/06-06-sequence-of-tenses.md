## The idea

Most of the rest of this part is about subordinate clauses in the subjunctive: purpose, result, indirect commands, fears, reported questions, *cum* clauses. Before you meet them, you need the rule that sets their tense. It is called the **sequence of tenses**: the tense of a subordinate subjunctive follows from the tense of the main verb (ag1903 §482).

Here is why such a rule is needed. The subjunctive has four tenses (part 4, lessons 17 and 18), and no future. In a main clause they mean different things. In a subordinate clause their job changes: they no longer tell you the time on their own. They tell you two things only:

1. **Is the main verb about present-and-future, or about the past?**
2. **Does the subordinate action come before the main action, or not?**

So the four subjunctive tenses form a grid (ag1903 §§483-484):

- main verb about present or future, subordinate action not yet complete: **present** subjunctive;
- main verb about present or future, subordinate action already complete: **perfect** subjunctive;
- main verb about the past, subordinate action not complete: **imperfect** subjunctive;
- main verb about the past, subordinate action complete: **pluperfect** subjunctive.

Allen and Greenough call the first group of main verbs **primary** tenses and the second **secondary** (ag1903 §482). Primary: present, future and future perfect indicative, the imperative, and the present and perfect subjunctive. Secondary: imperfect, perfect and pluperfect indicative, and the imperfect and pluperfect subjunctive.

This is relative time again, the same idea as the infinitives of the last lesson and the participles of part 5: the subordinate verb is timed from the main verb.

One honest warning. The dossier's sources stress that the sequence of tenses is a schoolbook regularisation of what Roman writers usually did, and that exceptions in real authors are normal (pinkster2015). Allen and Greenough themselves list a page of special cases (ag1903 §485). Learn the rule, because it is right most of the time and it lets you build correct Latin. Then expect the exceptions this lesson shows.

## How it is built

**The first place you will use it: clauses inside a reported statement.** The last lesson put the main verb of a reported statement into the infinitive. Any clause *inside* it, a relative clause or a *quod* clause, goes into the subjunctive (ag1903 §580). The subjunctive here does not add a meaning of wish or possibility; Allen and Greenough say it only marks the clause as part of what someone else said (ag1903 §577).

Say "he says that the book which he is reading is good".

1. The main verb *dīcit* is present: primary.
2. The reported statement: *librum bonum esse*.
3. The relative clause inside it, "which he is reading", is not complete before the saying: present subjunctive, *quem legat*.
4. *Dīcit librum quem legat bonum esse.*

Now "he said that the book which he was reading was good".

1. *Dīxit* is perfect: secondary.
2. The reading is not complete before the saying: imperfect subjunctive, *quem legeret*.
3. *Dīxit librum quem legeret bonum esse.*

And "he said that the book which he had read was good": complete before the saying, secondary, so pluperfect: *quem lēgisset*.

**The same rule gives the reported reason.** Lesson 3 said *quod* "because" takes the indicative when the speaker vouches for the reason. When the reason is someone else's, *quod* takes the subjunctive, and the sequence chooses the tense (ag1903 §540): *Noctū ambulābat Themistoclēs, quod somnum capere nōn posset* "Themistocles used to walk about at night because (he said) he could not sleep".

**What about the future?** The subjunctive has no future tense. When the subordinate action lies after the main verb and the future must be clear, Latin uses the future participle with *sim* or *essem*: *quid factūrus sīs* "what you will do", *quid factūrus essēs* "what you would do" (ag1903 §483). Purpose and result clauses do not need it: their meaning already points forward.

## Where it applies

- **Clauses inside reported statements**: relative, causal, temporal clauses all go into the subjunctive and follow the sequence (ag1903 §580).
- **Reported reasons**: *quod*, *quia* + subjunctive (ag1903 §540).
- **Every subordinate subjunctive in the rest of this part**: purpose (lesson 7), result (lesson 9), indirect commands and fears (lesson 10), indirect questions (lesson 11), *cum* clauses (lesson 12), and the subjunctive relatives and *quīn* clauses (lesson 15).
- **The future in the subjunctive**: *-ūrus sim*, *-ūrus essem* (ag1903 §483).
- **After the future perfect**: a future perfect in the direct form becomes perfect subjunctive after a primary verb, pluperfect after a secondary one (ag1903 §484c).

The exceptions are part of the map (ag1903 §485).

- **Perfect with primary sequence**, when it means "have done" with the present in view: *mīsit … ut iūdicet* (§485a).
- **Historic present with either sequence**, even both in one sentence: Caesar's *mittunt … quī dīcerent … ut liceat* (§485e).
- **Result clauses keep their own time**: after a past main verb, a result that is a fact may be perfect subjunctive, and one still true now may be present (§485c; lesson 9).
- **A general truth after a past verb** still follows the sequence in Latin, though English keeps the present: "it became clear how much power anger *has*" is *quantam vim īra habēret* (Livy, quoted in ag1903 §485d).
- **After a primary verb, the perfect subjunctive covers every past**: it can stand for a direct perfect or imperfect (§485b). *Mīror quid fēcerit* can be "what he has done", "what he did" or "what he was doing".

## Watch out

**Do not choose the subjunctive tense by English.** "He said that the book he was reading…" has "was", but that does not make Latin past for its own sake. The choice is the grid: secondary main verb, simultaneous action, so imperfect *legeret*. After *dīcit*, the same "is reading" is present *legat*.

**Do not use a future subjunctive.** There is none. *\*Dīcit librum quem leget* uses the future indicative inside a reported statement. Use the present subjunctive, or *lēctūrus sit* when the future must be clear (ag1903 §483).

**Do not use the indicative inside a reported statement.** *\*Dīxit librum quem legēbat bonum esse* is how English and Spanish work, but Latin marks clauses inside reported speech with the subjunctive: *quem legeret* (ag1903 §580). Writers sometimes keep an indicative for a remark of their own inside the report; that is the writer speaking, not the reported speaker.

**Do not apply the sequence to main-clause subjunctives.** A wish or proposal in a main clause chooses its tense by meaning (part 4, lesson 19): *utinam veniat*, *utinam venīret*. The sequence governs only subordinate subjunctives.

**Do not apply it to the infinitive.** The infinitive of reported statements follows its own relative-time rule (lesson 5): *dīxit sē venīre*. Only the clauses inside take the sequence.

**Do not treat every perfect as secondary without thinking.** A perfect that means "has done" may take primary sequence (ag1903 §485a). When you read, accept either; when you write, the secondary sequence after a perfect is always safe.

**Do not "correct" Caesar.** When the historic present takes both sequences in one sentence, the text is not wrong. The rule describes a habit, not a law (pinkster2015; ag1903 §485e).

## Connections

Spanish and French have their own sequence of tenses, inherited in spirit though not in form: *dice que venga* but *dijo que viniera*; *il veut qu'il vienne*, and in formal French *il voulait qu'il vînt*. Spanish *viniera* comes from the Latin pluperfect indicative *vēnerat*, not from the imperfect subjunctive (part 4, lesson 17), so the forms changed while the habit of matching tenses survived. Japanese and Korean have nothing like it: the subordinate verb's tense is relative to the main verb, but there is no special mood.

## Exercises

1. Primary or secondary? *audit*, *audiēbat*, *audīvit*, *audiet*, *audīverat*, *audī!*
2. Report after *dīcit* and after *dīxit*: "the soldiers who are fighting are brave".
3. Report after *dīcit* and after *dīxit*: "the soldiers who have fought are tired".
4. Say "he says the city which he will see is large", using the future participle.
5. Say "the boy was crying because (he said) he had lost his book".
6. Correct *\*Dīxit urbem quam vīdit magnam esse*.
7. Correct *\*Dīcit librum quem legeret bonum esse*.
8. Translate *Noctū ambulābat Themistoclēs, quod somnum capere nōn posset*, and explain *posset* twice: its mood and its tense.
9. In Caesar's *mittunt … quī dīcerent … ut liceat*, what is unusual, and why is it not a mistake?

## Worked answers

1. *Audit*, *audiet*, *audī!*: primary. *Audiēbat*, *audīvit*, *audīverat*: secondary (ag1903 §482). *Audīvit* can be primary if it means "has heard" with the present in view (§485a).
2. *Dīcit mīlitēs quī pugnent fortēs esse*; *Dīxit mīlitēs quī pugnārent fortēs esse*. Present after primary, imperfect after secondary, for action not complete (ag1903 §484).
3. *Dīcit mīlitēs quī pugnāverint fessōs esse*; *Dīxit mīlitēs quī pugnāvissent fessōs esse*. Perfect and pluperfect subjunctive for completed action (ag1903 §484).
4. *Dīcit urbem quam vīsūrus sit magnam esse.* The subjunctive has no future, so the future participle with *sit* (ag1903 §483).
5. *Puer flēbat quod librum āmīsisset.* The reason is the boy's, so subjunctive; secondary main verb and completed action, so pluperfect (ag1903 §540).
6. *Dīxit urbem quam vīdisset magnam esse.* A clause inside a reported statement is subjunctive; after the secondary *dīxit*, the earlier seeing is pluperfect (ag1903 §580).
7. *Dīcit librum quem legat bonum esse.* *Dīcit* is primary, so the present subjunctive, not the imperfect (ag1903 §484a).
8. "Themistocles used to walk about at night, because, he said, he could not sleep." Mood: subjunctive, because the reason is Themistocles' own (ag1903 §540). Tense: imperfect, because the main verb *ambulābat* is secondary and the not-sleeping is simultaneous (§484b).
9. One historic present takes secondary sequence (*dīcerent*) and then primary (*liceat*). Allen and Greenough say the historic present is felt sometimes as primary, sometimes as secondary (ag1903 §485e). The rule is a regular habit, not a law (pinkster2015).
