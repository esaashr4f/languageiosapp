## The idea

A **purpose clause** says what an action is meant to achieve: "he came *to see his brother*", "they fled *so as not to be caught*". Allen and Greenough also call it a **final clause**, from Latin *fīnis* "end, aim" (ag1903 §530).

English, Spanish and French use an infinitive: "to see", *para ver*, *pour voir*. Classical Latin does not. Allen and Greenough are firm: purpose is "never (except in idiomatic expressions and rarely in poetry)" expressed by the plain infinitive (ag1903 §533). Latin uses a clause with *ut* "so that" and the subjunctive: *vēnit ut frātrem vidēret* "he came so that he might see his brother". The negative is *nē* "so that … not": *fūgērunt nē caperentur* "they fled so as not to be caught" (ag1903 §531).

Why the subjunctive? A purpose is not a fact: it is something wanted, an action not yet done. That is the subjunctive's territory (part 4, lesson 17). Allen and Greenough trace the purpose clause to the subjunctive of command (ag1903 §529): *mīsit lēgātōs quī dīcerent* "he sent envoys who were to say" reports an order whose direct form was *dīcite* "say!". The two clauses were once side by side, an order beside a statement; the order became subordinate.

Why *ut*? Wiktionary derives it from the same question stem as *quis* and *ubi*, with the *c* lost as in *ubi*, and explains its use as a conjunction like this: commands and wishes were often accompanied by an adverb *ut* "somehow, in some way"; it became habitual, lost its own meaning, and was weakened into the conjunction that governs the subjunctive. The negative *nē* is the same *nē* as in *nē timeant* "let them not be afraid" (part 4, lesson 17); Wiktionary calls the conjunction *nē* a shortening of *ut nē*.

So a purpose clause is a subordinated wish, and that tells you everything about it: it takes the subjunctive, its negative is *nē* not *nōn*, and it looks forward from the main verb.

## How it is built

**Building a purpose clause.** Say "he came to Rome to see his brother".

1. Main clause: *Rōmam vēnit*.
2. Purpose: "so that he might see his brother": *ut* + subjunctive.
3. Sequence of tenses (lesson 6): *vēnit* is secondary; the seeing is not complete when he comes; so imperfect: *vidēret*.
4. *Rōmam vēnit ut frātrem vidēret.*

With a primary main verb, the present: *Rōmam venit ut frātrem videat* "he is coming to Rome to see his brother". A purpose clause is always present or imperfect, because a purpose always lies ahead of the action (ag1903 §§483-484).

**The negative.** Say "the soldiers fled so as not to be captured".

1. Negative purpose: *nē*.
2. Secondary main verb *fūgērunt*: imperfect, passive: *caperentur*.
3. *Mīlitēs fūgērunt nē caperentur.*

"So that no one" is *nē quis*, with the indefinite *quis* after *nē* (part 5, lesson 10): *nē quis effugeret* "so that no one should escape". "So that nothing" is *nē quid*; "so that never" *nē umquam*. "And so that not", joining a second negative purpose, is *nēve* or *neu*.

**With a comparative: *quō*.** When the purpose clause contains a comparative, Latin uses *quō* "by which (the more)" instead of *ut* (ag1903 §531a): *quō facilius* "so that the more easily". *Quō* is the ablative of degree of difference (part 5, lesson 8): "by which, the more".

**With a relative: *quī*.** When the purpose attaches to one noun, especially the people sent, Latin uses the relative pronoun with the subjunctive (ag1903 §§531.2, 533a): *lēgātōs mīsit quī pācem peterent* "he sent envoys to ask for peace", literally "who were to ask". *Quī* here equals *ut eī* "so that they". Relative adverbs work the same way: *habēbam quō cōnfugerem* "I had somewhere to flee to" (Cicero, quoted in ag1903 §531.2).

**A word announcing the purpose.** The main clause often prepares it with *idcircō*, *ideō* "for this reason", or *eō cōnsiliō* "with this plan" (ag1903 §531 note 1).

**Purpose without a main verb.** A purpose clause can stand alone at the head of a sentence, with "I say" understood: *ut ad Dionȳsium redeāmus* "to return to Dionysius"; *nē longum sit* "not to be long-winded" (ag1903 §532).

## Where it applies

- **Pure purpose**, *ut* / *nē*: after verbs of any kind (ag1903 §531.1).
- **Negative purpose** with indefinites: *nē quis*, *nē quid*, *nē ūllus*, *nē umquam*; a second one with *nēve* (*neu*).
- **A comparative inside**: *quō* (ag1903 §531a): *quō facilius*, *quō celerius*.
- **Purpose attached to a noun**, especially with verbs of sending, choosing, giving: *quī* + subjunctive (ag1903 §§531.2, 533a). With relative adverbs: *ubi*, *unde*, *quō*: *nōn habēbat quō fugeret* "he had nowhere to flee to".
- **Announced in the main clause**: *idcircō*, *ideō*, *eō cōnsiliō … ut* (ag1903 §531 note 1).
- **Standing alone**: *ut ad rem redeam* "to come back to the point"; *nē dīcam* "not to say" (ag1903 §532).
- **Other ways of saying purpose**, taught in part 7: the gerund and gerundive (*ad pācem petendam*, *pācis petendae causā*), and the supine after verbs of motion (*pācem petītum*) (ag1903 §533).
- **Clauses that are the object of a verb of wanting or ordering** ("he asked them to come") use the same *ut*/*nē*: lesson 10.

## Watch out

**Do not use the infinitive for purpose.** *\*Vēnit vidēre frātrem*, by analogy with English "to see", Spanish *vino a ver* or French *il est venu voir*, is not classical prose (ag1903 §533). Say *vēnit ut frātrem vidēret*. (Poets and early Latin sometimes use the infinitive after verbs of motion.)

**Do not use *ut nōn* for a negative purpose.** *\*Fūgērunt ut nōn caperentur* is the Vulgate's late habit or a result clause (lesson 9). Classical purpose takes *nē*: *nē caperentur*. *Ut nōn* is used only when *nōn* belongs to one word: *ut plūra nōn dīcam* "not to say more" (ag1903 §531 note 2).

**Do not write *nē nēmō* or *ut nēmō* for "so that no one".** The negative is already in *nē*; "anyone" after it is *quis*: *nē quis effugeret* (ag1903 §310a; part 5, lesson 10).

**Do not use the perfect or pluperfect subjunctive in a purpose clause.** A purpose always looks ahead, so after primary verbs the present and after secondary the imperfect. *\*Vēnit ut vīdisset* has no meaning.

**Do not use *ut* with a comparative where *quō* is usual.** *Ut facilius* occurs, but the normal prose form is *quō facilius* (ag1903 §531a).

**Do not use the future participle for purpose in prose.** *Vēnit pācem petītūrus* "he came intending to ask for peace" is a later construction "of inferior authority" (ag1903 §533d). In Cicero and Caesar use *ut* or *quī*.

**Do not read every *ut* as "so that".** With the indicative it is "as" or "when" (lesson 3): *ut vīdit* "when he saw". Only with the subjunctive is it purpose, or result (lesson 9), or a reported command (lesson 10). Check the mood first.

**Do not translate *quī* + subjunctive as "who".** *Lēgātōs mīsit quī pācem peterent* is not "he sent envoys who asked for peace", which would be indicative *petēbant*. It is "to ask".

## Connections

Spanish and French express purpose with a preposition and an infinitive (*para ver*, *pour voir*) when the subject is the same, but with a conjunction and the subjunctive when it changes: *para que lo vea*, *pour qu'il le voie*, "so that he may see it". That second pattern is the Latin one, *ut videat*. Japanese uses a nominalised verb with *tame ni* "for the sake of" (*au tame ni* "in order to meet"), closer to the Latin gerund of part 7 than to the *ut* clause.

## Exercises

1. Say "we work so that we may live" and "we worked so that we might live".
2. Say "he hid the money so that no one would find it".
3. Say "Caesar sent cavalry to pursue the enemy" with *quī* and with *ut*.
4. Say "they built a wall to defend the city more easily" with *quō*.
5. Translate *Portās obstruit, nē mīlitēs oppidum irrumperent* and explain the tense of *irrumperent*.
6. Correct *\*Vēnērunt pācem petere* and *\*Fūgit ut nōn caperētur*.
7. Correct *\*Lēgātōs mīsit quī pācem petant* (main verb perfect, secondary sequence wanted).
8. Translate *Lēgum idcircō servī sumus ut līberī esse possīmus* and explain *idcircō* and *possīmus*.
9. Translate *Ut ad rem redeam, Caesar profectus est* and say what is understood.

## Worked answers

1. *Labōrāmus ut vīvāmus*: primary, present subjunctive. *Labōrāvimus ut vīverēmus*: secondary, imperfect (ag1903 §§483-484).
2. *Pecūniam cēlāvit nē quis eam invenīret.* Negative purpose *nē*, with *quis* for "anyone"; secondary sequence, imperfect.
3. *Caesar equitēs mīsit quī hostēs persequerentur*, or *… ut hostēs persequerentur*. The relative form is usual when the purpose belongs to the people sent (ag1903 §533a). *Persequerentur*: imperfect subjunctive of a deponent.
4. *Mūrum fēcērunt quō facilius urbem dēfenderent.* *Quō* because of the comparative *facilius* (ag1903 §531a).
5. "He blocked the gates so that the soldiers should not burst into the town." *Obstruit* is perfect, secondary; the bursting in is still ahead, so imperfect (ag1903 §484b).
6. *Vēnērunt ut pācem peterent*: no infinitive of purpose in prose (ag1903 §533). *Fūgit nē caperētur*: negative purpose is *nē* (ag1903 §531).
7. *Lēgātōs mīsit quī pācem peterent.* After the secondary *mīsit*, the imperfect.
8. "We are slaves of the laws for this reason, so that we can be free." *Idcircō* announces the purpose clause (ag1903 §531 note 1). *Possīmus*: present subjunctive of *possum*, primary sequence after *sumus*.
9. "To return to the subject: Caesar set out." The main verb of the purpose clause, something like "I say", is understood (ag1903 §532).
