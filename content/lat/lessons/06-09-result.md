## The idea

A **result clause** says what followed from something: "the storm was *so* violent *that no ship could hold its course*". It is also called a **consecutive clause**, from Latin *cōnsequor* "follow" (ag1903 §536).

Latin builds it exactly like a purpose clause in the positive: *ut* and the subjunctive. "So great is the power of goodness *that we love it even in an enemy*": *tanta vīs probitātis est ut eam in hoste dīligāmus* (Cicero, quoted in ag1903 §537). The two constructions differ in three places, and this lesson is about those three (ag1903 §538).

1. **A signpost in the main clause.** A result clause is usually announced by a word of degree or manner: *tam* "so", *ita*, *sīc* "in such a way", *adeō* "to such a degree", *tantus* "so great", *tālis* "such", *tot* "so many" (ag1903 §537 note 2). A purpose clause has none, or has *idcircō* "for this reason".
2. **The negative.** Purpose: *nē*. Result: *ut nōn*, *ut nēmō*, *ut nihil*, *ut numquam* (ag1903 §537a).
3. **Sometimes the tense.** A result clause may break the sequence of tenses to present the result as a fact (ag1903 §485c).

Allen and Greenough give the cleanest pair (ag1903 §538): *custōdītus est nē effugeret* "he was guarded in order that he *might* not escape" (purpose: what was intended) and *custōdītus est ut nōn effugeret* "he was guarded, so that he *did* not escape" (result: what happened).

Why should a result, which is often a plain fact, take the subjunctive at all? Allen and Greenough derive it from the relative clause of characteristic, which describes what *kind* of thing something is (ag1903 §§534, 536): "a storm of such a kind that no ship could hold its course". The subjunctive there marks a description of type rather than a report of one event. That construction is lesson 15; for now, note that the result clause grew out of it, and that a sharp line between the two cannot always be drawn (ag1903 §537 note 1).

## How it is built

**Building a result clause.** Say "the storm was so great that no ship could hold its course".

1. Main clause with the signpost: *tanta tempestās erat*.
2. The result: *ut* + subjunctive.
3. "No ship": a negative result, so *ut* + a negative word: *ut nūlla nāvis*.
4. Sequence: secondary main verb, simultaneous result: imperfect: *cursum tenēre posset*.
5. *Tanta tempestās erat ut nūlla nāvis cursum tenēre posset.*

Say "he was so tired that he fell asleep at once".

1. Signpost: *tam fessus erat*.
2. Result: *ut statim dormīret* (imperfect, secondary sequence).
3. *Tam fessus erat ut statim dormīret.*

**The tense exception.** After a secondary main verb a result clause is regularly in the perfect subjunctive, rarely the present, when Latin wants to stress the result as a separate fact (ag1903 §485c). Cicero says of the orator Hortensius: *ārdēbat cupiditāte sīc ut in nūllō umquam flagrantius studium vīderim* "he burned with desire, so that I have never seen keener enthusiasm in anyone". The present is used when the result still holds now (§485c).

**Result as the object or subject of a verb.** Some verbs take a result clause as their object or subject (ag1903 §§568-569):

- *faciō*, *efficiō* "bring it about that": *efficiam ut intellegātis* "I shall make you understand";
- *accidit*, *fit* "it happens that": *accidit ut esset lūna plēna* "it happened to be a full moon";
- *restat*, *relinquitur* "it remains that"; *sequitur* "it follows that".

These are called **substantive clauses**, because the clause does the work of a noun (ag1903 §567). The negative is still *ut nōn*.

**A relative instead of *ut*.** *Quī* + subjunctive can express result, with *quī* = *ut is* (ag1903 §537.2): *nūlla est celeritās quae possit cum animī celeritāte contendere* "there is no speed that can compete with the speed of the mind". This shades into the clause of characteristic of lesson 15.

## Where it applies

- **After words of degree and manner**: *tam*, *ita*, *sīc*, *adeō*, *usque eō*, *tantus*, *tālis*, *tot* (ag1903 §537 note 2).
- **Without a signpost**, when the sense is clear: *multa rūmor adfingēbat, ut paene bellum cōnfectum vidērētur* "rumour added many details, so that the war seemed almost over" (Caesar, quoted in ag1903 §537).
- **Negative results**: *ut nōn*, *ut nēmō*, *ut nihil*, *ut nūllus*, *ut numquam* (ag1903 §§537a, 538).
- **"Too … to"** in English: *tam longē aberam ut nōn vidērem* "I was too far away to see" (ag1903 §537c).
- **As object** of *faciō*, *efficiō*, *perficiō* "bring about" (ag1903 §568).
- **As subject** of *accidit*, *fit*, *ēvenit* "it happens"; *restat*, *relinquitur* "it remains"; *sequitur* "it follows"; *efficitur* "it is proved" (ag1903 §569).
- **"It will happen that"**: *fore ut* + subjunctive replaces a future infinitive, especially for verbs with no supine: *spērō fore ut contingat id nōbīs* "I hope that will be our lot" (Cicero, quoted in ag1903 §569a).
- **After *mōs est*** "it is the custom to" (ag1903 §571).
- **Relative result**: *quī* = *ut is* (ag1903 §537.2), close to the characteristic clause (lesson 15).
- **A restriction, "only so far as"**: *ita … ut* can mean "on condition that" (ag1903 §537b): lesson 14.

## Watch out

**Do not use *nē* for a negative result.** *\*Tanta tempestās erat nē nāvis cursum tenēret* turns the result into an aim. The storm did not intend anything: *ut nūlla nāvis … posset* (ag1903 §537a).

**Do not use *ut nē quis* or *nē quis* in a result.** Purpose takes *nē quis*; result takes *ut nēmō*, *ut nihil*, *ut nūllus* (ag1903 §538). *\*Ita clāmāvit ut nē quis dormīret* should be *ita clāmāvit ut nēmō dormīret*.

**Do not use the infinitive for result.** English "he was too tired to walk" and French *trop fatigué pour marcher* invite *\*nimis fessus erat ambulāre*. Latin: *tam fessus erat ut ambulāre nōn posset*. Result is never the infinitive except in a few poets (ag1903 §537c note).

**Do not use the indicative because the result really happened.** Spanish and French put a real result in the indicative: *tan cansado que se durmió*, *si fatigué qu'il s'endormit*. Latin uses the subjunctive even for fact: *ut dormīret*.

**Do not forget the signpost when you write.** A result clause with no *tam*, *ita*, *tantus* reads like purpose. Put the word of degree in the main clause, as Roman writers usually did.

**Do not "correct" a perfect subjunctive after a past verb.** *Ut … vīderim* after *ārdēbat* is normal in result clauses (ag1903 §485c). In a purpose clause it would be wrong.

**Do not confuse *ut* + subjunctive with *ut* + indicative.** *Ut vīdit* "when he saw" (lesson 3); *ita ut vīderit* "so that he saw". And *ut* "as" with the indicative often follows *ita*: *ita ut dīxī* "just as I said". The mood tells you which.

**Do not read every *tantus … ut* as result.** *Tantus … quantus* compares ("as great as", lesson 2). Only *ut* + subjunctive gives a result.

## Connections

Spanish *tan … que*, *tanto … que* and French *si … que*, *tellement … que* follow the Latin pattern, a signpost and a clause, but they use the indicative for a real result: *llovió tanto que nadie salió*. Latin's subjunctive here is a matter of construction, not of doubt. English "so … that" matches Latin closely, but English also has "too … to", which Latin must turn into *tam … ut nōn*.

## Exercises

1. Say "the city was so large that no one could defend it".
2. Say "there were so many enemies that our men fled".
3. Say "he spoke in such a way that everyone understood" (*ita*).
4. Turn into purpose and into result, and translate both: *Mīles custōdītus est … (effugere)*.
5. Translate *Tam longē aberam ut nōn vidērem.*
6. Translate *Accidit ut nēmō domī esset* and say what the *ut* clause is doing.
7. Correct *\*Tanta tempestās erat nē nāvēs nāvigārent* and *\*Ita clāmāvit ut nē quis dormīret*.
8. Explain the tense of *vīderim* in *Ārdēbat cupiditāte sīc ut in nūllō umquam flagrantius studium vīderim*.
9. In John 3:16, which *ut* is result and which purpose, and what would a classical writer change in the second?
10. Say "I shall bring it about that you are free" with *efficiō*.

## Worked answers

1. *Urbs tam magna erat ut nēmō eam dēfendere posset.* *Tam* signposts the result; *ut nēmō* for the negative; imperfect after *erat* (ag1903 §§537a, 484).
2. *Tot hostēs erant ut nostrī fugerent* (or *fūgerint*, stressing the fact: ag1903 §485c). *Tot* is the signpost and does not change.
3. *Ita dīxit ut omnēs intellegerent.* *Ita* "in such a way" before a result clause.
4. Purpose: *Mīles custōdītus est nē effugeret* "the soldier was guarded so that he should not escape". Result: *Mīles custōdītus est ut nōn effugeret* "the soldier was guarded, so that he did not escape" (ag1903 §538).
5. "I was too far away to see", literally "so far away that I did not see" (ag1903 §537c). *Aberam*: imperfect of *absum*; *ut nōn* for a negative result.
6. "It happened that no one was at home." The *ut* clause is the subject of *accidit*, a substantive clause of result (ag1903 §569); *domī* is the locative (part 3, lesson 13).
7. *Tanta tempestās erat ut nāvēs nāvigāre nōn possent* (or *ut nūlla nāvis nāvigāret*): a negative result takes *ut nōn*, not *nē*. *Ita clāmāvit ut nēmō dormīret*: result takes *ut nēmō*, not *nē quis* (ag1903 §§537a, 538).
8. After the secondary *ārdēbat* the rule expects the imperfect *vidērem*. The perfect is regular in result clauses that state the result as a fact in its own right, here Cicero's own lifetime of experience (ag1903 §485c).
9. The first, *ut … daret*, is result, announced by *sīc*. The second, *ut … nōn pereat*, is purpose. Classical Latin would write *nē pereat* for the negative purpose, and after *daret* would use secondary sequence, *nē perīret* (lesson 7; ag1903 §§531, 484).
10. *Efficiam ut līberī sītis* (to several people) or *ut līber sīs*. A result clause as the object of *efficiō*; primary sequence after the future (ag1903 §568).
