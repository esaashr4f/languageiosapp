## The idea

**An indirect command** reports an order, request, warning or piece of advice: "he ordered them *to fortify the camp*", "I beg you *to help him*". Latin puts the content in an *ut* clause with the subjunctive, negative *nē*: *imperāvit eīs ut castra mūnīrent* "he ordered them to fortify the camp" (ag1903 §563). English, Spanish and French use an infinitive after many of these verbs; Latin, as with purpose, mostly does not.

This is the purpose clause of lesson 7 doing a new job. In lesson 7 the clause modified the main verb: "he came *so that he might see*". Here it is the object of the verb, the thing ordered, as a noun would be: "he ordered *that they should fortify*". Allen and Greenough call it a **substantive clause of purpose** (ag1903 §563), and the logic is the same: what is ordered, like what is intended, is not yet done.

Allen and Greenough explain how the construction arose (ag1903 §560). Its simplest form is two verbs side by side, like *volō abeās* "I want: may you go away". The second was a command or wish in the subjunctive; the first was added in front. *Ut* and *nē* came to join them.

**A fear clause** says what someone is afraid may happen: "I fear *that he may come*". Here Latin seems to turn logic upside down:

- *timeō nē veniat* "I am afraid that he will come";
- *timeō ut veniat* "I am afraid that he will not come".

So after a verb of fearing *nē* means "that" and *ut* means "that not" (ag1903 §564). Allen and Greenough explain why (ag1903 §564 note). The *nē* clause was once an independent wish: *nē veniat!* "may he not come!". Put *timeō* in front, and *timeō, nē veniat* is "I am afraid: may he not come!", that is, "I fear he will come". The wish is the opposite of the fear. The *ut* clause began the same way: *timeō, ut veniat* "I am afraid: may he come!", "I fear he won't".

## How it is built

**Indirect commands.** Say "the general ordered the soldiers to fortify the camp".

1. Main verb: *imperāvit*, which takes the dative of the person (part 3, lesson 3): *mīlitibus*.
2. What was ordered: *ut* + subjunctive.
3. Sequence: secondary main verb, so imperfect: *mūnīrent*.
4. *Dux mīlitibus imperāvit ut castra mūnīrent.*

Negative: "he ordered them not to throw back any weapon": *imperāvit nē quod tēlum reicerent*. As in purpose, "any" after *nē* is the indefinite: *nē quis*, *nē quid*, *nē quod* (ag1903 §563).

Each verb keeps its own case for the person told: *imperō*, *persuādeō*, *mandō*, *suādeō* take the dative; *moneō*, *hortor*, *rogō*, *ōrō*, *obsecrō* the accusative; *petō*, *postulō*, *quaerō* "ask from" take *ā* or *ab* with the ablative (Wiktionary).

**Two verbs that take the infinitive instead.** *Iubeō* "order" and *vetō* "forbid" take the accusative and infinitive, as in English (ag1903 §563a): *Labiēnum ascendere iubet* "he orders Labienus to climb". So do *patior* and usually *sinō* "allow" (§563c). *Volō*, *nōlō*, *mālō*, *cupiō* prefer the infinitive, especially when the subject stays the same (§563b), but *volō* can take a bare subjunctive: *volō amēs* "I want you to love" (§565).

**A verb of saying used as a command.** *Persuādeō* and *moneō* can report a fact or give an order (ag1903 §580d). With the accusative and infinitive: *eī persuāsit hoc vērum esse* "he convinced him that this was true". With *ut*: *eī persuāsit ut venīret* "he persuaded him to come".

**Fear clauses.** Say "the soldiers were afraid that the enemy would attack the camp".

1. Main verb: *timēbant* (or *verēbantur*, *metuēbant*).
2. Feared outcome: *nē* + subjunctive.
3. Secondary sequence; the attack lies ahead: imperfect: *nē hostēs castra oppugnārent*.
4. *Mīlitēs timēbant nē hostēs castra oppugnārent.*

"That … not" after a fear is *ut* or *nē nōn* (ag1903 §564). *Nē nōn* is the usual form when the main verb is negative: *nōn vereor nē nōn veniat* "I am not afraid that he won't come".

The fear may be about something already done: *timeō nē fēcerit* "I fear he has done it", perfect subjunctive by primary sequence.

## Where it applies

**Indirect commands with *ut* / *nē*** (ag1903 §563). After verbs of:

- **ordering**: *imperō*, *mandō*, *praecipiō*, *ēdīcō*, *dēcernō* (*dēcernō* also takes the infinitive, §563d);
- **asking, begging**: *rogō*, *ōrō*, *petō*, *postulō*, *obsecrō*, *precor*, *flāgitō*;
- **urging, warning, advising**: *hortor*, *cohortor*, *moneō*, *admoneō*, *suādeō*, *impellō*;
- **persuading**: *persuādeō* (dative);
- **resolving**: *cōnstituō*, *statuō* (also with the infinitive, §563d);
- **permitting**: *permittō*, *concēdō* (§563c);
- **taking care, striving**: *cūrō*, *videō*, *operam dō* "take pains": *cūrā ut valeās* "take care to keep well", a common letter ending (ag1903 §563e). But *cōnor* "try" takes the infinitive.
- **caution**: *caveō nē* "take care not to" (§563e note 2).

**The infinitive instead**: *iubeō*, *vetō* (§563a); *patior*, *sinō* (§563c); usually *volō*, *nōlō*, *mālō*, *cupiō* (§563b). Poets use the infinitive with almost any of these verbs (§563 note).

**Subjunctive without *ut***: after *volō*, *nōlō*, *mālō*, the impersonals *licet* and *oportet*, and the imperatives *fac* and *dīc*: *fac veniās* "be sure to come" (ag1903 §565).

**Fear clauses** (ag1903 §564): after *timeō*, *metuō*, *vereor*, and nouns and phrases of fear: *perīculum est nē* "there is danger that", *metus est nē*.

- *nē* "that", *ut* or *nē nōn* "that … not";
- present or imperfect for a fear about the future, perfect or pluperfect for a fear about the past.

**Fear with the infinitive**: "I am afraid *to* do" is the complementary infinitive, as in English: *vereor dīcere* "I am afraid to speak". Use *nē* only for "afraid that".

## Watch out

**Do not use the infinitive after *imperō*, *persuādeō*, *hortor*.** *\*Mīlitibus imperāvit castra mūnīre*, by analogy with English "ordered them to", Spanish *les mandó fortificar*, or *iubeō*. Write *imperāvit ut castra mūnīrent* (ag1903 §563).

**Do not use *ut* after *iubeō*.** The opposite mistake: *\*Iubet ut Labiēnus ascendat*. *Iubeō* takes the accusative and infinitive: *Labiēnum ascendere iubet* (ag1903 §563a). "Order not to" is *vetō*, not *iubeō … nē*: *vetat eōs discēdere*.

**Do not use *nōn* in a negative indirect command.** *\*Hortātur ut nōn dēficiant* is wrong for "urges them not to lose heart": *nē dēficiant*.

**Do not take the dative or accusative from English.** "Persuade him" is *eī persuādeō*, not *\*eum persuādeō*; "urge him" is *eum hortor*, not *\*eī hortor*. Check the verb (Wiktionary; part 3, lesson 3).

**Do not confuse *persuādeō ut* and *persuādeō* + infinitive.** *Persuāsit eī ut venīret* "he persuaded him to come" (a command); *persuāsit eī hoc vērum esse* "he convinced him that this was true" (a statement) (ag1903 §580d).

**Do not translate *timeō nē* as "I fear that … not".** It is the other way round: *nē* "that", *ut* "that not" (ag1903 §564). The English "lest" helps: *timeō nē veniat* "I fear lest he come", "I fear he will come".

**Do not write *\*timeō quod veniat* or *\*timeō eum ventūrum esse*.** Spanish *temo que venga* and French *je crains qu'il ne vienne* use a "that" clause; Latin uses *nē*. (French keeps a trace of the Latin *nē*: the "expletive" *ne* in *je crains qu'il ne vienne*.)

**Do not mix up fear and purpose.** *Fūgit nē caperētur* is purpose, "he fled so as not to be caught". *Timuit nē caperētur* is fear, "he feared he would be caught". The main verb decides.

**Do not ignore the sequence.** *\*Imperāvit ut castra mūniant* puts a present after a perfect; the imperfect is required: *mūnīrent* (lesson 6).

## Connections

Spanish and French put both indirect commands and fears into *que* + subjunctive: *le pidió que viniera*, *je crains qu'il ne vienne*. French's "expletive *ne*" after verbs of fearing is a survival of the Latin habit of marking the feared event with a negative word, though French *ne* comes from *nōn*, not from *nē* (Wiktionary). English keeps the logic in "lest": "I fear lest he come".

## Exercises

1. Say "I beg you to help me" with *ōrō* and with *rogō*.
2. Say "Caesar persuaded the Gauls not to cross the river".
3. Say "the consul ordered the cavalry to set out" with *imperō* and with *iubeō*.
4. Say "he forbade them to leave the camp".
5. Say "I am afraid that the enemy will come" and "I am afraid that the enemy will not come".
6. Translate *Nōn vereor nē nōn veniat.*
7. Translate *Timeō nē Mārcus hoc fēcerit* and explain *fēcerit*.
8. Correct *\*Imperāvit mīlitēs castra mūnīre* and *\*Iussit ut discēderent*.
9. Correct *\*Hortātus est eōs ut nōn fugerent*.
10. In *Nē eius suppliciō Dīviciācī animum offenderet verēbātur*, explain *nē* and *offenderet*.

## Worked answers

1. *Ōrō tē ut mē iuvēs*; *Rogō tē ut mē iuvēs.* Both take the accusative of the person and *ut* + present subjunctive after a primary verb (ag1903 §563).
2. *Caesar Gallīs persuāsit nē flūmen trānsīrent.* *Persuādeō* takes the dative; *nē* for the negative command; imperfect after the perfect (ag1903 §563; lesson 6).
3. *Cōnsul equitibus imperāvit ut proficīscerentur*; *Cōnsul equitēs proficīscī iussit.* *Imperō* + dative + *ut*; *iubeō* + accusative and infinitive (ag1903 §563a). *Proficīscī* is the deponent infinitive.
4. *Eōs ē castrīs discēdere vetuit.* *Vetō* takes the accusative and infinitive (ag1903 §563a).
5. *Timeō nē hostēs veniant*; *Timeō ut hostēs veniant* (or *nē hostēs nōn veniant*). After verbs of fearing, *nē* is "that", *ut* "that not" (ag1903 §564).
6. "I am not afraid that he will not come." After a negative main verb, *nē nōn* is the usual "that not" (ag1903 §564).
7. "I am afraid that Marcus has done this." *Fēcerit*: perfect subjunctive, because the feared thing is already past and *timeō* is primary (lesson 6; ag1903 §564).
8. *Imperāvit mīlitibus ut castra mūnīrent*: *imperō* takes the dative and an *ut* clause. *Iussit eōs discēdere*: *iubeō* takes the accusative and infinitive (ag1903 §563a).
9. *Hortātus est eōs nē fugerent.* A negative indirect command uses *nē*, not *ut nōn* (ag1903 §563).
10. *Nē* after the verb of fearing *verēbātur* means "that" (ag1903 §564). *Offenderet*: imperfect subjunctive after the secondary *verēbātur*, for something that might happen later (lesson 6).
