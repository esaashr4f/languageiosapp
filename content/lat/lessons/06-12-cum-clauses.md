## The idea

Part 3 taught *cum* the preposition, "with". There is a second *cum*, a conjunction: "when", "since", "although". It is the commonest way to open a subordinate clause in classical prose, and Caesar uses it on nearly every page. The two are different words: the conjunction was once spelled *quom* and is "a case form of the relative pronoun *quī*" (ag1903 §544). It belongs with *quod*, *quoniam* and the other relative words of lesson 3.

*Cum* takes either mood, and the mood tells you what kind of clause it is.

1. **With the indicative**, *cum* dates the main action: it says *at what time* it happened. "When Roscius was killed, the slaves were there" (ag1903 §545). This is the use English "when" has, and the use early Latin had for all tenses (§545 note 1).
2. **With the subjunctive**, *cum* describes the *circumstances* of the main action: what was going on, what had happened, why it was natural or surprising (ag1903 §546). From "the circumstances" it is a short step to "since" (the circumstances explain the action) and to "although" (the circumstances should have prevented it) (ag1903 §549).

Allen and Greenough give an English pair (ag1903 §546 note 3). "He had a fever when he was in Spain": the *when* clause dates the fever, so Latin would use *cum* with the indicative. "Columbus discovered America when he was seeking a new route to India": the *when* clause describes the circumstances of the discovery, so Latin would use the subjunctive.

Why did the subjunctive use spread so far? Allen and Greenough point to a gap you already know (ag1903 §544): Latin's present participle is limited and it has almost no perfect active participle. Where Greek or English says "having heard this, he left", Latin needs a clause, and *cum* + subjunctive became that clause. In classical writers it has largely pushed out *cum* + imperfect or pluperfect indicative (§544).

## How it is built

**Narrative *cum*: "when", in a past story.** The commonest use. Imperfect subjunctive for what was going on, pluperfect for what had happened, by secondary sequence (ag1903 §546).

Say "when Caesar was in Gaul, he received a letter".

1. A past story; the clause gives the background: *cum* + subjunctive.
2. Being in Gaul is simultaneous: imperfect, *esset*.
3. *Cum Caesar in Galliā esset, litterās accēpit.*

Say "when this had been reported to Caesar, he hurried".

1. The report came first: pluperfect subjunctive, *nūntiātum esset*.
2. *Caesarī cum id nūntiātum esset, mātūrat* (with Caesar's historic present).

**Causal *cum*: "since".** Subjunctive in any tense, by the sequence (ag1903 §549): *id difficile nōn est, cum tantum equitātū valeāmus* "this is not difficult, since we are so strong in cavalry". *Praesertim cum* "especially since", *quippe cum*, *utpote cum* "inasmuch as" make the sense explicit (§549 note 1).

**Concessive *cum*: "although".** Subjunctive (ag1903 §549). A *tamen* "still" in the main clause usually shows it: *cum prīmī ōrdinēs concidissent, tamen reliquī resistēbant* "though the front ranks had fallen, the rest still resisted".

**How to choose the English.** The subjunctive tells you only "circumstances". Read the main clause and pick the link that makes sense: "when", "since", "although", or a participle: "having seen". If there is a *tamen*, it is "although".

**The indicative uses** (ag1903 §§545, 547-549a):

- dating a past time, often with *tum* "then" in the main clause: *tum cum urbem condidit* "at the time when he founded the city";
- present and future time: *cum vēneris, cognōscēs* "when you come, you will find out" (future perfect, as in lesson 3 of part 4);
- "whenever", a repeated action: *cum … prohibent* "whenever they keep them out" (ag1903 §548);
- "inverted *cum*", where the main event is in the *cum* clause, often after *iam* or *nōndum*: *iam lūx appārēbat, cum prōcēdit* "day was already dawning when he came forward" (ag1903 §546a);
- "in that", explaining: *cum tacent, clāmant* "in keeping silent, they cry out" (§549a).

***Cum … tum*** "both … and", "not only … but also", usually with the indicative (ag1903 §549b).

## Where it applies

**With the subjunctive**

- **Narrative "when"** in a past story: imperfect for the background, pluperfect for a completed earlier step (ag1903 §546). The commonest use.
- **"Since"**: any tense by the sequence; often *praesertim cum*, *quippe cum*, *utpote cum* (ag1903 §549).
- **"Although"**: often with *tamen* in the main clause (ag1903 §549).
- **"While", in contrast**: *cum … tum* with the subjunctive when the *cum* clause concedes something (ag1903 §549b).

**With the indicative**

- **Dating a past time**, often with *tum*, *eō tempore* in the main clause; with the perfect or historic present it is always indicative (ag1903 §545 note 1).
- **Exactly the same time** as the main verb, same tense: *laetus fuī cum audīvī* "I was glad when I heard" (ag1903 §545a).
- **Present and future time**: present, future, future perfect (ag1903 §547).
- **"Whenever"**: perfect for present time, pluperfect for past time (ag1903 §548; lesson 13 on general conditions).
- **Inverted *cum***: after *iam*, *vix*, *nōndum* (ag1903 §546a).
- **Explaining "in that"**, and after verbs of thanks: *grātulor tibi cum* "I congratulate you that" (ag1903 §549a).
- **"Both … and"**: *cum … tum* (ag1903 §549b).

## Watch out

**Do not read *cum* + ablative as "when".** *Cum amīcīs vēnit* "he came with his friends" has a noun in the ablative and no verb in the clause: that is the preposition. The conjunction introduces a clause with its own verb.

**Do not assume the subjunctive means "since".** In a past story, *cum* + imperfect or pluperfect subjunctive is most often plain "when". Try "when" first; switch to "since" or "although" only if the sense demands it.

**Do not use the indicative for a narrative background by English habit.** *\*Cum Caesar in Galliā erat, litterās accēpit* is the early-Latin pattern. Classical prose describes the circumstances with the subjunctive: *cum … esset* (ag1903 §546).

**Do not use the subjunctive with *cum* for future time.** *\*Cum veniās, cognōscēs* for "when you come". The future uses the indicative, usually the future perfect: *cum vēneris, cognōscēs* (ag1903 §547). Spanish *cuando vengas* uses the subjunctive here; Latin does not.

**Do not use *cum* for purpose or result.** Unlike *quī*, *cum* never introduces a purpose or result clause (ag1903 §544). "So that" is *ut*.

**Do not translate a concessive *cum* as "when" if *tamen* follows.** *Cum … concidissent, tamen … resistēbant* is "although", not "when".

**Do not forget the sequence.** Causal *cum* after a primary verb is present or perfect: *cum … valeāmus*; after a secondary verb imperfect or pluperfect.

**Do not confuse *cum … tum* with "when … then".** *Cum multa nōn probō, tum illud in prīmīs* "while I disapprove of many things, I disapprove of this above all" (Cicero, quoted in ag1903 §549b). *Tum* here is "especially", not "then".

## Connections

Spanish *cuando* and French *quand* come from *quandō*, not from *cum*, which left no descendant in Spanish or French (Wiktionary). The Latin habit survives in another form: Spanish uses the subjunctive after *cuando* for future time (*cuando vengas*), where Latin used the indicative, and the indicative for past time, where classical Latin preferred the subjunctive. Japanese marks the same distinctions with different endings: *-ta toki* "at the time when", *-node* "since", *-noni* "although".

## Exercises

1. Say "when the soldiers were fighting, the general arrived" (narrative *cum*).
2. Say "when the city had been taken, Caesar left" with *cum*, and then with an ablative absolute.
3. Say "since you are my friend, I shall help you".
4. Say "although he was ill, he came to the senate" with *cum … tamen*.
5. Translate *Cum Rōmam vēneris, mē vidēbis* and explain the mood and tense.
6. Translate *Quae cum ita sint, discēde* and explain *quae*.
7. Translate *Vix ad castra pervēnerant, cum hostēs impetum fēcērunt* and name the use.
8. Correct *\*Cum Caesar Rōmae erat, epistulam scrīpsit* (narrative background) and *\*Cum veniās, cognōscēs*.
9. Translate *Cum multa nōn probō, tum hoc in prīmīs.*
10. In *Caesarī cum id nūntiātum esset, … mātūrat*, why pluperfect subjunctive, and why does Caesar's name stand before *cum*?

## Worked answers

1. *Cum mīlitēs pugnārent, dux advēnit.* Narrative *cum* with the imperfect subjunctive for the background (ag1903 §546).
2. *Cum urbs capta esset, Caesar discessit*; *Urbe captā, Caesar discessit* (lesson 4). The pluperfect subjunctive for the earlier event (ag1903 §546).
3. *Cum amīcus meus sīs, tē iuvābō.* Causal *cum*; present subjunctive by primary sequence after the future (ag1903 §549).
4. *Cum aeger esset, tamen in senātum vēnit.* Concessive *cum*, shown by *tamen* (ag1903 §549).
5. "When you come to Rome, you will see me." Future time takes the indicative with *cum*, here the future perfect *vēneris* for the action completed before the seeing (ag1903 §547).
6. "Since this is so, go away." *Quae* is a connecting relative, neuter plural, "these things" (lesson 2); *sint* is subjunctive after causal *cum*.
7. "They had hardly reached the camp when the enemy attacked." Inverted *cum* with the indicative after *vix*: the main event is in the *cum* clause (ag1903 §546a).
8. *Cum Caesar Rōmae esset, epistulam scrīpsit*: classical narrative *cum* uses the subjunctive for background (ag1903 §546). *Cum vēneris, cognōscēs*: future *cum* takes the indicative (ag1903 §547).
9. "While I disapprove of many things, I disapprove of this above all." *Cum … tum* with the indicative (ag1903 §549b).
10. The report came before the hurrying, and the main verb is a historic present treated as secondary, so pluperfect (ag1903 §546; lesson 6). *Caesarī* is put first for emphasis, outside its clause; Latin often moves the topic ahead of the conjunction.
