## The idea

A **conditional sentence** has two parts: an "if" clause and a main clause that depends on it. Allen and Greenough call the "if" clause the **protasis** ("what is put forward") and the main clause the **apodosis** ("what is given back") (ag1903 §512). "*If he is here*, all is well": protasis, then apodosis. The apodosis is the main clause; the protasis is subordinate.

The word for "if" is *sī*, and its negative is *nisi* "if not, unless". Allen and Greenough explain that *sī* was once a demonstrative meaning "in that way", the same word as *sīc* "so"; it gained the sense "if" from pairs like "thus … thus" (ag1903 §511).

What makes Latin conditions worth a lesson is that the mood tells you what the speaker thinks about the condition. Allen and Greenough sort them into three families (ag1903 §514).

1. **Simple conditions**, indicative: the speaker claims nothing about whether the condition is true. *Sī adest, bene est* "if he is here, all is well".
2. **Future conditions**, about what has not yet happened. Two kinds:
   - **more vivid**, indicative: the speaker treats the future as real. *Sī aderit, bene erit* "if he is here (tomorrow), all will be well".
   - **less vivid**, present subjunctive: the speaker treats it as a mere possibility. *Sī adsit, bene sit* "if he were to be here, all would be well".
3. **Conditions contrary to fact**, subjunctive: the speaker says the condition is false. Imperfect for the present: *sī adesset, bene esset* "if he were here (he isn't), all would be well". Pluperfect for the past: *sī adfuisset, bene fuisset* "if he had been here (he wasn't), all would have been well".

So the indicative states, the present subjunctive imagines, the past subjunctives deny. Allen and Greenough trace the two moods to two old sentence types (ag1903 §511): an "if" clause was once either a statement of fact (indicative) or a mild command, "suppose he were here" (subjunctive).

Why should the imperfect, a past tense, be used for an unreal present? Allen and Greenough answer from history (ag1903 §517e note 2). In early Latin the present subjunctive could still express an unreal present and the imperfect an unreal past. Over time the present subjunctive was kept for the less vivid future, the imperfect moved to the unreal present, and the pluperfect took over the unreal past. English did the same thing: "if he *were* here" uses a past form for the present.

## How it is built

**Step 1: decide the time.** Present, past or future.

**Step 2: decide the speaker's attitude.** Open (no claim), vivid future, remote future, or unreal.

**Step 3: pick the moods and tenses** (ag1903 §514). The same tense usually stands in both clauses.

Say "if you come tomorrow, you will see my brother".

1. Future, and treated as real: future more vivid.
2. The coming will be complete before the seeing: future perfect in the protasis, future in the apodosis (ag1903 §516c).
3. *Sī crās vēneris, frātrem meum vidēbis.* English uses the present "come"; Latin usually marks the future, though a present indicative is also often found (§516a note).

Say "if you had been here, my brother would not have died".

1. Past, and false: past contrary to fact.
2. Pluperfect subjunctive in both clauses.
3. *Sī hīc fuissēs, frāter meus nōn fuisset mortuus.* (This is Martha's sentence in the Vulgate, below.)

Say "if I were rich, I would buy a house".

1. Present, and false: imperfect subjunctive in both.
2. *Sī dīves essem, domum emerem.*

**Negatives.** *Nisi* (shortened *nī*) "unless, if not" makes the whole condition negative and usually means "except if": *nisi vēneris, nōn tē vidēbō* "unless you come, I shall not see you". *Sī nōn* negates one word or is used when the clause is contrasted with a positive one: *sī exerceās … sī nōn exerceās* "if you use it … if you don't use it" (Cato, quoted in ag1903 §518a). *Sīn* "but if" introduces a second, contrary condition. *Quod sī* "and if, but if" connects with what went before.

**"If anyone", "if anything".** After *sī* and *nisi*, "any" is the plain indefinite *quis*, *quid* (part 5, lesson 10): *sī quis venit* "if anyone comes".

**Mixed conditions.** The two clauses can refer to different times: *sī hīc fuissēs, nunc laetus essēs* "if you had been here (then), you would be happy (now)". Each clause takes the tense its own time needs.

**General conditions.** A condition about any occasion, "if ever", "whenever", usually has the ordinary forms. Two special patterns (ag1903 §518): the second person singular subjunctive for "you" meaning "anyone": *sī exerceās, conteritur* "if you use it, it wears away"; and perfect protasis with present apodosis, or pluperfect with imperfect, for repeated action.

## Where it applies

- **Simple present and past**: indicative in both, any tense: *sī valēs, bene est*; *sī aderat, bene erat* (ag1903 §515). The apodosis can be any kind of sentence: a command (*amā*), a question, a wish (§515a).
- **Future more vivid**: future or future perfect in the protasis, future in the apodosis (ag1903 §516a, c). The apodosis can also be an imperative or anything pointing to the future (§516d).
- **Future less vivid**: present (or perfect) subjunctive in both (ag1903 §516b-c). A present subjunctive protasis may have an indicative apodosis "from a change in the point of view" (§516b note).
- **Present contrary to fact**: imperfect subjunctive in both (ag1903 §517). The imperfect can also refer to a continuing past state (§517a).
- **Past contrary to fact**: pluperfect subjunctive in both (ag1903 §517).
- **Mixed**: each clause with its own time.
- **Verbs of duty and possibility** in an unreal apodosis often stay indicative: *dēbuit*, *potuit*, *oportuit* (ag1903 §517c).
- **"Almost" with the perfect indicative**: *paene dedit, nisi …* (ag1903 §517b note 2).
- **Future participle with *eram* or *fuī*** in an unreal apodosis: "was going to" (ag1903 §517d).
- **General conditions**: second person singular subjunctive for "anyone"; perfect + present, pluperfect + imperfect for repeated action (ag1903 §518).
- **Relative and temporal clauses with conditional force**: *quisquis*, *quīcumque*, *cum* "whenever" (ag1903 §§519, 542).
- **Conditions inside reported speech** take the subjunctive (ag1903 §589).
- **"Even if", "although"**: *etsī*, *etiam sī*, *tametsī*: next lesson.

## Watch out

**Do not use the present tense in a Latin future condition.** English "if you come tomorrow, you will see" has a present; *\*sī crās venīs, vidēbis* is loose. Latin normally uses the future or future perfect: *sī vēneris, vidēbis* (ag1903 §516a note).

**Do not use the imperfect subjunctive for a possibility.** "If he were to come" (it might happen) is present subjunctive *sī veniat*; "if he were here" (he isn't) is imperfect *sī adesset*. English "were" covers both; Latin distinguishes (ag1903 §§516b, 517).

**Do not use the indicative for an unreal past.** Spanish *si hubieras estado aquí* and French *si tu avais été là* use the pluperfect in the "if" clause and a conditional in the main clause. Latin has no conditional mood: both clauses take the pluperfect subjunctive: *sī fuissēs, nōn fuisset mortuus*.

**Do not put *sī* with the subjunctive just because the idea feels uncertain.** "If he is at home (I don't know whether he is), go in": simple condition, indicative: *sī domī est, intrā*. The subjunctive is for the remote future and the unreal.

**Do not write *\*nisi nēmō*, and do not reach for *aliquis* by English habit.** After *sī* and *nisi* "anyone" is normally *quis*: *sī quis*, *nisi quis*; *sī aliquis* is possible but emphatic, "if someone, anyone at all" (ag1903 §310b; part 5, lesson 10). *Nisi nēmō* is a double negative.

**Do not use *nisi* where you need *sī nōn*.** *Nisi* means "except if" and negates the whole condition. When "not" belongs to one word, or contrasts with a positive condition, use *sī nōn*: *sī exerceās …, sī nōn exerceās …* (ag1903 §518a).

**Do not mix the tenses of an unreal condition by English habit.** *\*Sī hīc fuissēs, frāter nōn morerētur* (pluperfect + imperfect) means "if you had been here, he would not now be dying", a mixed condition. For a single past event, pluperfect in both.

**Do not read the imperfect subjunctive in a condition as past.** *Sī dīves essem* is "if I were rich (now)". Only in a few special cases does it refer to the past (ag1903 §517a).

## Connections

Spanish *si* and French *si* come from Latin *sī* (Wiktionary). Both languages have a separate "conditional" verb form for the main clause (*vendría*, *viendrait*), so they do not need the subjunctive in both halves as Latin does. Japanese and Korean express conditions with verb endings (*-ba*, *-tara*; *-myeon*) rather than a separate word, and mark unreality with additional endings, much as Latin does with mood.

## Exercises

1. Say "if he is in the city, he is safe" and "if he was in the city, he was safe".
2. Say "if you work, you will be rich" with a future and with a future perfect in the protasis.
3. Say "if you were to work, you would be rich".
4. Say "if you worked (now), you would be rich" and "if you had worked, you would have been rich".
5. Say "unless the enemy come, we shall stay in the camp".
6. Translate *Sī quid novī audieris, scrībe mihi.*
7. Translate *Sī hoc fēcissēs, nunc laetus essēs* and name the type.
8. Correct *\*Sī hīc erās, frāter nōn mortuus esset* (meant as unreal), and give the usual form of *Sī aliquis venit, vocā mē* ("if anyone comes, call me").
9. In Livy's *pōns iter paene hostibus dedit, nī ūnus vir fuisset*, what is unusual, and why?
10. Name the type and translate: *Sī adsit, bene sit*; *Sī adesset, bene esset*; *Sī aderit, bene erit*.

## Worked answers

1. *Sī in urbe est, tūtus est*; *Sī in urbe erat, tūtus erat.* Simple conditions: indicative, same tense in both (ag1903 §515).
2. *Sī labōrābis, dīves eris*; *Sī labōrāveris, dīves eris.* Future more vivid; the future perfect stresses that the work is complete first (ag1903 §516a, c).
3. *Sī labōrēs, dīves sīs.* Future less vivid: present subjunctive in both (ag1903 §516b).
4. *Sī labōrārēs, dīves essēs* (present unreal, imperfect subjunctive); *Sī labōrāvissēs, dīves fuissēs* (past unreal, pluperfect subjunctive) (ag1903 §517).
5. *Nisi hostēs vēnerint, in castrīs manēbimus.* *Nisi* with the future perfect, future in the apodosis (ag1903 §516c).
6. "If you hear any news, write to me." *Quid* "anything" after *sī*; *novī* partitive genitive (part 5, lesson 9); future perfect protasis with an imperative apodosis (ag1903 §516d).
7. "If you had done this, you would be happy now." A mixed condition: past unreal protasis (pluperfect), present unreal apodosis (imperfect).
8. *Sī hīc fuissēs, frāter nōn fuisset mortuus* (or *mortuus esset*): a past unreal condition takes the pluperfect subjunctive in both clauses (ag1903 §517). *Sī quis venit, vocā mē*: after *sī* the plain *quis* is usual; *aliquis* is not wrong but adds emphasis, "anyone at all" (ag1903 §310b).
9. The apodosis of a past unreal condition is in the perfect indicative, *dedit*. With *paene* "almost", Latin states what nearly happened as a fact (ag1903 §517b note 2). English: "would almost have given".
10. Future less vivid: "if he were to be here, it would be well". Present contrary to fact: "if he were here (he is not), it would be well". Future more vivid: "if he is here, it will be well" (ag1903 §514).
