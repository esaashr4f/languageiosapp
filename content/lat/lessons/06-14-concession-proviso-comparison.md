## The idea

Three kinds of clause close the map of the Latin subordinate clause.

1. A **concessive clause** grants a fact or a supposition that might have prevented the main action, and then the main action happens anyway: "*although he was tired*, he worked" (ag1903 §§526-527). The main clause often has *tamen* "nevertheless".
2. A **clause of proviso** names the one condition on which the main statement holds: "let them hate me, *provided that they fear me*" (ag1903 §528).
3. A **clause of comparison** compares the main action with an imagined one: "he spoke *as if he were king*" (ag1903 §524).

Allen and Greenough warn that "the concessive idea is rather vague and general, and takes a variety of forms, each of which has its distinct history" (ag1903 §526). So there is no single rule. Each conjunction keeps the mood of the construction it came from, and once you know where it came from, its mood makes sense.

- ***Quamquam*** "however much" states an admitted fact, so it takes the **indicative** (ag1903 §527d).
- ***Etsī***, ***etiam sī***, ***tametsī*** "even if" are *sī* with a prefix: they take **the constructions of *sī***, indicative or subjunctive by the type of condition (lesson 13; ag1903 §527c).
- ***Quamvīs*** is "as much as you like": *quam* "as much as" + *vīs* "you want". Its clause was once a command, "let them be as … as you like", so it takes the **subjunctive** (ag1903 §527a note).
- ***Licet*** is a verb, "it is allowed": *licet* + subjunctive was "it is granted that…", so it is a present tense and takes the present or perfect subjunctive by the sequence (ag1903 §527b note).
- ***Ut*** "granting that" takes the subjunctive; its origin is uncertain (ag1903 §527a note).
- ***Cum*** "although" takes the subjunctive (lesson 12).

For proviso, *dum*, *modo* and *dummodo* "provided that" take the subjunctive, negative *nē* (ag1903 §528). Allen and Greenough explain *modo* + subjunctive as a wish ("only let it be…") and *dum* + subjunctive as growing out of *dum* "until" (lesson 3): English "so long as my health is good, I don't care" shows the same step (§528 note).

For comparison, *quasi*, *tamquam*, *velut sī*, *ac sī*, *ut sī* "as if" take the subjunctive. Allen and Greenough explain that these are really conditions with the main clause folded into the particle (ag1903 §524 note 1); *quasi* itself is *quam* + *sī*, "as (it would be) if" (Wiktionary).

## How it is built

**"Although", stating a fact.** Say "although he was tired, he worked".

1. A real fact conceded: *quamquam* + indicative.
2. *Quamquam fessus erat, tamen labōrāvit.*

**"However much", "although (granted)".** Say "however brave they may be, they will be beaten".

1. *Quamvīs* + subjunctive.
2. Primary sequence after the future: present subjunctive.
3. *Quamvīs fortēs sint, vincentur.*

**"Even if".** Treat it as a condition (lesson 13):

- *Etiam sī veniet, nōn eum vidēbō* "even if he comes, I shall not see him" (future more vivid);
- *Etiam sī vēnisset, nōn eum vīdissem* "even if he had come, I would not have seen him" (past unreal).

**"Provided that".** Say "let them hate, provided that they fear".

1. *Dum* (or *modo*, *dummodo*) + subjunctive.
2. *Ōderint, dum metuant.* (*Ōderint* is the perfect subjunctive of *ōdī*, which has only perfect forms with present meaning: part 4, lesson 15.)

The negative is *nē*: *dum nē*, *modo nē*, *dummodo nē*.

**"As if".** Say "he speaks as if he were king".

1. *Quasi* + subjunctive.
2. The tense follows the sequence, unlike English, which always uses "were" (ag1903 §524 note 2): *loquitur quasi rēx sit*; *locūtus est quasi rēx esset*.
3. With *quam sī* "than if", and often with *ac sī* and *ut sī*, the imperfect or pluperfect is used even after a primary verb (§524a).

**Quamquam "and yet".** At the start of a sentence, *quamquam* often means "and yet", correcting what has just been said (ag1903 §527d note): *Quamquam quid loquor?* "And yet why am I speaking?" (Cicero, Against Catiline 1.22).

## Where it applies

**Concession** (ag1903 §527)

- **A fact admitted**: *quamquam* + indicative. Also "and yet", opening a sentence (§527d note).
- **A supposition granted, "however much"**: *quamvīs* + subjunctive; poets and later writers also use the indicative (§527e).
- **"It is granted that"**: *licet* + present or perfect subjunctive: *licet omnēs mihi terrōrēs perīculaque impendeant* "though all terrors and dangers should threaten me" (Cicero, quoted in ag1903 §527b).
- **"Granting that"**: *ut* (*ut nōn*) + subjunctive (§527a).
- **"Even if"**: *etsī*, *etiam sī*, *tametsī*, with the moods of *sī* (§527c). *Tametsī* with the subjunctive is very rare (§527c note 1).
- **A plain *sī* clause** can be concessive: *sī essent inimīcitiae, tamen …* "even if there were quarrels, still …" (§527c note 2).
- **"Although"**: *cum* + subjunctive (lesson 12); *quī* + subjunctive (lesson 15); an ablative absolute (lesson 4); a participle (part 5).
- **An independent concession**: the hortatory subjunctive, *sit fūr* "suppose he is a thief", negative *nē* (ag1903 §440).

**Proviso** (ag1903 §528)

- *dum*, *modo*, *dummodo*, *tantum ut* + subjunctive; negative *nē*.
- *ita … ut* "only on condition that" (§528b; lesson 9).

**Comparison "as if"** (ag1903 §524)

- *quasi*, *tamquam*, *tamquam sī*, *velut*, *velut sī*, *ac sī*, *ut sī* + subjunctive, following the sequence;
- *quam sī* "than if", and often *ac sī*, *ut sī*: imperfect or pluperfect even after a primary verb (§524a and note).
- **Plain comparison with a fact**, "just as": *sīcut*, *velut*, *ut* + indicative, no subjunctive: *sīcut dīxī* "as I said".

## Watch out

**Do not put *quamquam* with the subjunctive in classical prose.** *\*Quamquam fessus esset, labōrāvit* follows Spanish *aunque estuviera*. Cicero and Caesar use the indicative for a real fact: *quamquam fessus erat*. (Livy and later writers relax this: ag1903 §527e.)

**Do not put *quamvīs* with the indicative in prose.** *\*Quamvīs fortēs sunt* is poetic (Vergil's *quamvīs est rūstica*). In prose: *quamvīs fortēs sint*.

**Do not give *licet* "although" a past tense.** *Licet* is itself present, so its clause takes the present or perfect subjunctive: *licet veniat*, *licet vēnerit*; not *\*licet venīret* (ag1903 §527b note).

**Do not use *nōn* with *dum*, *modo*, *dummodo*.** *\*Dummodo nōn veniat* should be *dummodo nē veniat* (ag1903 §528).

**Do not confuse *dum* "provided that" with *dum* "while".** *Dum* + present indicative is "while" (lesson 3); *dum* + subjunctive is "until" or "provided that". The main clause tells you which: *ōderint, dum metuant* is a condition on the hating, not a time.

**Do not use "were" by English habit after *quasi*.** *\*Loquitur quasi rēx esset* breaks the sequence after the primary *loquitur*. Cicero almost always follows the sequence with *quasi* and *tamquam* (ag1903 §524a note): *loquitur quasi rēx sit*.

**Do not use the subjunctive for a real comparison.** *Sīcut dīxī* "as I said" compares with a fact: indicative. Only "as if" clauses imagine, and take the subjunctive.

**Do not translate *etsī* always as "although".** It is "even if" when its clause is a supposition (*etiam sī vēnerit*) and "although" when it states a fact (*etsī aberat*).

**Do not leave out *tamen* when you write.** It is not required, but Latin writers very often mark the main clause of a concession with it, and it makes your meaning plain.

## Connections

Spanish *aunque* takes the indicative for a fact and the subjunctive for a supposition (*aunque llueve*, *aunque llueva*), a single word doing the work of Latin *quamquam* and *quamvīs*. Spanish *como si* "as if" always takes the past subjunctive, *como si fuera rey*, where Latin follows the sequence. Japanese has one main concessive ending, *-temo* or *-noni*, and marks "as if" with *yō ni* or *mitai ni* after a plain clause.

## Exercises

1. Say "although the city is large, it is not safe".
2. Say "however rich he may be, he is not happy".
3. Say "even if you had come, you would not have seen him".
4. Say "I shall stay, provided that you do not leave".
5. Say "he spoke as if he had seen everything", after a past main verb.
6. Translate *Licet omnēs mē relinquant, tamen nōn timēbō.*
7. Translate *Quamquam quid loquor?* and explain *quamquam*.
8. Correct *\*Quamquam fessus esset, labōrāvit* and *\*Dummodo nōn veniat*.
9. Correct *\*Loquitur quasi rēx esset* (Ciceronian prose) and *\*Licet venīret*.
10. Explain the mood of *dēsint* in *Ut dēsint vīrēs, tamen est laudanda voluntās*, and where the construction comes from.

## Worked answers

1. *Quamquam urbs magna est, tamen tūta nōn est.* A conceded fact: *quamquam* + indicative (ag1903 §527d).
2. *Quamvīs dīves sit, nōn est fēlīx* (or *beātus*). *Quamvīs* + subjunctive, primary sequence (ag1903 §527a).
3. *Etiam sī vēnissēs, eum nōn vīdissēs.* *Etiam sī* follows *sī*: past unreal, pluperfect subjunctive in both (ag1903 §527c; lesson 13).
4. *Manēbō, dummodo nē discēdās.* Proviso with *dummodo* + subjunctive; the negative is *nē* (ag1903 §528).
5. *Locūtus est quasi omnia vīdisset.* After a secondary verb, an earlier action: pluperfect subjunctive (ag1903 §524; lesson 6).
6. "Although everyone should desert me, I shall still not be afraid." *Licet* + present subjunctive, "granted that all leave me" (ag1903 §527b).
7. "And yet, why am I talking?" Opening a sentence, *quamquam* corrects what went before: "and yet" (ag1903 §527d note).
8. *Quamquam fessus erat, labōrāvit*: *quamquam* takes the indicative in classical prose (ag1903 §527d). *Dummodo nē veniat*: the negative of a proviso is *nē* (§528).
9. *Loquitur quasi rēx sit*: Cicero keeps the sequence with *quasi* (ag1903 §524a note). *Licet veniat* (or *vēnerit*): *licet* is present, so only present or perfect (§527b note).
10. Subjunctive after concessive *ut* "granting that" (ag1903 §527a). Allen and Greenough call the origin of this *ut* uncertain (§527a note), unlike *quamvīs*, whose subjunctive goes back to a command.
