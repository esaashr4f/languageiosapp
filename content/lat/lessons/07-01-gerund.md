## The idea

English can turn any verb into a noun with *-ing*: "the art of *writing*", "by *reading*", "ready for *fighting*". The noun can then stand wherever a noun stands: after a preposition, as a possessor, as a means.

Latin already has one verbal noun, the infinitive (part 4). *Scrībere est ūtile* "writing is useful": *scrībere* is the subject. But the infinitive does not decline. It can be a subject, and it can be the object of verbs like *possum*, *volō*, *discō*, and that is all. It cannot follow a preposition, it cannot be a genitive, and it cannot be an ablative of means.

The gap is filled by the **gerund**: a verbal noun that has the other cases. A **gerund** is a noun made from a verb, meaning "the act of doing", declined in the genitive, dative, accusative and ablative singular. *Scrībendī* "of writing", *scrībendō* "for writing" or "by writing", *ad scrībendum* "for writing". The infinitive supplies the nominative, and the gerund supplies the rest (ag1903 §§159a, 502 note).

The gerund is still a verb on the inside. It can have an adverb (*bene disserendī* "of discussing well") and it can have an object in the case its verb takes (*salūtem dandō* "by giving safety"). Outside, it is a noun, and some other word governs its case.

Allen and Greenough explain the gerund as the neuter of an adjective, the gerundive, used as a noun (ag1903 §501). The gerundive (lesson 2) is probably the original construction (ag1903 §503, footnote). How the *-nd-* suffix itself arose is not explained here.

## How it is built

1. Take the present stem, the same stem the present participle uses (part 5): *ama-*, *mone-*, *reg-e-*, *capi-e-*, *audi-e-*.
2. Add *-nd-*. In the third and fourth conjugations a linking *e* comes first: *amand-*, *monend-*, *regend-*, *capiend-*, *audiend-*.
3. Add the endings of the second-declension neuter singular, without the nominative: genitive *-ī*, dative and ablative *-ō*, accusative *-um*.

So *amandī, amandō, amandum, amandō*; *regendī, regendō, regendum, regendō*. The irregular verbs follow their present stems: *eō* gives *eundī*, *ferō* *ferendī*, *dō* *dandī* with short *a* (Wiktionary). *Sum*, *possum* and *volō* have no gerund (Wiktionary).

The gerund is built on the present stem, not the supine. So verbs with no supine still have one: *discō* gives *discendī* "of learning", *timeō* *timendī*.

Deponents have an active gerund, like their active forms: *loquendī* "of speaking", *sequendī* "of following", *pābulandī* "of foraging".

Older and legal Latin often has *-und-* in the third and fourth conjugations: *faciundī*, and in the gerundive *dēdūcundīs* (ag1903 §179, footnote; §505b). Read it as *-end-*.

**Choosing the case.** Ask what the noun "doing" is doing in the sentence.

Say "the art of writing". "Of writing" depends on "art": genitive, *ars scrībendī*.

Say "they went out to play". Purpose after a verb of motion: *ad* + accusative, *ad lūdendum*.

Say "we learn by reading". Means: ablative, *legendō discimus*.

Say "they were thinking about escaping". "About" is *dē* + ablative: *dē fugiendō*.

Say "he went out for the sake of foraging". *Causā* "for the sake of" follows a genitive: *pābulandī causā*.

## Where it applies

- **Genitive after a noun**: *ars scrībendī*; *cōnsilium fugiendī* "a plan of escaping"; *occāsiō pugnandī* "a chance to fight"; *facultās dīcendī* "the ability to speak". Allen and Greenough quote Cicero, *vīvendī est fīnis optimus* "the best end of living is …" (On Old Age 72; ag1903 §504 prints the words in the order *vīvendī fīnis est optimus*).
- **Genitive after an adjective**: *cupidus vincendī*, *perītus nāvigandī* "skilled in sailing" (part 5, lesson 9).
- **Genitive + *causā* or *grātiā***, for purpose: *pābulandī causā*, *simulandī grātiā* "in order to deceive" (Sallust, quoted in ag1903 §504b). *Pābulandī causā* is also attested in Caesar, Civil War 1.80. This is one of the ways to say purpose promised in part 6, lesson 7.
- **Accusative after *ad***, for purpose: *ad scrībendum*, *ad lūdendum*. Rarely after *inter* "while": *inter agendum* "while you are driving them" (Vergil, Eclogues 9.24).
- **Ablative of means or cause**: *legendō discimus*; *cūnctandō restituit rem*.
- **Ablative after *in*, *dē*, *ex*, *ab***: *in legendō* "in reading, while reading"; *dē fugiendō* (ag1903 §507).
- **Ablative after a comparative**, as the thing compared (ag1903 §507): *nihil est melius legendō* "nothing is better than reading".
- **Dative**: rare, in a few phrases after verbs and adjectives of fitness: *solvendō esse* "to be able to pay", literally "to be for paying" (ag1903 §505). Livy uses it more than Cicero.
- **In apposition to a noun**: Livy calls obeying and commanding *rēs dīversissimās, pārendum atque imperandum* "the most opposite things, obeying and commanding" (21.4, quoted in ag1903 §503 note 2).
- **With an object**: in the genitive and the ablative without a preposition the gerund can take an accusative object, especially a neuter pronoun or adjective (*vēra ac falsa dīiūdicandī*). With a noun object, Latin prefers the gerundive construction of lesson 2 (ag1903 §503 note 1).
- **Objects in other cases** are always fine: *hominibus dandō* (dative), *mihi pārendum* (dative, with *pāreō*).

## Watch out

**Do not use the gerund as a subject.** *\*Scrībendum est ūtile* for "writing is useful" fails: the gerund has no nominative. Worse, *scrībendum est* means "it must be written" (lesson 2). The subject is the infinitive: *scrībere est ūtile*.

**Do not use the gerund as the object of *volō*, *possum*, *discō*.** *\*Discō natandum* fails; the complementary infinitive does this job: *discō natāre* "I am learning to swim" (part 4). The accusative of the gerund appears only after a preposition.

**Do not put an infinitive after a preposition.** *\*Ad lūdere*, by analogy with Spanish *para jugar* or French *pour jouer*, is impossible. Latin says *ad lūdendum*. The same goes for "by", "about", "of": *\*dē fugere* is wrong; *dē fugiendō* is right.

**Do not translate the gerund as passive.** *Amandī* is "of loving", never "of being loved". The gerund is active, even for deponents: *loquendī* "of speaking".

**Do not use the ablative gerund for "while doing" with a subject.** In classical Latin, "she sat weeping" is *flēns sēdit*, with the present participle (part 5), because the action describes a person. Medieval Latin uses the ablative gerund here, *flendō sēdit*, and that is the origin of the Spanish and Italian gerund (ag1903 §507, footnote 5). In Cicero, *legendō* answers "by what means?", not "in what state?".

**Do not build the gerund on the supine stem.** *\*Captandī* (from *captum*) and *\*vīsendī* are not gerunds of *capiō* and *videō* (though *captō* and *vīsō* are separate verbs with their own gerunds). The gerund comes from the present: *capiendī*, *videndī*.

**Do not forget the linking *e* in the third and fourth conjugations.** *\*Regndī*, *\*audindī* are impossible. *Regendī*, *audiendī*, *capiendī*.

**Do not read *-ndō* as a first-person verb.** *Dandō* is not "I give" (*dō*). The *-nd-* marks the gerund; first-person verbs end in a bare *-ō* after the stem.

**Do not set the genitive after *causā*.** *\*Causā pābulandī* is not the classical order; *causā* and *grātiā* follow their genitive: *pābulandī causā*.

## Connections

The Latin ablative gerund became the Spanish and Italian gerund: *cantando* is the Latin *cantandō* "by singing", which medieval writers had already begun to use for "while singing" (ag1903 §507, footnote 5). Spanish *estoy hablando* and *aprendo leyendo* "I learn by reading" keep both senses. English *-ing* covers both the Latin gerund and the present participle, which is why "reading" can be either.

## Exercises

1. Give the gerund (genitive and ablative) of *nāvigō*, *doceō*, *dīcō*, *fugiō*, *veniō* and *sequor*.
2. Say "the skill of speaking Latin".
3. Say "they came into the city to watch" using *ad* + a gerund, and say why *\*ad spectāre* is impossible.
4. Say "by fighting bravely they saved the town".
5. Translate *Philosophī dē bene vīvendō disserēbant.*
6. Translate *Nūlla erat facultās fugiendī.*
7. Correct *\*Natandum est ūtile* (meant as "swimming is useful") and *\*Volō lūdendum*.
8. In *Ūnus homō nōbīs cūnctandō restituit rem*, explain the case of *cūnctandō* and why the form is active although *cūnctor* is deponent.
9. Why does *discō*, which has no supine, still have a gerund?

## Worked answers

1. *Nāvigandī, nāvigandō*; *docendī, docendō*; *dīcendī, dīcendō*; *fugiendī, fugiendō*; *veniendī, veniendō*; *sequendī, sequendō*. First and second conjugations add *-nd-* straight to the stem; third, third *-iō* and fourth add *-end-* (ag1903 §179). The deponent has an ordinary active gerund.
2. *Ars Latīnē loquendī.* The gerund depends on *ars*, so it is genitive; *Latīnē* is an adverb, which the gerund takes as a verb would.
3. *In urbem vēnērunt ad spectandum.* Purpose after a verb of motion: *ad* + the accusative of the gerund. A preposition cannot govern an infinitive; the gerund supplies the accusative case the preposition needs (ag1903 §506).
4. *Fortiter pugnandō oppidum servāvērunt.* Ablative of means; *fortiter* is an adverb modifying the gerund (ag1903 §507).
5. "The philosophers were discussing how to live well" (literally "about living well"). *Dē* + ablative gerund; *bene* modifies it.
6. "There was no opportunity of escaping." The gerund in the genitive depends on *facultās* (ag1903 §504).
7. *Natāre est ūtile*: the subject must be the infinitive, and *natandum est* would mean "one must swim" (lesson 2). *Volō lūdere*: after *volō* the complementary infinitive, not the gerund (part 4).
8. Ablative of means: "by delaying" (ag1903 §507). Deponents have passive endings but active meanings; their gerund, like their present participle, is active in form and meaning: *cūnctandō*, not a passive form.
9. The gerund is built on the present stem (*disc-e-*), which every verb has. Only the perfect participle, the future participle and the supine need the fourth principal part.
