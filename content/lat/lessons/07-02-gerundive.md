## The idea

Lesson 1 built a noun from the present stem with *-nd-*. Put the endings of *bonus, -a, -um* on the same stem and you get an adjective: *scrībendus, -a, -um*. This is the **gerundive**: a passive verbal adjective that means "to be done", "needing to be done". *Epistula scrībenda* is "a letter to be written". You met one as a preview in part 6: Ovid's *laudanda voluntās* "the will deserves praise".

The gerundive has two jobs, and they look quite different in English.

**Job 1: obligation.** With a form of *sum*, the gerundive says what must or ought to be done: *epistula scrībenda est* "the letter is to be written", "the letter must be written". Allen and Greenough call this the second (or passive) periphrastic conjugation, the partner of *scrīptūrus est* "is about to write" (part 5; ag1903 §§194, 196). The person who has to do it goes in the dative: *mihi epistula scrībenda est* "the letter is to be written by me", that is, "I must write the letter". This is the **dative of the agent**: the dative naming the person on whom a necessity rests (ag1903 §374).

**Job 2: the gerund with an object.** "For seeking peace" could be *ad petendum pācem*, gerund + object. Latin prefers to make the noun the head and the gerundive its adjective: *ad pācem petendam*, "for peace to-be-sought". The meaning is the same and English translates both as "for seeking peace" (ag1903 §503). Allen and Greenough think this gerundive construction is the original one and the gerund grew out of it (§503, footnote).

The two jobs share one idea: the gerundive describes something as not yet done but in prospect. "A city to be taken" can be a duty (*urbs capienda est*) or the aim of an action (*ad urbem capiendam*).

## How it is built

**The form.** Present stem + *-nd-* (with a linking *e* in the third and fourth conjugations) + *-us, -a, -um*: *amandus*, *monendus*, *regendus*, *capiendus*, *audiendus*. It declines and agrees like *bonus*. Deponents have one too, and it is passive like every gerundive: *hortandus* "to be encouraged", *sequendus* "to be followed" (ag1903 §190d).

**Obligation, personal.** Say "the city must be destroyed".

1. The thing that must be acted on is the subject: *urbs*, feminine singular nominative.
2. The verb is *dēleō*; the gerundive agrees with *urbs*: *dēlenda*.
3. Present obligation: *est*. *Urbs dēlenda est.*
4. Add "by us" as a dative: *Nōbīs urbs dēlenda est* "We must destroy the city."

Change the tense by changing *sum*: *dēlenda erat* "had to be destroyed", *dēlenda erit* "will have to be destroyed". Report it with *esse*: *dīcit urbem dēlendam esse* "he says the city must be destroyed".

**Obligation, impersonal.** Say "we must go". *Eō* has no object to be the subject. Use the neuter singular with *est*, and no subject at all: *eundum est* "there must be going", with the dative of the agent: *nōbīs eundum est*. The same goes for verbs with a dative or ablative object, which keep that case: *lēgibus pārendum est* "the laws must be obeyed" (ag1903 §500.3).

**Gerundive for gerund.** Say "they sent envoys to seek peace".

1. "For seeking peace": the gerund phrase would be *ad petendum pācem*.
2. Make *pācem* the head, keeping the case *ad* needs: *ad pācem*.
3. Make the gerundive agree with it: *pāx* is feminine singular accusative, so *petendam*.
4. *Lēgātōs ad pācem petendam mīsērunt.*

With *causā*: the noun goes into the genitive and the gerundive agrees: *pācis petendae causā*.

## Where it applies

- **Personal obligation, transitive verbs**: *epistula scrībenda est*; *urbs dēlenda est*. Any tense of *sum*: *erant agenda*, *erit faciendum*.
- **Obligation in subordinate clauses**, with the subjunctive of *sum*: *nesciō quid faciendum sit* "I do not know what is to be done" (an indirect question, part 6).
- **Obligation in reported statements**, with *esse*: *dīxit pontem faciendum esse* "he said a bridge had to be built".
- **Impersonal obligation, intransitive verbs**: *eundum est*, *pugnandum est*, *nunc est bibendum*.
- **Impersonal obligation, verbs with a dative or ablative object**, which keep their case: *temporī serviendum est*; *lēgibus pārendum est*; *ūtendum est exercitātiōnibus modicīs* "we must use moderate exercise" (Cicero, quoted in ag1903 §500.3).
- **Agent in the dative**: *mihi*, *nōbīs*, *Caesarī*. When the verb already has a dative and two datives would be unclear, Cicero uses *ā* + ablative: *quibus est ā vōbīs cōnsulendum* "for whom you must take thought" (ag1903 §374a note 1).
- **No agent at all**: the obligation is general, "one must": *temporī serviendum est*.
- **Gerundive for gerund + object, in every case**:
  - genitive after nouns and adjectives: *cōnsilium urbis capiendae* "a plan of taking the city" (ag1903 §503a);
  - genitive + *causā* or *grātiā* for purpose: *vītandae suspīciōnis causā* "to avoid suspicion" (Cicero, Against Catiline 1.19, quoted in ag1903 §504b);
  - dative, in a few set phrases: *triumvirī reī pūblicae cōnstituendae* "the board of three for settling the state", the official title of the triumvirs (ag1903 §505b);
  - accusative after *ad*: *ad pācem petendam*, *ad omnia perīcula subeunda*;
  - ablative of means and after *in*, *dē*: *in rē gerendā* "in managing affairs" (Cicero, quoted in ag1903 §507).
- **With verbs of giving, entrusting, undertaking, having, caring for**: the gerundive agrees with the object: *pontem faciendum cūrat*; *aedem Castoris habuit tuendam* "he had the temple of Castor to look after" (Cicero, quoted in ag1903 §500.4). Allen and Greenough list *cūrō*, *dō*, *trādō*, *relinquō*, *suscipiō*, *locō*, *mandō*, *praebeō* and others.
- **As a plain adjective**, "worth doing", "fit to be done": *iniūria nōn ferenda* "an intolerable wrong" (Cicero, quoted in ag1903 §500.1).
- **With *ūtor*, *fruor* in the gerundive phrase**: here they behave as if they took the accusative: *ad perfruendās voluptātēs* "for enjoying pleasures" (ag1903 §503 note 2).

## Watch out

**Do not put the subject in the accusative.** "I must write the letter" tempts English speakers to make the letter an object: *\*Mihi epistulam scrībendam est*. The gerundive is passive; the letter is its subject: *mihi epistula scrībenda est*.

**Do not use *ā* + ablative for the agent by default.** *\*Ā mē epistula scrībenda est* is not the normal form. With the gerundive the agent is a dative: *mihi* (ag1903 §374). *Ā* is a special case, for avoiding two datives.

**Do not make intransitive verbs personal.** *\*Nōs eundī sumus* for "we must go" fails: *eō* has no passive subject. Use the impersonal neuter: *nōbīs eundum est*.

**Do not make dative verbs personal.** *\*Lēgēs pārendae sunt*, by analogy with English "the laws must be obeyed", treats *pāreō* as transitive. It takes the dative, so: *lēgibus pārendum est*.

**Do not read the gerundive as a future passive participle "about to be".** *Urbs capienda est* is not "the city is about to be taken"; it is "the city must be taken". "About to be taken" is said with the future: *urbs mox capiētur*.

**Do not read *nōn* + gerundive as "need not".** *Nōn est faciendum* means "it must not be done", "it ought not to be done". For "it need not be done", say *nōn necesse est id facere* (part 6).

**Do not use a gerund with a noun object after a preposition.** *\*Ad capiendum urbem* is "inadmissible" in Allen and Greenough's word (§503); classical Latin says *ad urbem capiendam*.

**Do not confuse the gerund and the gerundive in the neuter.** *Ad legendum* can be the gerund ("for reading"); *ad librum legendum* has a gerundive agreeing with *librum*. Look for a noun the *-nd-* form agrees with.

**Do not give the deponent gerundive an active meaning.** *Hortandus* is "to be urged", not "urging". "Urging" is *hortāns*, and "of urging" is the gerund *hortandī*.

**Do not try to use *habeō* + infinitive for "I have to".** *\*Habeō epistulam scrībere* imitates Spanish *tengo que escribir* or English "I have to write". Classical Latin says *mihi epistula scrībenda est*. *Habeō* with a gerundive does exist, but means "have something to do": *aedem habuit tuendam*.

## Connections

English has borrowed gerundives as nouns: an *agenda* is "things to be done", a *memorandum* "a thing to be remembered", an *addendum* "a thing to be added". Spanish *hay que* and French *il faut* do the work of the impersonal gerundive: *nōbīs eundum est*, *hay que irse*, *il faut partir*.

## Exercises

1. Give the gerundive (masculine nominative singular) of *laudō*, *videō*, *mittō*, *faciō*, *audiō* and *sequor*.
2. Say "the walls must be defended by the soldiers".
3. Say "you (singular) must obey your father".
4. Say "we had to fight".
5. Say "he came to see the city", with the gerundive and *ad*, and again with *causā*.
6. Translate *Caesarī omnia ūnō tempore erant agenda.* Who has to act, and how do you know?
7. Translate *Dīxit pontem faciendum esse.*
8. Correct *\*Nōbīs epistulās scrībendum est* and *\*Lēgēs pārendae sunt*.
9. Translate *Eī aedēs tuenda trādita est.*
10. Explain why *pācem petendam* is preferred to *petendum pācem* after *ad*.

## Worked answers

1. *Laudandus*, *videndus*, *mittendus*, *faciendus*, *audiendus*, *sequendus*. Present stem + *-nd-* (*-end-* in the third and fourth conjugations) + *-us* (ag1903 §179).
2. *Mīlitibus mūrī dēfendendī sunt.* *Mūrī* is the subject, masculine plural, so *dēfendendī sunt*; the agent is dative (ag1903 §374).
3. *Tibi patrī pārendum est.* *Pāreō* takes the dative, so the gerundive is impersonal, and *patrī* stays dative. Two datives here are clear in context; if not, *ā tē* could be used (ag1903 §374a note 1).
4. *Nōbīs pugnandum erat.* Intransitive, so impersonal; past obligation, so *erat*.
5. *Vēnit ad urbem videndam* and *Vēnit urbis videndae causā.* The noun takes the case the construction needs (accusative after *ad*, genitive before *causā*), and the gerundive agrees with it (ag1903 §§503, 504b, 506).
6. "Caesar had to do everything at the same time." *Caesarī* is the dative of the agent; *omnia … agenda* (neuter plural) is the subject; *erant* puts the obligation in the past.
7. "He said that a bridge had to be built." A reported statement: the subject *pontem* is accusative, and the gerundive with *esse* is the infinitive of obligation (part 6, lesson 5).
8. *Nōbīs epistulae scrībendae sunt*: the letters are the subject, so nominative plural with a plural gerundive. *Lēgibus pārendum est*: *pāreō* takes the dative, so the construction is impersonal (ag1903 §500.3).
9. "The temple was handed over to him to look after." After *trādō*, the gerundive agrees with the object to give the purpose (ag1903 §500.4); here the object has become the subject of the passive *trādita est*, and *tuenda* agrees with *aedēs*.
10. A gerund after a preposition does not take a direct object in classical Latin (ag1903 §506 note 2). The noun is made the head instead, and the gerundive agrees with it (§503).
