## The idea

Since part 4 you have learned every verb with a fourth principal part: *amātum*, *captum*, *vīsum*. It was called the supine, and it has worked only as a stem, for the perfect participle and the future participle. In part 6 it appeared once as a form in its own right, in the future passive infinitive *captum īrī*. This lesson explains what it is.

The **supine** is a verbal noun of the fourth declension, the declension of *cursus* and *exitus* (part 3), that survives in only two cases (ag1903 §508):

- an accusative in *-um*: *spectātum*, *rogātum*, *dictum*;
- a dative or ablative in *-ū*: *dictū*, *vīsū*, *factū*.

It has no tense, no person and no other cases. That is why it is called a fossil: it is the remains of a noun whose other forms have gone out of use.

Each survivor has kept one job, and the job follows from its case.

**The *-um* supine** is an accusative of the end of motion, the same accusative as *Rōmam* in *Rōmam eō* "I go to Rome" (part 3). Towns take no preposition, and neither does the supine (ag1903 §§428i, 509). So *spectātum veniunt* is "they come to the watching", "they come to watch". The supine says what you are going in order to do. Allen and Greenough list a few other old accusatives of motion without a preposition: *vēnum dare* "give to sale", which became *vendere* "sell" (ag1903 §428i).

**The *-ū* supine** goes with adjectives and says in what respect the adjective holds: *mīrābile dictū* "wonderful to tell", literally "wonderful in the telling" (ag1903 §510). It looks like an ablative of respect (part 5), though Allen and Greenough think it was in origin a dative of purpose (§508). This guide glosses it SUP.ABL.

## How it is built

The supine is the fourth principal part as it stands, or with *-ū* in place of *-um*.

1. Find the fourth principal part: *spectō, spectāre, spectāvī, spectātum*.
2. For purpose after a verb of motion, use it unchanged: *spectātum*.
3. For "to do" after an adjective, replace *-um* with *-ū*: *dictum* gives *dictū*, *vīsum* gives *vīsū*.

It never agrees with anything. *Mittunt rogātum*, *mittit rogātum*, *mīsērunt rogātum*: the form does not change.

The *-um* supine can take an object in the case its verb takes: *rogātum auxilium* "to ask for help", *questum iniūriās* "to complain of wrongs" (ag1903 §509). The *-ū* supine never takes an object (ag1903 §510, footnote).

Say "they sent envoys to Caesar to ask for help".

1. A verb of motion: *mittunt* "they send". Its purpose: "to ask".
2. *Rogō, rogātum*: the *-um* supine, *rogātum*.
3. Its object, *auxilium*, accusative.
4. *Lēgātōs ad Caesarem mittunt rogātum auxilium.* That is Caesar's own sentence.

Say "a thing shameful to say". An adjective, and a respect in which it holds: *rēs turpis dictū*.

## Where it applies

**The *-um* supine**

- **After verbs of motion**, to state the purpose: *eō*, *veniō*, *mittō*, *discēdō*, *proficīscor*: *spectātum veniunt*; *mittunt rogātum*.
- **With an object of its own**: *rogātum auxilium*, *questum iniūriās*.
- **In set phrases**: *cubitum īre* "to go to bed" (Cicero, On the State 6.10: *ut cubitum discessimus* "when we had gone off to bed"); *nūptum dare* "to give (a woman) in marriage" (ag1903 §509).
- **With *eō* in a loose sense, "set about"**: Sallust says some citizens *rem pūblicam perditum īrent* "went about ruining the state" (Catiline 36.4, quoted in ag1903 §509 note 1).
- **In the future passive infinitive**, with *īrī*: *urbem captum īrī*. Allen and Greenough call this rare except in Cicero; *fore ut* + subjunctive is the commoner way (part 6, lesson 9; ag1903 §509 note 1).
- **Where motion is only implied**, occasionally (ag1903 §509 note 2).

**The *-ū* supine**

- **With adjectives of the senses and feelings**: *mīrābile dictū*, *horrendum dictū*, *turpe dictū*, *vīsū foedus* "foul to see".
- **With adjectives of ease and difficulty**: *facilis vīsū*, *difficilis dictū*, *incrēdibile memorātū* "incredible to relate". With *facilis* and *difficilis*, however, *ad* + gerund is commoner in prose: *difficilis ad distinguendum* (ag1903 §510 note 2).
- **With *optimum*, *fās*, *nefās* and *opus***: *quid optimum factū sit*; *fās est dictū*; *nefās est dictū* "it is wicked to say" (Cicero, On Old Age 13).
- **In *maior nātū*, *minimus nātū*.**
- **Only a few verbs**: the common ones are *audītū*, *dictū*, *factū*, *inventū*, *memorātū*, *nātū* and *vīsū* (ag1903 §510, footnote).

## Watch out

**Do not use the infinitive for purpose after a verb of motion in prose.** *\*Vēnērunt spectāre* imitates English "they came to watch" and Spanish *vinieron a ver*. Classical prose uses the supine (*spectātum*), *ad* + gerund (*ad spectandum*) or *ut* + subjunctive (*ut spectārent*). Poets sometimes use the infinitive of purpose (ag1903 §460c).

**Do not make the supine agree.** *\*Urbem captam īrī* treats *captum* as a participle. The supine is a noun and never changes: *urbem captum īrī*, *mūrōs captum īrī*.

**Do not put a preposition before the supine.** *\*Ad spectātum vēnērunt* mixes two constructions. The supine is an accusative of motion without a preposition (ag1903 §428i); *ad* takes the gerund: *ad spectandum*.

**Do not use the supine after verbs that are not of motion.** *\*Rogāvit pācem petītum* fails: asking is not going. Say *rogāvit ut pācem peterent* (part 6, lesson 10).

**Do not read *-ū* as a participle.** *Facile dictū* "easy to say" is not *facile dictum* "a thing easily said". The *-ū* form belongs to the adjective; *dictum* would be a neuter participle agreeing with something.

**Do not give the *-ū* supine an object.** *\*Facile dictū rem* is impossible (ag1903 §510, footnote). Use the infinitive or a clause: *rem dīcere facile est*.

**Do not coin *-ū* supines freely.** *\*Scrīptū facilis*, *\*cantātū difficilis* are hardly found. Outside the common few, say *ad scrībendum facilis* or *facile est scrībere*.

**Do not confuse the *-um* supine with the perfect participle or the gerund.** *Spectātum* can be the supine "to watch", the neuter participle "watched", or the accusative masculine "watched (man)". *Spectandum* is gerund or gerundive. After a verb of motion with no noun to agree with, *spectātum* is the supine.

**Do not look for a supine where there is no fourth principal part.** *Timeō* and *discō* have none (part 4); they cannot say *\*timitum*. Use *ad* + gerund: *ad discendum vēnit*.

## Connections

Spanish and French dropped the supine, and use *a* or *pour* + infinitive (*vinieron a ver*, *ils sont venus voir*). Its stem survives everywhere in English: *visit* comes, through French, from *vīsitāre*, a verb built on the stem of *vīsum*, and *monster* is *mōnstrum*. Japanese has a close parallel to the *-um* supine: in *mi ni iku* "go to see", the verb's stem form works as a noun and takes *ni*, the particle of direction, as *spectātum īre* puts a verbal noun in the accusative of direction.

## Exercises

1. Give the *-um* supine of *videō*, *audiō*, *capiō*, *dīcō* and *pōtō*.
2. Say "they came to see the city" with the supine, and then two other ways.
3. Say "a thing incredible to hear".
4. Translate *Lēgātōs mīsērunt questum iniūriās.*
5. Translate *Hoc nefās est dictū.*
6. Correct *\*Lēgātōs mīsērunt ad rogātum auxilium* and *\*Spērat urbem captam īrī*.
7. In *Spectātum veniunt, veniunt spectentur ut ipsae*, name the two ways purpose is expressed.
8. Why does the *-um* supine take no preposition after a verb of motion?
9. Why can *timeō* not form a supine of purpose, and what do you say instead?

## Worked answers

1. *Vīsum*, *audītum*, *captum*, *dictum*, *pōtum* (or *pōtātum*). The supine is the fourth principal part.
2. *Urbem vīsum vēnērunt*; *ad urbem videndam vēnērunt* (gerundive, lesson 2); *vēnērunt ut urbem vidērent* (part 6, lesson 7). The supine keeps *urbem* as its own object (ag1903 §509).
3. *Rēs incrēdibilis audītū*. The *-ū* supine limits the adjective: "incredible in the hearing" (ag1903 §510).
4. "They sent envoys to complain of the wrongs." *Mittō* is a verb of motion in Latin's sense (it sends someone somewhere), and the supine of the deponent *queror* takes an accusative object.
5. "This is wicked to say." *Nefās* takes the *-ū* supine (ag1903 §510).
6. *Lēgātōs mīsērunt rogātum auxilium*: the supine takes no preposition (ag1903 §428i). *Spērat urbem captum īrī*: the supine in the future passive infinitive never agrees (part 6, lesson 5).
7. First a supine after *veniunt*, then an *ut* clause with the present subjunctive passive *spectentur*, in primary sequence.
8. Because it is an accusative of the end of motion, like *Rōmam* and *domum*, which also go without a preposition (ag1903 §§428i, 509).
9. *Timeō* has no fourth principal part, so no supine. Use the gerund (*ad timendum*) or a clause.
