## The idea

Some statements have no doer. Rain falls, but nobody rains. Shame is felt, but the shame does the acting. A thing is allowed, but who allows it does not matter. English fills the empty subject slot with "it": "it rains", "it is allowed". Latin never needed a word for "it", because the verb ending already carries the person (part 2). So Latin says *pluit*, one word, third person singular, with no subject at all.

An **impersonal verb** is a verb used only in the third person singular (and the infinitive), with no personal subject (ag1903 §207). Allen and Greenough sort them into four groups (§208), and each group has its own logic.

1. **Weather and nature**: *pluit* "it rains", *tonat* "it thunders", *ningit* "it snows". Nobody is thought of as the subject. A poet can still say *Iuppiter tonat* "Jupiter thunders" (§208a note).
2. **Feelings**: *pudet* "it shames", *paenitet* "it makes sorry", *piget* "it irks", *taedet* "it wearies", *miseret* "it moves to pity". The person who feels is in the accusative, as the one affected, and the cause of the feeling is in the genitive: *mē pudet huius reī* "it shames me of this thing", "I am ashamed of this" (§§208b, 354b). The feeling acts on you; you do not act.
3. **Verbs whose subject is a whole infinitive or clause**: *licet* "it is allowed", *oportet* "it is right, one ought", *decet* "it is fitting", *libet* and *placet* "it pleases", *necesse est* "it is necessary", *cōnstat* "it is agreed", *accidit* "it happens", *interest* and *rēfert* "it matters". Here there is a subject, but it is an action: *morī necesse est* "to die is necessary" (§§208c, 454).
4. **The impersonal passive of intransitive verbs**: *pugnātur* "there is fighting", *ītur* "there is going", *ventum est* "they came" (§208d). Part 4 met this briefly.

The fourth group needs a reason. How can "fight", which has no object, be passive? Allen and Greenough explain that the Latin passive began as a reflexive or middle voice, "the action accomplishing itself" (§208d note). *Pugnātur* is "fighting goes on". French *cela se fait* "that is done", with a reflexive, is the same idea. The impersonal passive names the action and leaves out the doers, because they are obvious, many, or unimportant.

## How it is built

**The forms.** Impersonals conjugate normally, in the third person singular: *licet, licēbat, licēbit, licuit, liceat, licēret*; infinitive *licēre*. The impersonal passive uses the third singular passive, and in the perfect system the neuter participle: *pugnātur, pugnābātur, pugnātum est, pugnātum erat*.

**Choosing the case of the person.** Each verb has its own case for the person concerned (ag1903 §455 note):

- dative with *licet*, *libet*, *placet*, *vidētur* "it seems good": *mihi licet* "I am allowed";
- accusative with *oportet*, *decet* and the verbs of feeling: *mē oportet* "I ought", *mē pudet* "I am ashamed";
- either with *necesse est*;
- genitive with *interest* and *rēfert*, but *meā*, *tuā*, *nostrā* for a personal pronoun: *Caesaris interest* "it matters to Caesar", *meā interest* "it matters to me" (§355).

Wiktionary derives *rēfert* from *rē*, the ablative of *rēs* "matter", + *fert*: that is why its *ē* is long, unlike *referō* "bring back". Why *meā* is an ablative feminine with both verbs is not explained here.

**Building with a feeling.** Say "I am ashamed of this business".

1. The feeling: *pudet*.
2. The person who feels: accusative, *mē*.
3. The cause: genitive, *huius negōtiī*.
4. *Mē huius negōtiī pudet.*

**Building with an infinitive subject.** Say "we are allowed to stay here".

1. *Licet*, with the person in the dative: *nōbīs licet*.
2. The action allowed is the subject: *hīc manēre*.
3. *Nōbīs hīc manēre licet.*

Say "you ought to do this". *Oportet* takes an accusative and infinitive: *tē hoc facere oportet*. It can also take the subjunctive without *ut*: *hoc faciās oportet* (part 6, lesson 10).

**Building an impersonal passive.** Say "there was long and fierce fighting". Intransitive *pugnō*, so the passive is impersonal: perfect *pugnātum est*. Add the adverbs: *diū atque ācriter pugnātum est*.

## Where it applies

**Weather**: *pluit*, *tonat*, *ningit*, *vesperāscit* "evening comes", *grandinat* "it hails" (ag1903 §208a).

**Feelings** (accusative of the person, genitive of the cause, §354b):

- *pudet*: *mē pudet tuī* "I am ashamed of you";
- *paenitet*: *mē paenitet cōnsiliī* "I regret the plan";
- *piget*, *taedet*: *mē vītae taedet* "I am weary of life";
- *miseret*: *mē miseret tuī* "I pity you"; the personal deponent *misereor* (part 5) says the same with a nominative;
- **the cause as an infinitive or clause**: *neque mē vīxisse paenitet* "nor do I regret having lived" (Cicero, On Old Age 84, quoted in ag1903 §454).

**Clauses and infinitives as subject** (§§208c, 454–455):

- *licet* + dative and infinitive: *nōbīs manēre licet*; + subjunctive without *ut*: *licet abeās* "you may go"; as "although", part 6, lesson 14;
- *oportet* + accusative and infinitive, or + subjunctive without *ut*;
- *decet* + accusative and infinitive: "it is particularly unbecoming for an orator to lose his temper" is Cicero's *ōrātōrem īrāscī minimē decet* (Tusculans 4.54, quoted in ag1903 §455);
- *libet*, *placet* + dative and infinitive; *placet* also for an official decision, "it was resolved";
- *vidētur*, *vīsum est* + dative: "it seems good to …", "… decides";
- *necesse est* + infinitive, with dative or accusative of the person, or + subjunctive: *necesse est morī*;
- *cōnstat*, *appāret* + accusative and infinitive (part 6, lesson 5);
- *accidit*, *ēvenit*, *contingit*, *fit* + *ut* clause of result (part 6, lesson 9): *accidit ut esset lūna plēna* "it happened that there was a full moon" (Caesar, Gallic War 4.29.1);
- *interest*, *rēfert* + genitive or *meā*, *tuā*; + an infinitive or a clause: *Clōdiī intererat Milōnem perīre* "it was in Clodius' interest that Milo should die" (Cicero, quoted in ag1903 §355);
- *restat ut*, *superest ut* "it remains that".

**Impersonal passive** (§208d):

- intransitive verbs: *pugnātur*, *ītur*, *ventum est*, *curritur*;
- verbs with a dative object, which keep it: *mihi parcitur* "I am spared" (part 4, lesson 11); *eī persuāsum est* "he was persuaded";
- in all tenses and as an infinitive: *concurrī*, *pugnātum esse*;
- in the gerundive of obligation: *eundum est*, *pugnandum est* (lesson 2).

**Personal *videor***. Besides impersonal *vidētur mihi* "it seems good to me", *videor* is used personally with a nominative and infinitive: *vidētur esse sapiēns* "he seems to be wise", *videor mihi vidēre* "I seem to myself to see". This is the usual way to say "seem".

## Watch out

**Do not supply a subject pronoun.** *\*Id pluit* and *\*Hoc licet manēre* imitate English "it". The impersonal stands alone: *pluit*; *manēre licet*.

**Do not make the person the subject.** *\*Ego pudeō* and *\*Ego oportet*, built on English "I am ashamed", "I ought", fail. *Pudet* and *oportet* take the person in the accusative: *mē pudet*, *mē oportet*. Spanish *me gusta*, where the thing liked is the subject and the person is indirect, is a better model.

**Do not give *licet* an accusative of the person.** *\*Mē licet manēre* is not the classical form; *licet* takes the dative: *mihi licet manēre* (ag1903 §455 note).

**Do not put the cause of a feeling in the accusative.** *\*Mē pudet hanc rem* fails: the cause is genitive, *huius reī*. A neuter pronoun can stand as the subject instead: *nōn tē haec pudent?* "doesn't this shame you?" (Terence, quoted in ag1903 §354c note).

**Do not use *meī* with *interest*.** *\*Meī interest* looks regular but is not used; say *meā interest* (ag1903 §355a).

**Do not make the impersonal passive plural.** *\*Ācriter pugnātī sunt* treats the fighters as the subject of a passive. The verb is intransitive and impersonal: *ācriter pugnātum est*, neuter singular.

**Do not add *ā* + ablative to name the doers of an impersonal passive.** *\*Ā Rōmānīs pugnātum est* is rare and clumsy. If the doers matter, use the active: *Rōmānī pugnāvērunt*.

**Do not confuse *licet* "it is allowed" with *licet* "although".** Both are the same verb (part 6, lesson 14). With an infinitive or a dative it means "is allowed"; with a subjunctive and *tamen* to follow, "granted that".

**Do not confuse *rēfert* "it matters" with *refert* "he brings back, reports".** The first has a long *ē* and an impersonal construction; the second is *referō*, an ordinary verb with a subject.

**Do not read *oportuit* as "it was right" alone.** *Tē hoc facere oportuit* means "you ought to have done this": Latin puts the past on *oportet*, English on the infinitive. The same holds for *licuit* "might have", *decuit*, *necesse fuit* (ag1903 §§455a, 486a).

## Connections

Spanish *me gusta*, *me duele* and French *il me faut* keep the Latin habit of putting the person affected in an object case. Spanish *llueve* and French *il pleut* continue *pluit*; French adds a subject pronoun *il* that Latin and Spanish do without. Korean and Japanese also leave weather verbs and many feeling statements without a subject (Japanese *ame ga furu* names the rain, but *hazukashii* "(I am) ashamed" needs no "I").

## Exercises

1. Say "it is thundering".
2. Say "I regret my words" with *paenitet*.
3. Say "the soldiers were allowed to go home".
4. Say "a wise man ought not to be afraid" with *oportet*.
5. Translate *Mē tuī miseret.*
6. Translate *Diū pugnātum est; tandem ad castra ventum est.*
7. Translate *Hoc meā nihil rēfert.*
8. Correct *\*Ego pudeō huius reī* and *\*Mē licet abīre*.
9. Correct *\*Ācriter pugnātī sunt* (meant as "there was fierce fighting").
10. Why can a verb with no object, like *pugnō*, have a passive at all?

## Worked answers

1. *Tonat.* Weather verbs take no subject (ag1903 §208a).
2. *Mē verbōrum meōrum paenitet.* The person affected is accusative, the cause genitive (ag1903 §354b).
3. *Mīlitibus domum īre licuit.* *Licet* takes the dative of the person and an infinitive; *domum* without a preposition (part 3). Past: *licuit*.
4. *Sapientem timēre nōn oportet.* *Oportet* takes the accusative and infinitive (ag1903 §455).
5. "I pity you." *Tuī* is the genitive of *tū*, the cause of the pity (ag1903 §354b).
6. "There was fighting for a long time; at last they reached the camp." Two impersonal perfect passives of intransitive verbs, *pugnātum est* and *ventum est* (ag1903 §208d).
7. "This does not matter to me at all." *Meā* is the feminine ablative of the possessive, used with *rēfert* and *interest* (ag1903 §355a); *nihil* is an adverbial accusative of degree.
8. *Mē huius reī pudet*: the person is accusative, the cause genitive. *Mihi licet abīre*: *licet* takes the dative (ag1903 §455 note).
9. *Ācriter pugnātum est.* The impersonal passive has no personal subject, so the participle is neuter singular.
10. Allen and Greenough explain that the Latin passive was in origin reflexive or middle, "the action accomplishing itself" (ag1903 §208d note). *Pugnātur* presents fighting as something that goes on, with no subject needed.
