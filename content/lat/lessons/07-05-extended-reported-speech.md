## The idea

Part 6 taught the pieces one at a time: a reported statement is an accusative and infinitive (lesson 5); a clause inside it goes into the subjunctive (lesson 6); a reported question is subjunctive (lesson 11); conditions inside reported speech are subjunctive (lesson 13). Caesar, Livy and Tacitus put these pieces together to report whole speeches, sometimes for pages, often with no verb of saying after the first line. This lesson shows how the pieces fit, so that you can follow such a speech sentence by sentence.

The Latin name is ***ōrātiō oblīqua*** "slanting speech", opposed to ***ōrātiō rēcta*** "straight speech", the speaker's own words (ag1903 §578). In English we keep a long report readable by repeating "he said that". Latin does not need to, because the moods themselves show what is reported. Every main statement is an infinitive; every subordinate clause and every command is a subjunctive. As long as you see infinitives and subjunctives with no indicative main verb, you are still inside the speech.

Allen and Greenough make two points about why it works this way (ag1903 §577). The accusative and infinitive in reported speech is a fairly late development, found in this form only in Latin and Greek. And the subjunctive in its subordinate clauses has no meaning of its own: it is there to mark the clauses as subordinate, as part of someone else's words.

## How it is built

Take a direct speech and change it clause by clause. Allen and Greenough set out Divico's speech to Caesar both ways (ag1903 §590); the examples below follow it.

1. **Main statements become accusative and infinitive.** *Ībunt Helvētiī* "the Helvetii will go" becomes *itūrōs Helvētiōs*. Caesar often omits *esse* with the future and perfect infinitives.
2. **Subordinate clauses become subjunctive**, with tenses by the sequence of the verb of saying, usually secondary: *ubi eōs tū cōnstitueris* "where you settle them" becomes *ubi eōs Caesar cōnstituisset* (ag1903 §§580, 585).
3. **Commands become subjunctive**, following the sequence; a negative command takes *nē*: *reminīscere* "remember!" becomes *reminīscerētur*; *nōlī dēspicere* "do not despise" becomes *nē dēspiceret* (ag1903 §588).
4. **Real questions become subjunctive**: *cūr venīs?* "why are you coming?" becomes *cūr venīret?* (§586).
5. **Rhetorical questions**, which expect no answer, become accusative and infinitive, like statements: *num possum?* "can I?" becomes *num sē posse?* (§586).
6. **Conditions**: the *sī* clause is subordinate, so subjunctive; the main clause is an infinitive. A contrary-to-fact main clause uses *-ūrum fuisse*; if the direct main clause was an indicative, as *nōn difficile fuit* "it would not have been hard", it becomes a perfect infinitive, *nōn fuisse difficile* (§589b).
7. **Persons shift.** "I", "we" and "my", meaning the speaker, become *sē* and *suus*. "You", meaning the person addressed, becomes *is*, *ille*, or the name: *tū* becomes *Caesar*. Words of here and now follow: *hic* tends to become *ille*, *nunc* becomes *tum*.

**The tense of the subjunctives** follows the verb of saying: after *dīxit* the imperfect and pluperfect. Writers sometimes switch to the present and perfect subjunctive to make a long report vivid, as if the speaker were heard now; Allen and Greenough call this ***repraesentātiō*** (ag1903 §585b note). Expect both in the same speech.

**Reading strategy.**

1. Find where the speech begins: a verb of saying, or a word implying one (*lēgātī*, *respondit*, *ōrābant*).
2. Translate each infinitive with "he said that …", each subjunctive main clause with "he told him to …" or "should", each subjunctive question as a question.
3. Read every *sē* and *suus* as the speaker unless the clause has its own subject it must refer to.
4. The speech ends where an indicative main verb returns.

## Where it applies

- **Speeches of envoys, generals and opponents** in Caesar, Sallust, Livy and Tacitus. Caesar reports almost every speech this way.
- **With no verb of saying** after the start, or none at all, when a word implies speech or thought. Livy writes that the name of the other consul was *invīsum* "hateful" to the people, and goes straight on with what they thought: *nimium Tarquiniōs rēgnō adsuēsse* "the Tarquins had grown too used to kingship" (Livy 2.2, quoted in ag1903 §580a).
- **Statements**: accusative and infinitive (part 6, lesson 5).
- **Subordinate clauses of every kind**: relative, causal, temporal, concessive, conditional: subjunctive (ag1903 §580).
- **Commands, exhortations and wishes**: subjunctive, negative *nē*, by sequence (ag1903 §588).
- **Real questions**: subjunctive; **rhetorical questions**: accusative and infinitive (ag1903 §586). Where the line between them falls is often the writer's choice (§586 note 1).
- **Deliberative questions** keep their subjunctive: *cūr aliquōs ex suīs āmitteret?* "why should he lose some of his men?" (Caesar, Civil War 1.72, quoted in ag1903 §587).
- **Conditions**: protasis subjunctive; apodosis infinitive; contrary to fact *-ūrum fuisse*, or *futūrum fuisse ut* + subjunctive when the verb is passive or has no supine (ag1903 §589b).
- **After *quam*** following a comparative, the infinitive continues: *sē prius occīsum īrī quam mē violātum īrī* "that he would be killed before I was harmed" (Cicero, quoted in ag1903 §583c).
- **Relative clauses equal to "and he"**: a connecting relative counts as a main clause and takes the infinitive (ag1903 §583b).
- **Thoughts as well as words**: reported speech covers what anyone thinks or perceives (§577), and clauses that give someone else's reason take the subjunctive even outside a full speech: Cicero writes that Paetus gave him *omnēs librōs quōs frāter suus relīquisset* "all the books which (as Paetus put it) his brother had left" (Letters to Atticus 2.1.12, quoted in ag1903 §592: informal reported speech).

## Watch out

**Do not leave the main verbs in the indicative.** *\*Dīxit Helvētiōs ībunt* mixes two systems. Every main statement in reported speech is an infinitive: *itūrōs (esse)*.

**Do not put subordinate clauses in the indicative**, as English and Spanish do: *\*sī pācem faciet* inside a report fails. Use the subjunctive by sequence: *sī pācem faceret*. Allen and Greenough allow the indicative for a clause the writer adds as his own fact, and occasionally where a fact is emphasised (ag1903 §583 and 583a).

**Do not keep the imperative.** *\*Dīxit: reminīscere* is direct speech. In reported speech the command is *reminīscerētur*; *nōlī* becomes *nē* + subjunctive (ag1903 §588 note 2).

**Do not keep the first and second persons.** *\*Dīxit ego ventūrum esse* fails twice over: the subject must be accusative and the speaker is *sē*. *Dīxit sē ventūrum esse.* "You" (the person addressed) becomes *is*, *ille* or a name.

**Do not read every *sē* as the subject of its own clause.** In reported speech *sē* and *suus* usually mean the speaker, even deep inside a subordinate clause; but they can also refer to the subject of that clause, as *sibi* in *quid sibi vellet*. Ask who is the speaker and who is the subject, and choose the reading that makes sense.

**Do not put rhetorical questions in the subjunctive by habit.** *Num … posse?* expects "no" and is a disguised statement; that is why it takes the infinitive.

**Do not translate the vivid present as present time.** When a report after *dīxit* switches to *velint*, *possint*, it is still past for you: translate "they wanted", "they could" (ag1903 §585b).

**Do not expect a contrary-to-fact apodosis to keep its subjunctive.** An indicative apodosis such as *nōn difficile fuit* becomes *nōn fuisse difficile*; *vēnisset* in a main clause becomes *ventūrum fuisse* (ag1903 §589b).

**Do not expect *inquit* to introduce reported speech.** *Inquit* introduces direct words only (ag1903 §579 note). A report in direct form after *inquit* is ordinary quotation.

## Connections

French and Spanish report speech with a conjunction and an ordinary finite verb (*il dit qu'ils iraient*, *dijo que irían*), so their moods do not mark a whole speech the way Latin's do. Their conditional *iraient*, *irían* shifts tense as Latin's subjunctive does. Japanese and Korean mark the end of reported words with a quotative particle (*to iu*, *-go hada*), where Latin marks every clause inside them.

## Exercises

1. Put into reported speech after *dīxit*: *Hostēs fugiunt.*
2. Put into reported speech after *dīxit*: *Venīte mēcum!* (said to soldiers)
3. Put into reported speech after *rogāvit*: *Cūr nōn venīs?*
4. Put into reported speech after *dīxit*: *Sī mē sequēminī, vincēmus.*
5. Translate *Respondit sē nōn esse ventūrum; nē exspectārent.*
6. In example 1, give the direct form of *itūrōs* and *cōnstituisset*, and say why *ubi eōs Caesar* replaces *ubi eōs tū*.
7. In example 3, who is meant by *suae* and who by *ipsōs*?
8. Why does *num … posse?* take the infinitive while *cūr … venīret?* takes the subjunctive?
9. Correct *\*Dīxit sē venīre sī pācem faciet* and *\*Dīxit: nōlī timēre* (meant as reported speech).
10. Put into reported speech after *dīxit*: *Nōn difficile fuit*, the main clause of a contrary-to-fact condition ("it would not have been hard"). What changes and why?

## Worked answers

1. *Dīxit hostēs fugere.* A main statement becomes accusative and infinitive; present infinitive for a simultaneous action (part 6, lesson 5).
2. *Dīxit sēcum venīrent.* A command becomes the subjunctive, imperfect by secondary sequence; *mēcum* "with me" becomes *sēcum*, the speaker (ag1903 §588). *Iussit eōs sēcum venīre* says the same as a plain command.
3. *Rogāvit cūr nōn venīret.* A real question becomes the subjunctive, imperfect after *rogāvit* (ag1903 §586; part 6, lesson 11).
4. *Dīxit, sī sē sequerentur, sē victūrōs esse.* The protasis is subordinate, so subjunctive (*sequerentur*, imperfect by sequence); the apodosis is an infinitive (*victūrōs esse*); "me" and "we" become *sē* (ag1903 §589).
5. "He replied that he would not come; they were not to wait." A statement (*sē ventūrum esse*) and a prohibition (*nē* + subjunctive) (ag1903 §588 note 2).
6. *Ībunt* (future: "they will go") and *cōnstitueris* (future perfect: "you will have settled"). Divico spoke to Caesar as "you"; reported, the addressee is named or becomes *is*.
7. *Suae* refers to Caesar, the subject of *tribueret*: "his own courage". *Ipsōs* means the Helvetii, the speakers, emphasised: "them themselves".
8. *Num … posse?* is rhetorical, expecting "no", so it is treated as a statement: accusative and infinitive. *Cūr … venīret?* is a real question and takes the subjunctive (ag1903 §586).
9. *Dīxit sē ventūrum esse sī pācem faceret*: the main verb is a future infinitive, and the *sī* clause is subjunctive by secondary sequence. *Dīxit nē timēret*: *nōlī* + infinitive becomes *nē* + subjunctive (ag1903 §588 note 2).
10. *Dīxit nōn fuisse difficile.* An indicative in the main clause of a contrary-to-fact condition becomes a perfect infinitive (ag1903 §589b); Caesar has exactly this in Gallic War 1.14.2. Every main statement in reported speech is an infinitive.
