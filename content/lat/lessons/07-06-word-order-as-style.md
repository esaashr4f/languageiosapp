## The idea

Part 2 showed that Latin endings, not positions, say who does what, and that verb-final order is the neutral default of formal prose. This lesson turns the question round. If order does not carry grammar, what does it carry? Allen and Greenough's answer: importance. "In connected discourse the word most prominent in the speaker's mind comes first, and so on in order of prominence" (ag1903 §597). English shows prominence with the voice, by stressing a word. Latin shows it with position.

Four habits follow from that freedom, and you need all four to read Cicero, Livy or any poet.

1. **Emphatic positions.** The first place in a clause is the strongest. Words pulled forward out of their usual place are emphatic (§§597–598). A verb at the start, where it is not expected, stresses the action itself (§598d).
2. **Separation.** Words that belong together can be split, with other words between them. The general name is **hyperbaton**: the separation of words that belong together grammatically, such as an adjective and its noun. The endings let you rejoin them. Poets do it constantly; prose writers do it for emphasis.
3. **Patterns of pairs.** Two pairs can stand in the same order (*anaphora*, a-b a-b), in reverse order (*chiasmus*, a-b b-a, part 2), or interlocked (adjective-adjective-noun-noun, which Allen and Greenough call *synchysis*) (§598f, h).
4. **The period.** A **period** is a long sentence in which the subordinate clauses and phrases come first and the main verb is held back until the end, so that the sentence is complete only when it stops (§600). Latin can build such sentences because its endings show how the parts relate. English, which depends on position, mostly cannot.

Allen and Greenough also note a negative rule: "the more important word is never placed last for emphasis" (§597b). A word at the very end is either the expected verb or an afterthought.

## How it is built

**Reading a separated phrase.** Do not translate word by word. Collect.

1. When you meet an adjective with no noun beside it, note its gender, number and case: *saevae*, feminine genitive singular.
2. Keep reading until a noun matches: *Iūnōnis*.
3. Meanwhile, words in between often frame the phrase: *saevae memorem Iūnōnis ob īram* has the pair *memorem … īram* "unforgetting anger" wrapped round *saevae … Iūnōnis* "of cruel Juno".
4. Rejoin: *ob memorem īram saevae Iūnōnis* "because of the unforgetting anger of cruel Juno".

**Reading a period.** Hold the pieces until the main verb arrives.

1. Find the subject, usually first: *Caesar*.
2. Bracket each subordinate clause or ablative absolute: *(cum hostēs fugere vīdisset)*, *(quōs in silvā relīquerat)*.
3. Find the main verb at the end: *ēmīsit*.
4. Read the frame first ("Caesar sent out the cavalry"), then add the brackets in order ("when he had seen the enemy flee", "whom he had left in the wood").

**Building emphasis.** Say "it was the *city* the soldiers took" (not the camp). Put the contrasted word first: *Urbem mīlitēs cēpērunt.* Say "they *have* taken it" (it is done). Put the verb first: *Cēpērunt urbem mīlitēs.*

**Building a small hyperbaton.** A one-syllable preposition can sit between an adjective and its noun: *magnō cum metū* "with great fear", *quam ob rem* "for which reason", *omnibus cum cōpiīs* "with all his forces" (ag1903 §599d). The adjective comes first because it is the emphatic part.

## Where it applies

- **Topic first.** The subject of discussion opens the sentence, even when it is not the grammatical subject: Caesar begins the Gallic War with *Gallia* (ag1903 §597a).
- **Contrast first.** When two things are opposed, each opposed word comes first in its clause: *Cȳrus quidem haec moriēns; nōs … nostra videāmus* "Cyrus said this on his deathbed; let *us* consider our own case" (Cicero, On Old Age 82, quoted in ag1903 §597a).
- **Verb first** for emphasis on the act, its truth, or its tense: *facis amīcē*; *fuimus Trōēs* "we *were* Trojans" (part 4).
- ***Est* first** when it means "there is": *est virī magnī pūnīre sontīs* "it is the part of a great man to punish the guilty" (Cicero, quoted in ag1903 §598c).
- **Emphatic adjective first**, ordinary adjective after: *nōbilī genere*; *ingeniō malō* (ag1903 §598a). Words of number, quantity, demonstratives and question words usually precede (§598b).
- **Small hyperbaton in prose**: preposition between adjective and noun (*magnō cum metū*); a genitive or adverb inside a phrase (*dē commūnī hominum memoriā* "about the shared memory of mankind", §598g).
- **Large hyperbaton in poetry**: phrases split across a whole line (*multā … rosā*), interlocked pairs (*superiectō pavidae … aequore dammae*), and frames (*saevae memorem Iūnōnis ob īram*).
- **Chiasmus and anaphora** for antithesis: *suppliciō improbōs afficiunt, dēfendunt ac tuentur bonōs* (ag1903 §598f).
- **Relative clause first**, containing its antecedent: *quōs āmīsimus cīvīs, eōs …* (§599e).
- **Fixed phrases** whose order no longer signals emphasis: *rēs pūblica*, *populus Rōmānus*, *senātus populusque Rōmānus* (§598k).
- **The period**: Livy, Cicero's speeches and Caesar's narrative; also the end of a period chosen for its rhythm of long and short syllables (§601f; lesson 8 introduces the long and short pattern).
- **Second-place words**: *enim*, *autem*, *vērō*, *quidem* never stand first, and *igitur* usually stands second (§599b), so they mark the emphatic word just before them: *Cȳrus quidem*.

## Watch out

**Do not translate in Latin order.** "What much slender you boy on rose" is not English. Collect the phrases by their endings first, then write English order.

**Do not join a word to the nearest noun.** In *multā gracilis tē puer in rosā*, *multā* (ablative feminine) cannot go with *puer* or *tē*; it waits for *rosā*. Gender, number and case decide, never distance.

**Do not assume the last word is emphatic**, as in English "… and he did it *badly*". Latin keeps the last place for the verb or an afterthought (ag1903 §597b).

**Do not read a fronted object as the subject.** In *Urbem mīlitēs cēpērunt* the first word is accusative; the city did not take the soldiers. Spanish and French readers are used to the subject coming first; here the ending overrides position.

**Do not split phrases freely in your own prose.** Hyperbaton in Cicero is controlled and marks emphasis; in your sentences it will make the grammar unclear. Use the neutral order, and front only the word you mean to stress.

**Do not put *enim*, *autem* or *quidem* first.** *\*Enim Caesar vēnit* is wrong; *Caesar enim vēnit* (ag1903 §599b).

**Do not break up a fixed phrase for emphasis.** *\*Rōmānus populus* for *populus Rōmānus* is not the classical form (ag1903 §598k).

**Do not read every period as a list of separate sentences.** In *Caesar, cum …, equitēs, quōs …, ēmīsit*, the main clause is only *Caesar equitēs ēmīsit*. Everything else hangs on it.

## Connections

Spanish and French keep some Latin freedom for emphasis (Spanish *eso no lo sé*, French *cela, je ne le sais pas*), but they mark the moved object with a pronoun, because their nouns have no case endings to show it. Japanese and Korean share Latin's freedom for the same reason: the particles *o* and *ga* (or *eul* and *i*) do the work of endings, so *ringo o watashi ga tabeta* "it was the apple I ate" can front the object without confusion.

## Exercises

1. Rewrite *Mīlitēs urbem cēpērunt* to stress "took", and again to stress "the city".
2. Rejoin the phrases of *saevae memorem Iūnōnis ob īram* into prose order and translate.
3. In *Et superiectō pavidae natārunt aequore dammae*, which adjective goes with which noun, and how can you tell?
4. Mark the chiasmus in *(lēgēs) quae suppliciō improbōs afficiunt, dēfendunt ac tuentur bonōs.*
5. Translate *Magnā cum cūrā epistulam scrīpsit*, and say why *cum* stands where it does.
6. In the Horace example, list every split phrase.
7. Translate *Hostēs, quōs Caesar vīcerat, cum lēgātōs mīsissent, pācem petīvērunt*, and find the frame of the period.
8. Correct *\*Autem Caesar nōn vēnit* and *\*Rōmānus populus vīcit*.
9. Why can Latin move words so freely while English cannot?

## Worked answers

1. *Cēpērunt mīlitēs urbem* (verb first, ag1903 §598d). *Urbem mīlitēs cēpērunt* (object first, §597).
2. *Ob memorem īram saevae Iūnōnis*: "because of the unforgetting anger of cruel Juno". *Memorem* (accusative) waits for *īram*; *saevae* (genitive feminine) for *Iūnōnis*.
3. *Superiectō* (ablative neuter singular) goes with *aequore*; *pavidae* (nominative feminine plural) with *dammae*. The endings decide; the interlocked order is a-b-verb-a-b (ag1903 §598h).
4. *Suppliciō improbōs afficiunt* (object before verb) | *dēfendunt ac tuentur bonōs* (verbs before object). The opposed objects stand at the two ends (ag1903 §598f).
5. "He wrote the letter with great care." A one-syllable preposition often stands between an emphatic adjective and its noun (ag1903 §599d).
6. *Multā … in rosā*, *gracilis … puer*, *liquidīs … odōribus*, *grātō … sub antrō*.
7. "The enemy, whom Caesar had defeated, sent envoys and asked for peace" (literally "when they had sent envoys"). The frame is *Hostēs … pācem petīvērunt*; the relative clause and the *cum* clause sit inside it (ag1903 §601).
8. *Caesar autem nōn vēnit*: *autem* is never first (§599b). *Populus Rōmānus vīcit*: a fixed order (§598k).
9. Latin endings show each word's role, so position is free to show emphasis (ag1903 §600). English shows roles mainly by position, so moving words changes or destroys the meaning.
