## The idea

English verse is built on stress: "the CURfew TOLLS the KNELL of PARTing DAY" alternates stressed and unstressed syllables. Latin verse is built on something else: the time a syllable takes. A heavy syllable counts as twice a light one, and a line of verse is a fixed pattern of heavy and light syllables (ag1903 §602; bennett1918 §§361, 366). This is called quantitative verse, and the pattern of a line is its **metre**: the fixed scheme of heavy and light syllables that every line of a poem follows.

Allen and Greenough explain where this came from. Early Indo-European poetry seems to have depended on accent, as English does. The Greeks developed verse measured by syllable length, and the Romans of the classical period took their metres from the Greeks (ag1903 §602).

You already have the tools. Part 1 taught that a syllable is **heavy** if it has a long vowel or diphthong, or if its vowel is followed by two consonants; otherwise it is **light** (ag1903 §603e–f). Grammars of metre call these "long" and "short" syllables. This guide keeps "long" and "short" for vowels and says "heavy" and "light" for syllables, as in part 1, because a heavy syllable can contain a short vowel: the first syllable of *arma* is heavy, but its *a* is short.

The epic line of Latin is the **dactylic hexameter**, "six measures of dactyls". Its unit is the **foot**, a group of syllables that fills one beat. Two kinds of foot appear in it:

- the **dactyl**: heavy, light, light (— ∪ ∪), like *Ītalĭă* or *prīmus ab*;
- the **spondee**: heavy, heavy (— —), like *ōrīs* or *Rōmae*.

Two lights equal one heavy, so both feet take the same time. That is why they can replace each other.

## How it is built

**The pattern.** A hexameter has six feet (ag1903 §615; bennett1918 §368):

- feet 1 to 4: dactyl or spondee, the poet's choice;
- foot 5: almost always a dactyl;
- foot 6: two syllables, heavy and then either heavy or light. The last syllable of any line counts as heavy whatever it is: it is ***anceps***, "two-headed" (ag1903 §612c).

So every hexameter ends with the same rhythm, "— ∪ ∪ | — —", like *prīmus ab ōrīs* or *moenia Rōmae*. Find that ending first; it anchors the rest.

**Elision.** A word ending in a vowel, a diphthong, or a vowel + *m* loses that ending before a word beginning with a vowel or *h* (ag1903 §612e–f; bennett1918 §366.7). *Multum ille et* is read *mult' ill' et*. Allen and Greenough explain why *-m* does not stop elision: final *m* "has a feeble nasal sound" (§612f note 1), as part 1 said. **Elision** is the dropping of a final vowel (or vowel + *m*) before a following vowel. In reading aloud, it is usual to drop the elided syllable, though Allen and Greenough think it was sounded lightly (§612e).

Some words never elide: *dō*, *dem*, *spē*, *spem*, *sim*, *stō*, *stem*, plural *quī*, and interjections like *ō* (ag1903 §612f note 2). Leaving a vowel unelided is called **hiatus** ("gaping"); it is rare and marks a pause or emphasis (§612g).

**The caesura.** A **caesura** ("cutting") is a word-end inside a foot (ag1903 §611b). Every hexameter has a main one, usually in the third foot, after its first syllable: *Arma virumque canō ‖ Troiae quī prīmus ab ōrīs*. If not, it falls in the fourth foot, often with another in the second (§615c). The caesura usually coincides with a pause in the sense.

**Scanning a line.** To scan is to mark each syllable heavy or light and divide the line into feet (ag1903 §612d). Take *Arma virumque canō, Troiae quī prīmus ab ōrīs*.

1. **Elide** where a vowel-final word meets a vowel: none here.
2. **Divide into syllables**, letting a single consonant go with the next vowel even across words: *ar·ma·vi·rum·que·ca·nō·Troi·iae·quī·prī·mu·sa·bō·rīs*. *Ab ōrīs* becomes *a·bō·rīs*. *Troiae* has consonantal *i* between vowels, pronounced double (part 1): *Troi·iae*.
3. **Mark the weight** of each syllable. Long vowels and diphthongs: *nō*, *iae*, *quī*, *prī*, *bō*, *rīs* are heavy. Closed by a consonant: *ar* (r + m), *rum* (m + q), *Troi* (consonantal i) are heavy. The rest (*ma*, *vi*, *que*, *ca*, *mu*, *sa*) are light.
4. **Start from the end**: *prī·mu·sa | bō·rīs* is — ∪ ∪ | — —, feet 5 and 6.
5. **Work from the start**: *ar·ma·vi* (— ∪ ∪) dactyl; *rum·que·ca* (— ∪ ∪) dactyl; *nō·Troi* (— —) spondee; *iae·quī* (— —) spondee.
6. **Result**: — ∪ ∪ | — ∪ ∪ | — — | — — | — ∪ ∪ | — —, dactyl, dactyl, spondee, spondee, dactyl, spondee. Caesura after *canō*.

**Where vowel lengths come from.** This guide prints macrons. Most editions of poetry do not, so you need three sources: the dictionary for stem vowels (*fātum*, *prīmus*); the endings you know (ablative *-ā*, genitive plural *-ōrum*, *-īs* in the dative and ablative plural); and the rules for final syllables, which Allen and Greenough list (ag1903 §604): final *-a* is short in words with case endings except the first-declension ablative, and long elsewhere (imperatives, adverbs); final *-ī* and *-u* usually long; final *-ō* long in datives and ablatives and of either length in many other words; final *-is* and *-us* usually short, except in plurals and the fourth declension. Position then does the rest.

## Where it applies

- **Epic**: Ennius's Annals, Lucretius, Vergil's Aeneid, Ovid's Metamorphoses, Lucan.
- **Pastoral and didactic poetry**: Vergil's Eclogues and Georgics.
- **Satire and verse letters**: Horace's Satires and Epistles, Juvenal.
- **The first line of the elegiac couplet** (lesson 9).
- **Prose rhythm**: orators ended sentences on chosen patterns of heavy and light syllables (ag1903 §601f). The same weights apply, though not in feet.
- **Elision** applies in all classical verse, across punctuation too (*atqu' altae*; *tantaen' animīs*).
- **The caesura** in the third foot (after its first syllable, or between its two lights, the "feminine" caesura, bennett1918 §368.3c), or in the fourth.
- **Spondaic lines**, with a spondee in foot 5, are rare and usually end in a four-syllable word: *cāra deum subolēs, magnum Iovis incrēmentum* (Vergil, Eclogues 4.49; bennett1918 §368.2).
- **Poetic liberties**: a vowel can become a consonant (*Lāvīnja*), two vowels can merge (*deinde* as two syllables, bennett1918 §367.1), and stop + liquid can make a heavy or light syllable (*patrēs* with light or heavy *pa*, ag1903 §603f).

## Watch out

**Do not read the stress as the beat.** English readers put the rhythm on stressed syllables. In *canō* the word stress falls on *ca* (part 1), but the foot begins on *nō*; in *Troiae* the stress is on *Troi*, the foot begins on *iae*. Latin verse counts weight, not stress (ag1903 §602; bennett1918 §361).

**Do not confuse vowel length with syllable weight.** The *a* of *arma* is short, but *ar* is heavy. The *e* of *est* is short, but the syllable is heavy. Mark heavy syllables by both causes.

**Do not forget that position works across word boundaries.** In *prīmus ab ōrīs*, the *s* of *prīmus* moves on to *ab*: *prī·mu·sa·bō·rīs*, so *mu* is light. In *mūtātās dīcere* the final *s* closes *tās*, but that syllable is heavy anyway. In *animus mūtātās* the *s* + *m* make *mus* heavy.

**Do not count *h* or *qu* as two consonants.** *H* is not a consonant for weight (*nu·sho*); *qu* is one consonant [kʷ] (part 1). *X* and *z* count as two (ag1903 §603f).

**Do not forget elision of *-m*.** *Multum ille* loses *-um*. English readers expect an *m* to block it; in Latin it does not (ag1903 §612f).

**Do not elide across the end of a line.** The last syllable of a line is anceps and stands before a pause (bennett1918 §366.10). Rare exceptions, where a line has an extra syllable that elides into the next, are called hypermetric (bennett1918 §367.6).

**Do not trust prose quantities in every case.** Poets lengthen some vowels (*Ītalia*) and shorten others (*mihi*, *tibi* can have either length, ag1903 §604). If the line will not scan, look for a liberty before deciding the line is wrong.

## Connections

French verse counts syllables, and Spanish verse counts syllables and stress; neither counts weight. The Latin habit of elision lives on in both: French *l'ami* and the counting rule that a mute *e* before a vowel is not a syllable, Spanish *sinalefa*, where *de amor* counts as two syllables in verse. Japanese verse (5-7-5) also counts units of time, the mora, and a long vowel or a final *n* counts as two units, as a heavy Latin syllable counts as two lights.

## Exercises

1. Mark each syllable of *Albānīque patrēs* heavy or light, and give the reason for each.
2. Where are the elisions in *atque altae moenia Rōmae* and *multum ille et terrīs*? Write the lines as they are read.
3. Scan *moenia Rōmae* and *prīmus ab ōrīs*. Why do so many hexameters end like this?
4. Scan *Arma virumque canō* and mark the caesura that follows.
5. Why is the *nu* of *ūnus homō* light, while the *mus* of *animus mūtātās* is heavy?
6. In *lītora*, the first syllable is heavy and the other two light. Why?
7. Scan *Mūsa, mihī causās memorā* (Aeneid 1.8; the edition prints *mihī* with a long final *ī*). How many feet does it fill?
8. What is wrong with this scansion of *Ītaliam fātō*: *Ī | ta li am | fā tō*?
9. Why can a dactyl replace a spondee, but a single light syllable not replace a heavy one?

## Worked answers

1. *Al* heavy (short *a* + *l b*); *bā* heavy (long *ā*); *nī* heavy (long *ī*); *que* light (short *e*, open); *pa* light (short *a*; *t r* go together with the next vowel); *trēs* heavy (long *ē*) (ag1903 §603e–f).
2. *Atqu' altae*, *mult' ill' et*. Final *-e* and *-um* are dropped before a vowel (ag1903 §612e–f).
3. *Moe·ni·a | Rō·mae*, *prī·mu·sa | bō·rīs*: — ∪ ∪ | — —. Foot 5 is almost always a dactyl and foot 6 two syllables, so every line ends this way (ag1903 §615).
4. *Ar·ma·vi | rum·que·ca | nō ‖*: dactyl, dactyl, then the caesura after *canō*, the first syllable of the third foot.
5. *Ūnus homō*: *s* + *h*, and *h* does not count, so only one consonant follows the *u*, which goes with the next syllable (*nu·sho*). *Animus mūtātās*: *s* + *m* are two consonants, so *mus* is closed and heavy (ag1903 §603f).
6. *Lī* has a long vowel (*lītus*, *lītoris*); *to* and *ra* have short vowels and are open (neuter plural *-a* is short, ag1903 §604). — ∪ ∪, a dactyl.
7. *Mū·sa·mi | hī·cau | sās·me·mo | rā*: — ∪ ∪ | — — | — ∪ ∪ | —: three and a half feet, up to the caesura in foot 4 (the line goes on *quō nūmine laesō*).
8. Feet must start on a heavy syllable and fill one beat: *Ī·ta·li* is — ∪ ∪, then *am·fā* — —. *Ta·li·am* would be ∪ ∪ —, an anapaest, which the hexameter does not use.
9. Two lights take the same time as one heavy (ag1903 §602; bennett1918 §366.1), so — ∪ ∪ and — — are equal. A light alone is only half a heavy and would leave the foot short.
