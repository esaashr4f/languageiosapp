## The idea

Lesson 8 built the hexameter, the line of epic. The second great metre of Latin is the **elegiac couplet**: a hexameter followed by a shorter line, the **pentameter**, the two together making a unit of sense (ag1903 §616; bennett1918 §369). Allen and Greenough describe it as the metre of letters, love poems and laments, and Ovid's favourite (§616 note). Catullus, Tibullus, Propertius and Ovid wrote elegy; so did the authors of countless epitaphs.

The pentameter is a hexameter with something taken away. It has two halves. Each half is two and a half feet: two feet and one heavy syllable. Between the halves there is always a word-end (ag1903 §616a–b). In the first half the two feet may be dactyls or spondees; in the second half they must be dactyls (bennett1918 §369.1). So the line has a fixed, falling close: — ∪ ∪ | — ∪ ∪ | —.

Ovid makes a joke of this at the start of his love poems. He was writing epic, he says, in hexameters, when Cupid laughed and stole a foot from every second line. The missing foot is the pentameter.

The second half of this lesson is **poetic grammar**. Verse does not have a separate grammar, but the metre pushes poets towards some forms and constructions that prose avoids: an alternative ending that fits the line, a plural for a singular, a case without a preposition. You need to recognise them, because they are everywhere in Vergil, Horace and Ovid.

## How it is built

**Scanning a pentameter.** Take *ēdere, māteriā conveniente modīs*.

1. Elide where needed: none here.
2. Syllables: *ē·de·re·mā·te·ri·ā·con·ve·ni·en·te·mo·dīs*.
3. Weights: *ē* heavy (long, *ēdō*); *de*, *re* light; *mā* heavy; *te*, *ri* light (vowel before vowel, ag1903 §603a); *ā* heavy (ablative); *con* heavy (*n* + *v*); *ve*, *ni* light; *en* heavy (*n* + *t*); *te*, *mo* light; *dīs* heavy.
4. First half: *ē·de·re | mā·te·ri | ā*: — ∪ ∪ | — ∪ ∪ | —, ending with a word.
5. Second half: *con·ve·ni | en·te·mo | dīs*: — ∪ ∪ | — ∪ ∪ | —.

**Recognising poetic forms.** When a form looks wrong, ask whether it is one of these:

- *-ēre* for *-ērunt* in the third person plural perfect: *tenuēre* = *tenuērunt* (ag1903 §163a; Allen and Greenough call the origin of both endings doubtful).
- Contracted perfects: *natārunt* = *natāvērunt*, *amāsse* = *amāvisse* (ag1903 §181).
- *-um* for *-ōrum* in the genitive plural: *deum*, *superum*, *virum* (ag1903 §49d), and *-um* for *-ārum* in some words: *caelicolum* "of the gods" (§43d).
- *-re* for *-ris* in the second person singular passive: *vidēre* "you are seen" as well as "to see" (ag1903 §163).
- **Plural for singular**, "sometimes for metrical reasons, sometimes from a mere fashion": *ōra* "face", *scēptra* "sceptre", *silentia* "silence" (ag1903 §101 note 2).
- **No preposition** for place to which or from which: *Ītaliam vēnit* "came to Italy" (ag1903 §428g).
- **Dative of direction**: *it clāmor caelō* "a shout goes up to the sky" (ag1903 §428h).
- **Greek accusative** for the part affected: *nūda genū* "bare as to the knee" (ag1903 §397b).
- **Tmesis**, a compound split in two: *quō mē cumque* for *quōcumque mē* (bennett1918 §367.7).
- **Infinitive of purpose** after verbs of motion and giving (ag1903 §460c).

## Where it applies

**The elegiac couplet**

- **Love elegy**: Catullus, Tibullus, Propertius, Ovid's Amores and Art of Love.
- **Letters and laments**: Ovid's Heroides, Tristia and Letters from the Black Sea; Catullus 101.
- **Epigram and epitaph**: Martial, and many verse inscriptions (lesson 14).
- **Each couplet a unit**: the sense is normally complete at the end of the pentameter (ag1903 §616 note).
- **Elision and caesura** work as in the hexameter. The pentameter's mid-line word-end is fixed, not optional (ag1903 §616b).

**Poetic forms and constructions** (all in prose too, but much rarer there)

- *-ēre* perfects: *tenuēre*, *fuēre* (Sallust uses them in prose too: *fuēre cīvēs*, lesson 3).
- *-um* genitives: *deum*, *superum*, *virum*.
- Contracted perfects: *natārunt*, *amāsse*, *nōsse* (ag1903 §181).
- *-re* passives: *vidēre*, *loquere* (also an imperative: context decides).
- Poetic plurals: *ōra*, *colla* "neck", *silentia*.
- Place without a preposition: *Ītaliam vēnit*; *Lāvīnia … lītora* (lesson 8).
- Dative of direction: *it clāmor caelō*.
- Greek accusative: *nūda genū*; *sinūs collēcta*.
- Tmesis: *quō mē cumque*.
- Infinitive of purpose and after adjectives: *facilēs aurem praebēre* "ready to lend an ear" (Propertius, quoted in ag1903 §510 note 3).
- Variable quantities: *mihi* and *mihī*, *Ītalia*, poets choosing whichever fits (ag1903 §604).
- Simple words for compounds and high-register vocabulary: *ēnsis* for *gladius* "sword", *pontus* and *aequor* for *mare*, *polus* and *aethēr* for *caelum*.

## Watch out

**Do not scan a pentameter as a short hexameter.** It is not "five feet". Count two halves of two and a half feet each: — ∪ ∪ | — ∪ ∪ | — ‖ — ∪ ∪ | — ∪ ∪ | —. The ancient name "pentameter" comes from a different way of dividing it (ag1903 §616, footnote).

**Do not put a spondee in the second half.** The two feet after the mid-line break are always dactyls (bennett1918 §369.1). If you get a spondee there, look again at the quantities.

**Do not ignore the mid-line word-end.** If your division of a pentameter puts the half-way point inside a word, the scansion is wrong (ag1903 §616b).

**Do not read *-ēre* as an infinitive.** *Tenuēre colōnī* has a subject and is finite: "settlers held". The infinitive is *tenēre*, with the stem vowel, not a perfect stem. Look for the perfect stem (*tenu-*) before *-ēre*.

**Do not read *deum* as an accusative singular.** In *rēgīna deum* "queen of the gods" (Aeneid 1.9) it is a genitive plural. Ask which reading the sentence needs.

**Do not translate a poetic plural as plural.** *Ōra* of one person is "face"; *silentia* "silence". Context decides.

**Do not add a preposition in your translation of every case.** *Caelō* after a verb of motion in Vergil is "to the sky", not "by the sky": a dative of direction (ag1903 §428h).

**Do not copy poetic constructions into prose.** *\*Caesar exercitum castrīs dūxit* for "Caesar led the army to the camp" is not classical prose, which says *in castra*. Only towns, small islands, *domum* and *rūs* go without a preposition in prose (part 3).

## Connections

French classical verse also pairs lines and closes sense at the end of a couplet, and the alexandrine has a fixed mid-line break, as the pentameter does; this is a parallel, not an inheritance. Spanish and Italian poetry also prefer some forms prose avoids. English "elegy" comes from *elegīa*, though in English it now means any lament, whatever the metre.

## Exercises

1. Scan *ēdere, māteriā conveniente modīs* and mark the break.
2. Scan the second line of the Catullus couplet and say where the elision is.
3. In *Pār erat īnferior versus*, why is the *ra* of *erat* light?
4. Write the prose forms of *tenuēre*, *natārunt* and *deum* (in *rēgīna deum*).
5. Translate *It clāmor caelō* and give the prose version.
6. Translate *Nūda genū* and name the construction.
7. In *Quō mē cumque rapit tempestās*, what is the figure, and what is the prose order?
8. Why can the second half of a pentameter not have a spondee, while the first half can?
9. Why does Ovid say Cupid stole one foot?

## Worked answers

1. *ē·de·re | mā·te·ri | ā ‖ con·ve·ni | en·te·mo | dīs*: — ∪ ∪ | — ∪ ∪ | — ‖ — ∪ ∪ | — ∪ ∪ | —. The break falls after *māteriā*, at a word-end (ag1903 §616b).
2. *Adveni' hās*: the final *-ō* of *adveniō* is elided before *h* (ag1903 §612e). *ad·ve·ni | hās·mi·se | rās ‖ frā·te·ra | dīn·fe·ri | ās*.
3. *Erat īnferior*: the *t* moves on to *īn-*, so *ra* is an open syllable with a short vowel (*ra·tīn*).
4. *Tenuērunt*; *natāvērunt*; *deōrum*. The poetic forms are alternative or contracted endings that fit the metre (ag1903 §§49d, 163a, 181).
5. "A shout goes up to the sky." Prose: *it clāmor ad caelum* (ag1903 §428h).
6. "Bare as to the knee", "with her knee bare": the Greek accusative of the part affected (ag1903 §397b).
7. Tmesis, a compound split by another word (bennett1918 §367.7). Prose: *quōcumque mē rapit tempestās*.
8. The rule of the metre: the second half is always two dactyls (bennett1918 §369.1). The first half allows the same substitutions as the hexameter.
9. The pentameter is a hexameter shortened by two half-feet, one at the break and one at the end (ag1903 §616): one foot in all. Ovid pictures Cupid stealing it.
