## The idea

You now know about 1,500 Latin words. Many more are within reach without a dictionary, because Latin builds words from parts that keep their meaning: a root, a suffix that says what kind of word it is, and often a prefix. *Scrīp-tor* is "one who writes"; *scrīp-tūra* "a writing"; *lēc-tiō* "a reading". Once you know *-tor*, *-tūra* and *-tiō*, every verb you know gives you three more nouns.

Allen and Greenough describe the endings as "significant": the Romans used them "with more or less consciousness of their meaning" (ag1903 §235). They go further back than that: "all formation of words is originally a process of composition", one meaningful element added to another, though the suffixes long ago stopped being separate words (§227). So a suffix today is a unit of meaning, not a word.

A **root** is the core that carries the basic meaning, shared by a family of words: *reg-* in *regō*, *rēx*, *rēgnum*, *regiō*. A **suffix** is an ending added to a root or stem to make a new word: *-tor*, *-tās*, *-ōsus*. A **derivative** is a word made this way. The suffix decides the part of speech and the declension, so it also tells you how to decline the new word.

Many suffixes attach to the supine stem, the fourth principal part without *-um*. Allen and Greenough explain that *-tor*, *-tiō*, *-tūra* and *-tus* show "the same phonetic change as the supine ending -tum" (ag1903 §§236a note 1, 238b note 1). That is why *scrīptor* has *-pt-* like *scrīptum*, and *sēnsus* has *-s-* like *sēnsum*.

## How it is built

**Taking an unknown word apart.**

1. **Strip the case ending** to find the stem: *lēctōribus* → *lēctōr-*.
2. **Strip any prefix** (part 4, lesson 16): *ad-iūtor* → *iūtor*.
3. **Find a known suffix** at the end: *-tor* "one who".
4. **What is left is a root or stem** you may know: *lēc-* from *legō, lēctum*; *iū-* from *iuvō, iūtum*.
5. **Combine the meanings**: *lēctor* "a reader"; *adiūtor* "a helper".
6. **Check the context**, because derivatives drift: *legiō*, "a gathering", came to mean "a legion" (ag1903 §237).

**The main suffixes.**

*Nouns*

- *-tor*, *-sor* (m.), feminine *-trīx*: the doer (ag1903 §236a). *Scrīptor* "writer", *ōrātor* "speaker", *audītor* "listener", *victor*, *victrīx* "winner".
- *-tiō*, *-siō* (f.), *-tus*, *-sus* (m., fourth declension), *-tūra* (f.): the action or its result (§238b). *Lēctiō* "reading", *mōtus* "movement", *scrīptūra* "writing".
- *-or* (m.) from verbs: the state (§238a). *Timor* from *timeō*, *amor* from *amō*.
- *-men*, *-mentum* (n.): the means or result (§239). *Agmen* "column" from *agō*; *ōrnāmentum* "ornament" from *ōrnō*; *certāmen* "contest" from *certō*.
- *-culum*, *-bulum* (n.): the instrument or place (§240). *Vehiculum* "vehicle" from *vehō*; *ōrāculum* "oracle" from *ōrō*.
- *-tās*, *-tūdō*, *-ia*, *-itia* (f.), from adjectives: the quality (§241). *Bonitās* "goodness", *fortitūdō* "bravery", *iūstitia* "justice", *laetitia* "joy".
- *-ulus*, *-olus*, *-ellus*, *-culus*: the diminutive, often affectionate or pitying (§243). *Libellus* "little book", *ocellus* "little eye", *misellus* "poor little".

*Adjectives*

- *-ōsus*: "full of" (§245). *Perīculōsus*, *glōriōsus*.
- *-eus*: "made of" (§247). *Aureus* "golden", *ferreus* "of iron".
- *-ālis*, *-āris*: "belonging to" (§248). *Nātūrālis*.
- *-ānus*, *-īnus*, *-icus*: "belonging to, coming from" (§249). *Rōmānus*, *Latīnus*.
- *-bilis*, *-ilis*: "able to be" (§252). *Amābilis* "lovable", *mōbilis* "movable", *crēdibilis*.
- *-āx*: "inclined to", often too much (§251). *Loquāx* "talkative", *audāx* "bold".

*Verbs*

- *-scō*: the inceptive, "begin to, become" (ag1903 §263.1). *Senēscō* "grow old", *calēscō* "grow warm".
- *-tō*, *-itō*, on the supine stem: the frequentative, "do hard or often" (§263.2). *Iactō* "toss" from *iaciō*; *dictō* "say often, dictate" from *dīcō*. The special sense "often disappears" (§263.2).
- First-conjugation verbs from nouns and adjectives (§259): *cūrō* from *cūra*, *nōminō* from *nōmen*, *nūntiō* from *nūntius*.

## Where it applies

- **Reading unseen texts**: most words outside the core vocabulary are derivatives of core words. *Dēfēnsor*, *dēfēnsiō*, from *dēfendō, dēfēnsum*; *cōnservātor* from *cōnservō*.
- **Agent nouns on any verb with a supine**: *ēmptor* "buyer" (*emō, ēmptum*), *vēnditor* "seller".
- **Feminine agents in *-trīx***: *victrīx*, *genetrīx* "mother" (from *gignō, genitum*). Masculines in *-sor* rarely have one (ag1903 §236a note 2).
- **Abstracts in *-tās* on almost any adjective**: *lībertās*, *celeritās*, *crūdēlitās*, *vetustās*; *-tūdō* on some: *magnitūdō*, *fortitūdō*, *multitūdō*.
- **Action nouns that become concrete**: *legiō* "a levy" → "a legion"; *regiō* "a direction" → "a region" (ag1903 §§237–238).
- **Diminutives** express smallness, affection, pity or contempt: *dēliciolae* "little darling", *Graeculus* "a wretched little Greek" (ag1903 §243 note 2).
- **Inceptives**, which often borrow the perfect of the simple verb: *calēscō*, perfect *caluī* (§263.1 note). Some have no simple verb: *proficīscor*.
- **Frequentatives** whose special sense has faded: *cantō* "sing" beside *canō*; *iactō*.
- **Verbs from nouns**: *cūrō*, *nōminō*, *laudō* (from *laus*, *laudis*).
- **Adjectives in *-ōsus*, *-eus*, *-ālis*, *-bilis***: *glōriōsus*, *ferreus*, *nātūrālis*, *mōbilis*.
- **With prefixes as well**: *in-* "not" before adjectives (*incrēdibilis*, *inūtilis*), and the verb prefixes of part 4.
- **Patronymics** in poetry: *Anchīsiadēs* "son of Anchises", that is Aeneas (ag1903 §244).

## Watch out

**Do not trust a suffix blindly.** *Mōtus* can be the participle "moved" or the noun "movement"; *audītor* is a person, *audītus* (fourth declension) the sense of hearing. Check the declension and the context.

**Do not take every *-or* for an agent.** *Timor* and *amor* are states, not doers. The agent suffix is *-tor* (or *-sor*), with the *t* of the supine.

**Do not build agent nouns on the present stem.** *\*Scrībtor*, *\*legtor* are wrong: the suffix follows the supine stem with its sound changes (ag1903 §236a note 1): *scrīptor*, *lēctor*.

**Do not read a diminutive as small in size only.** *Ocellī* in Catullus are not small eyes but dear eyes; *Graeculus* is contempt. Allen and Greenough note the emotional uses (§243 note 2).

**Do not translate a frequentative as "often" by reflex.** *Iactō* is usually just "throw, toss"; *dictō* is "dictate". Allen and Greenough say the special sense "often disappears" (§263.2).

**Do not assume English meanings.** Latin derivatives that became English words often shifted: *virtūs* is "courage, excellence" more than "virtue"; *ōrātor* is "speaker", including an envoy; *ingenium* is "talent, character", not "ingenuity".

**Do not coin words.** Recognising *-tor* nouns is safe; inventing *\*vīsor* "seer" is not. Use a dictionary when you write Latin.

## Connections

Spanish still forms new words with these suffixes. Wiktionary gives Spanish *-dor* (*escritor*, *lector*) as inherited from *-tor* and *-dad* from *-tātem*, while *-ción* is a learned borrowing of *-tiōnem*. French has matching suffixes in *-eur*, *-tion* and *-té*. English borrowed the Latin words whole: *orator*, *lection*, *vehicle*, *oracle*, *fortitude*. Japanese and Korean build words from Sino-Japanese and Sino-Korean roots in a similar way, but by compounding two meaningful roots rather than adding suffixes.

## Exercises

1. Take apart *dēfēnsor* and give its meaning.
2. Make agent nouns from *emō, ēmptum*; *vincō, victum*; *doceō, doctum*.
3. What do *lībertās*, *magnitūdō* and *laetitia* have in common, and what are they made from?
4. Give the meaning of *ferreus*, *glōriōsus* and *amābilis*, and the suffix of each.
5. What does *calēscō* mean, if *caleō* is "be warm"?
6. Why is it *scrīptor* and not *\*scrībtor*?
7. Translate *Ōrātor audītōribus placēre vult.*
8. In the Catullus passage, find the diminutives and say what each is made from.
9. Correct the reading of *mōtus* as "moved" in *Mōtus terrae magnus erat.*

## Worked answers

1. *Dē-* prefix + *fēns-* (the supine stem of *dēfendō*, *dēfēnsum*) + *-or*: "defender" (ag1903 §236a).
2. *Ēmptor* "buyer", *victor* "winner", *doctor* "teacher". Each is the supine stem + *-or* (the *-t-* is already there).
3. All are abstract nouns of quality, from adjectives: *līber*, *magnus*, *laetus* (ag1903 §241).
4. *Ferreus* "made of iron" (*-eus*, §247); *glōriōsus* "full of glory" (*-ōsus*, §245); *amābilis* "able to be loved, lovable" (*-bilis*, §252).
5. "Grow warm, become warm": the inceptive *-scō* (ag1903 §263.1).
6. Suffixes in *-tor* follow the supine stem with the same sound change, *b* + *t* > *pt*, as *scrīptum* (ag1903 §236a note 1; part 4, lesson 12).
7. "The speaker wants to please his listeners." *Placeō* takes the dative (part 3).
8. *Misellus* from *miser*; *turgidulus* from *turgidus*; *ocellus* from *oculus* (ag1903 §243). *Puella* itself looks like a diminutive, but this lesson does not explain its origin.
9. "There was a great movement of the earth", an earthquake. *Mōtus* here is the fourth-declension noun (genitive *mōtūs*), with *terrae* depending on it, not the participle.
