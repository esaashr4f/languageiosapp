## The idea

Three things in any Latin text are written in a code you have to learn separately: a person's name, a date, and a number in numerals. Each code has a logic, and once you see it you can read them at a glance.

**Names.** A Roman citizen man normally had three names (ag1903 §108; bennett1918 §373): the ***praenōmen***, a personal name chosen from a short list (*Mārcus*, *Gāius*, *Lūcius*); the ***nōmen***, the name of his clan or *gēns* (*Tullius*, "of the Tullian clan"); and the ***cognōmen***, the name of a branch of the family, often in origin a nickname (*Cicerō*, from *cicer* "chickpea", ag1903 §108). Because so few praenomina were used, they were almost always abbreviated: *M.* for *Mārcus*, *C.* for *Gāius* (§108c). A fourth name could honour a deed or mark an adoption: *Pūblius Cornēlius Scīpiō Āfricānus Aemiliānus* (§108a). Women in classical times usually had no personal name, only the feminine of the clan name: Cicero's daughter was *Tullia*, and a second daughter would have been *Tullia secunda* or *minor* (§108b).

Official records added the father's praenomen and the voting tribe, all abbreviated: *M. TVLLIVS M. F. … COR. CICERO* "Marcus Tullius Cicero, son of Marcus, of the Cornelian tribe" (§108, footnote).

**Dates.** The Romans did not number the days of the month forwards. They had three fixed days in each month and counted backwards to the next one (ag1903 §631; bennett1918 §371):

- the ***Kalendae*** (Kalends), the 1st;
- the ***Nōnae*** (Nones), the 5th, or the 7th in March, May, July and October;
- the ***Īdūs*** (Ides), the 13th, or the 15th in those same four months.

Allen and Greenough connect *Kalendae* with *calāre* "to call": on that day the priests announced the new moon. The Ides were the day of the full moon (ag1903 §631a–b). This lesson capitalises the three names, as Allen and Greenough do; part 1 printed *kalendae* in lower case.

The count is **inclusive**: the Romans counted both ends (bennett1918 §371.4). The day before the Ides is *prīdiē Īdūs*; the day before that is "the third day before the Ides", *ante diem tertium Īdūs*. So a Roman number is always one more than our count of days between.

**Years** were named after the two consuls of the year, in an ablative absolute: *L. Pīsōne A. Gabīniō cōnsulibus* "when Lucius Piso and Aulus Gabinius were consuls" (part 6). Later writers counted from the founding of Rome, *ab urbe conditā* (*a.u.c.*), which Varro placed in what we call 753 BC (ag1903 §630).

**Numerals.** Latin wrote numbers with letters: I 1, V 5, X 10, L 50, C 100, D 500, M 1000 (ag1903 §133). They add from left to right, largest first: *XXXVI* = 36. A smaller sign before a larger one subtracts: *IV* 4, *IX* 9, *XL* 40, *XC* 90. Allen and Greenough give both forms for 4, 9, 14, 40 and 90 (*IIII* or *IV*, *XXXX* or *XL*), and Caesar's text writes *XIIII* for 14. How the signs themselves arose is not explained here.

## How it is built

**Reading a name.** *C. Iūlius Caesar*: praenomen *C.* = *Gāius*, nomen *Iūlius*, cognomen *Caesar*. The abbreviation *C.* stands for *G* because *C* once had the sound *g* (ag1903 §108c note).

**Reading a date.** Take *a. d. V Kal. Apr.*

1. Expand: *ante diem quīntum Kalendās Aprīlēs* "on the fifth day before the Kalends of April". The month is an adjective agreeing with *Kalendās* (ag1903 §631d note), and *ante diem* + ordinal is followed by an accusative, as if it were a preposition (ag1903 §424g).
2. The fixed day is the Kalends of April, 1 April.
3. Rule for the Kalends: add 2 to the number of days in the month before, then subtract the given number (ag1903 §631d). March has 31 days: 31 + 2 − 5 = 28.
4. So 28 March.

For the Nones or Ides, add 1 to the day they fall on, then subtract: *IV Īd. Sept.* = 13 + 1 − 4 = 10 September (§631d).

**Saying a date.** The fixed days themselves go in the ablative of time: *Kalendīs Iānuāriīs* "on 1 January", *Īdibus Mārtiīs* "on the Ides of March". The day before is *prīdiē* + accusative: *prīdiē Nōnās Iūniās*.

**Reading a numeral.** *CCLXIII*: C C = 200, L = 50, X = 10, III = 3; total 263. In a sentence it stands for the Latin number word in the case the grammar needs: *mīlium CCLXIII* is read *mīlium ducentōrum sexāgintā trium*.

**Converting a year a.u.c.** Subtract from 754 for a year BC (ag1903 §630): *a.u.c. 696* is 58 BC.

## Where it applies

**Names**

- **In prose**, a man is often called by nomen and cognomen (*Tullius Cicerō*) or cognomen alone (*Cicerō*, *Caesar*); a friend in a letter by praenomen or nomen (*Tullius* in Cicero's heading).
- **Abbreviated praenomina** in texts and inscriptions: *A.* Aulus, *C.* Gāius, *Cn.* Gnaeus, *D.* Decimus, *L.* Lūcius, *M.* Mārcus, *M'.* Mānius, *P.* Pūblius, *Q.* Quīntus, *Sex.* Sextus, *Ser.* Servius, *T.* Titus, *Ti.* Tiberius (ag1903 §108c; bennett1918 §373.2).
- **Filiation** on stone: *M. F.* "son of Marcus"; for a woman, the father's or husband's name in the genitive: *Caecilia Metellī* "Caecilia, daughter of Metellus" (Cicero, quoted in ag1903 §108, footnote).
- **Two brothers** together share a plural cognomen: *Pūblius et Servius Sullae* (ag1903 §108 note).
- **Honorific names**: *Āfricānus*, *Augustus*.

**Dates**

- **The fixed days** in the ablative: *Kalendīs*, *Nōnīs*, *Īdibus*, with the month adjective agreeing.
- **The day before** with *prīdiē* + accusative; the day after with *postrīdiē* + accusative.
- **Other days**: *ante diem* + ordinal + accusative, abbreviated *a. d.*; or the ordinal alone: *XV Kal. Sextīlīs* (ag1903 §424g).
- **After a preposition**: *in ante diem V Kalendās Novembrīs* "to 28 October" (Cicero, quoted in ag1903 §424g): the whole phrase behaves like a noun.
- **Month names** are adjectives: *Iānuārius*, *Februārius*, *Mārtius*, *Aprīlis*, *Maius*, *Iūnius*, *Iūlius* (*Quīntīlis* before 46 BC), *Augustus* (*Sextīlis* before Augustus), *September*, *Octōber*, *November*, *December* (bennett1918 §371.1; ag1903 §630).
- **Years**: consuls in the ablative absolute; *a.u.c.* in later writers and editors' notes; in leap years 24 February was counted twice, hence *bissextīlis* (ag1903 §630).
- **Editors' date lines**: modern editions head letters with *Scr. … a. d. VI Kal. Dec. a.u.c. 696*, "written … on 26 November 58 BC". *Scr.* is *scrīpta* "written".

**Numerals**

- **Additive**: *VI*, *XIIII*, *XXXVI*, *CCLXIII*.
- **Subtractive**: *IV*, *IX*, *XL*, *XC*, *CD*, *CM*; both styles occur (ag1903 §133).
- **Thousands** with *mīlia* + genitive (part 5), or a bar over the numeral in some inscriptions (not treated here); Allen and Greenough also give the old sign *CIƆ* for 1,000 (§133).
- **Ordinals in dates**: *a. d. V* is read *quīntum*, not *quīnque*.

## Watch out

**Do not count the days exclusively.** *A. d. III Nōn.* is not three days before the Nones but two: the Romans counted both ends (bennett1918 §371.4). *A. d. III Nōn. Iān.* is 3 January.

**Do not read *ante diem V* as a cardinal.** It is an ordinal, *quīntum*, agreeing with *diem*.

**Do not put *ante diem* phrases in the ablative.** *\*Ante diē quīntō* imitates "on the fifth day"; the phrase is *ante diem quīntum* + accusative (ag1903 §424g). The full older form is *quīntō diē ante Kalendās* (§424g), with the ablative of time and *ante* as an adverb.

**Do not forget the four long months.** In March, May, July and October the Nones are the 7th and the Ides the 15th. *Īdūs Mārtiae* are 15 March, not 13.

**Do not read *C.* as "C".** As an abbreviation of *Gāius* it stands for *G*, a relic of early spelling (ag1903 §108c note). *Cn.* is *Gnaeus*.

**Do not treat the cognomen as a surname for women.** Cicero's daughter is *Tullia*, from the nomen, not *\*Cicerōnia*.

**Do not read numerals mechanically from right to left.** Add left to right; subtract only where a smaller sign stands directly before a larger one. *XIIII* = 14 and *XIV* = 14; *XLIX* = 49.

**Do not translate *a.u.c.* years without converting.** *A.u.c. 691* is 63 BC, Cicero's consulship (ag1903 §630).

## Connections

The month names have close parallels in Spanish, French and English. *September* to *December* contain the numbers seven to ten (*septem*, *octō*, *novem*, *decem*), though they are the ninth to twelfth months; why is not explained here. Japanese and Korean name the months by number (*ichigatsu*, *irwol*) and count forwards; the Roman backwards count has no parallel there.

## Exercises

1. Expand and translate *M. Tullius M. f. Cicerō*.
2. What are the praenomina in *Q. Fabius Maximus* and *Cn. Pompeius Magnus*?
3. Convert *a. d. IV Nōn. Mārt.* to a modern date.
4. Convert *a. d. VIII Kal. Feb.* to a modern date.
5. Say "on the Ides of May" and "on the day before the Kalends of July".
6. Read *CCCLXVIII* and *XCIX*.
7. Write 1999 and 44 in Roman numerals, both styles where there are two.
8. What year BC is *a.u.c. 709*?
9. Why is the day before the Ides *prīdiē Īdūs* but the day before that *a. d. III Īd.*?
10. Translate *L. Pīsōne A. Gabīniō cōnsulibus* and name the construction.

## Worked answers

1. *Mārcus Tullius Mārcī fīlius Cicerō*: "Marcus Tullius Cicero, son of Marcus" (ag1903 §108, footnote).
2. *Q.* = *Quīntus*; *Cn.* = *Gnaeus* (ag1903 §108c). *Fabius* and *Pompeius* are nomina; *Maximus* and *Magnus* cognomina.
3. March's Nones are the 7th: 7 + 1 − 4 = 4 March (ag1903 §631d).
4. January has 31 days: 31 + 2 − 8 = 25 January (ag1903 §631d).
5. *Īdibus Maiīs* (15 May); *prīdiē Kalendās Iūliās* (30 June).
6. *CCC* 300 + *L* 50 + *X* 10 + *VIII* 8 = 368. *XC* 90 + *IX* 9 = 99.
7. 1999: *MCMXCIX* (or *MDCCCCLXXXXVIIII*). 44: *XLIV* (or *XXXXIIII*).
8. 754 − 709 = 45 BC (ag1903 §630).
9. Counting includes both ends: the day before the Ides counts as the second day before, which has its own name *prīdiē*; the day before that is the third (bennett1918 §371.4).
10. "In the consulship of Lucius Piso and Aulus Gabinius": an ablative absolute of nouns without a participle (part 6, lesson 4; ag1903 §419a).
