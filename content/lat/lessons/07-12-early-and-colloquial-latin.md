## The idea

Everything so far has been Classical Latin, the written standard of Cicero and Caesar (part 0). That standard was one moment in a long history. Before it lies **Early Latin** (also called Old or archaic Latin): the language of the oldest inscriptions, of the comic playwright Plautus and of the epic poet Ennius. Beside it, at every period, lies **colloquial Latin**, the Latin of conversation, which writers show when they imitate ordinary people.

Early Latin is not a different language. Its grammar is the grammar you know. What differs is mostly spelling and endings, and they differ in a regular way, because classical forms grew out of the older ones. Allen and Greenough record many of the old forms in the notes to their paradigms; this lesson collects the commonest.

Three patterns explain most of what you will meet.

1. **Diphthongs that later became simple vowels.** Early Latin had diphthongs *ai*, *oi*, *ou* (ag1903 §2). *Ai* later appears as *ae* (*aidilis* → *aedīlis*); *oi* and *ou* as *ū* (*oino* → *ūnum*, *ploirume* → *plūrimī*; *abdoucit* → *abdūcit*). Wiktionary gives Old Latin *oinos* as the ancestor of *ūnus*.
2. **Endings that later lost a consonant or changed a vowel.** The ablative singular once ended in *-d*: *praidad* (later *praedā*), *Gnaivod* (later *Gnaeō*) (ag1903 §§43 note 1, 49e). Second-declension *-us*, *-um* were *-os*, *-om* (*Luciom*), and *-unt* was *-ont* (*cosentiont*). Old inscriptions often leave out a final *-m* (*viro* for *virum*), the weak sound part 1 described.
3. **Forms that classical Latin dropped.** The subjunctive of *sum* was *siem, siēs, siet, sient* as well as *sim* (ag1903 §170 note). The passive infinitive could end in *-ier*: *vocārier* for *vocārī* (§183.4). *Faxō* and *faxim* served as future perfect and perfect subjunctive of *faciō* (§183.3). *Mēd* and *tēd* were old forms of *mē* and *tē* (Wiktionary). *Quoius* and *quoi* were *cuius* and *cui* (Wiktionary).

**Colloquial Latin** shows itself in other ways: dropped syllables (*calda* for *calida*, *calfēcit* for *calefēcit*, both in Wiktionary as forms of the same words), words of everyday speech in place of literary ones (*caballus* "nag" beside *equus*; *bellus* "pretty" beside *pulcher*), neuters treated as masculines, and short, loosely joined sentences. Cicero himself noticed one feature: dropping final *-s* before a consonant, he says, sounded "countrified", *subrūsticum* (ag1903 §15.7 note).

Some colloquial words survived where the literary ones did not. Wiktionary derives Spanish *caballo* from *caballus* and French *beau* from *bellus*.

## How it is built

**Turning an early form into a classical one.** Take *duonoro optumo fuise viro*.

1. Look for the old diphthongs and vowels: none here, but *duonoro* has *duo-* where classical has *bo-*. Wiktionary lists *duonus* as Old Latin for "good", the classical *bonus*; how the sounds changed is not explained here.
2. Restore missing final *-m*: *duonoro(m)*, *optumo(m)*, *viro(m)*.
3. Replace old endings: *-orom* is the genitive plural *-ōrum*; *-om* is the accusative *-um*.
4. Note spelling habits: *optumus* for *optimus*; *fuise* with one *s* for *fuisse*.
5. Result: *bonōrum optimum fuisse virum* "to have been the best of good men".

**Reading Plautus.** Expect, in a single scene:

- *siem*, *siēs*, *siet*, *sient* for *sim*, *sīs*, *sit*, *sint*;
- infinitives in *-ier*: *adferrier*, *arbitrārier*;
- *faxō* "I'll see to it";
- *-os*, *-om* after *u* or *v*: *salvos*, *servos* for *salvus*, *servus*;
- contractions with *est*: *optumumst*, *mortuost* for *optimum est*, *mortuus est*;
- oaths and interjections: *hercle* "by Hercules", *edepol* "by Pollux", *ecastor* "by Castor";
- gerundives in *-und-*: *caedundum* (lesson 2).

## Where it applies

- **Old inscriptions**: epitaphs, dedications and laws from before the late Republic, such as the Scipio epitaphs (CIL I² 7, 9). Spellings like *ai*, *oi*, *ou*, *-os*, *-om*, *-d* and missing *-m* mark them.
- **Plautus and Terence**, the comic dramatists: *siem*, *-ier*, *faxō*, *mēd*, *tēd*, *quoius*, *-os* for *-us*, oaths.
- **Ennius and early tragedy**, known mostly from quotations in Cicero and the grammarians: *noenum*.
- **Legal and religious formulas**, which kept old forms long after they had gone from speech: *duim* for *dem* "may I give", "retained in religious formulas and often in comedy" (ag1903 §183.2).
- **Classical poets using old forms** now and then: the genitive in *-āī* (*aulāī*, ag1903 §603a), *-ier* infinitives (§183). You met *superum* and *deum* in lesson 9.
- **Spelling in classical texts**: Latin spelling "was never absolutely settled"; *lubet*, *vortō*, *optumus* stand beside the later *libet*, *vertō*, *optimus* (ag1903 §6), and editions differ.
- **Early syntax**: indirect questions often in the indicative (ag1903 §575c); *cum* "since, although" with the indicative (§549 note 3); verbs like *ūtor* with the accusative (§410 note 1).
- **Colloquial Latin**: Plautus's slaves, Petronius's freedmen, Pompeii's graffiti (part 8), and later the Vulgate (lesson 13): diminutives, contractions, *bellus*, *caballus*, short clauses, masculine for neuter.

## Watch out

**Do not "correct" an old form.** *Siet* in Plautus is not a mistake for *sit*; *Gnaivod* is not a misspelling. Read them as the older forms of the same words.

**Do not assume every *-os* is accusative plural.** *Salvos*, *servos*, *equos* in Plautus are often nominative singular for *salvus*, *servus*, *equus*. Check the grammar of the sentence.

**Do not read *-ier* as a separate word or a typo.** *Adferrier* is one form, the passive infinitive. Strip *-ier*, add *-ī*: *adferrī*.

**Do not take *ted* and *med* for words you have not met.** They are *tē* and *mē* with an old *-d*.

**Do not read *faxō* as a present.** It is a future perfect, "I shall have done", used for "I'll make sure" (ag1903 §183.3).

**Do not add a missing *-m* in your head as if it were sounded fully.** Inscriptions leave it out because it was weak (part 1). In verse it elides (lesson 8).

**Do not imitate these forms in classical Latin.** *\*Hominem dīvitem ted esse putō* is Plautine, not Ciceronian. Write *tē*.

**Do not treat colloquial Latin as bad Latin.** Petronius writes freedmen's Latin on purpose. Where it differs from Cicero (*vīnus*, *balneus*), it shows how real people spoke, and it often points towards the Romance languages.

## Connections

French *beau*, *belle* and Spanish *caballo* come from colloquial words, *bellus* and *caballus*, rather than from literary *pulcher* and *equus* (Wiktionary). That is a general lesson: the Romance languages grew out of spoken Latin, so the Latin of Plautus's slaves and Petronius's freedmen is often closer to Spanish and French than Cicero's is.

## Exercises

1. Give the classical forms of *oino*, *ploirume*, *duonoro*, *optumo* and *viro*.
2. What is the case of *Gnaivod*, and what is its classical form?
3. Give the classical forms of *siet*, *adferrier* and *ted*.
4. Translate *Salvos sīs* and say what *salvos* stands for.
5. In *familiāris accipiēre faxō*, explain *accipiēre* and *faxō*.
6. Why do old inscriptions often write *viro* for *virum*?
7. Which features of *Vīnus mihi in cerebrum abiit* are colloquial?
8. Rewrite *Honc oino ploirume cosentiont … duonoro optumo fuise viro* in classical spelling.
9. Translate *Quoius forma virtutei parisuma fuit.*

## Worked answers

1. *Ūnum*, *plūrimī*, *bonōrum*, *optimum*, *virum*. *Oi* became *ū*; *duonus* is the old word for *bonus*; final *-m* is restored (Wiktionary, *oinos*, *duonus*; part 1).
2. Ablative singular, with the old ending *-d*; classical *Gnaeō* (ag1903 §49e).
3. *Sit* (ag1903 §170 note), *afferrī* (§183.4), *tē* (Wiktionary).
4. "Be safe", a greeting. *Salvos* is the old spelling of *salvus*.
5. *Accipiēre* is the future passive, second person singular, with *-re* for *-ris* (lesson 9). *Faxō* is an old future perfect of *faciō*, "I shall have done", meaning "I'll see to it" (ag1903 §183.3).
6. Final *-m* was weakly pronounced, so it was often not written (part 1; the same weakness lets it elide in verse, lesson 8).
7. The masculine *vīnus* for neuter *vīnum*, and the everyday phrasing; *in cerebrum abiit* "went to my head" is a conversational image.
8. *Hunc ūnum plūrimī cōnsentiunt … bonōrum optimum fuisse virum.*
9. "Whose looks were fully equal to his courage." *Quoius* = *cuius*; *virtutei* = *virtūtī*, dative after "equal"; *parisuma* = *parissima*.
