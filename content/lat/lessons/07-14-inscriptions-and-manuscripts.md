## The idea

Everything you have read in this guide was printed by a modern editor: lower case, word spaces, punctuation, *u* and *v* told apart, macrons added (part 1). The Romans wrote none of that. Their texts survive in two main forms, and each needs its own reading skill.

**Inscriptions** are texts cut in stone or bronze, painted on walls or scratched on objects. They are the Latin that ordinary Romans actually saw: tombstones, dedications, laws, building records. They are written in capitals, often with dots between words, and they are heavily abbreviated, because cutting letters is slow and space is short. **Epigraphy** is the study of inscriptions. The great collection is the *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum* (CIL), cited by volume and number: CIL XIII 8648 is number 8648 in volume 13 (dossier §7).

**Manuscripts** are books copied by hand, and almost all classical literature reached us that way, through medieval copies. Scribes saved time and parchment with their own system of abbreviations: a stroke over a vowel for a missing *m* or *n*, special signs for *per*, *pro*, *quod*, *et*.

Both kinds of text reach you through an **edition**: a modern printed text in which an editor has expanded the abbreviations, filled gaps where possible, and marked what he has done with brackets. The signs are standard, known as the **Leiden conventions** from the meeting in Leiden in 1931 that agreed them. Learn them and you can always tell what is on the stone and what the editor has added.

The abbreviations follow one logic: a word is cut down to its first letter or letters when the reader can be trusted to know it. Formulas that appear on every tombstone (*Dīs Mānibus*) shrink to initials (*D M*); names shrink to their praenomen letters (lesson 11); common words shrink to their first syllable (*leg.* for *legiō*, *ann.* for *annōrum*).

## How it is built

**The editorial signs** (Leiden conventions):

- ***a(bc)***: an abbreviation expanded by the editor. The stone has *a*; the editor adds *bc*. *F(ilio)*: the stone has *F*.
- ***[abc]***: letters lost (the stone is broken or worn) and restored by the editor. *[ce]cidit*: only *CIDIT* survives.
- ***[…]*** or ***[- - -]***: letters lost and not restored.
- ***⟨abc⟩***: letters the carver or scribe left out by mistake, added by the editor: *aedifica⟨vi⟩ssem*.
- ***{abc}***: letters the editor thinks were cut by mistake and should be ignored.
- ***⟦abc⟧***: letters deliberately erased in antiquity (for example the name of a condemned emperor), still legible.
- ***ạ*** (dot under a letter): a letter damaged or unclear.

**Reading an inscription.**

1. Read the edited text, ignoring brackets: *M. Caelio T. f. Lem. Bon.*
2. Expand each abbreviation from the parentheses: *Mārcō Caeliō Titī fīliō Lemōniā (tribū) Bonōniā*.
3. Work out the grammar. Tombstones are usually in the dative ("to Marcus Caelius") or the nominative ("Marcus Caelius lies here"), with the person who set up the stone in the nominative.
4. Translate the formulas as formulas: *H. S. E.* is a fixed phrase, not a sentence to analyse.

**Common inscription abbreviations.**

- *D. M.* *Dīs Mānibus* "to the spirits of the dead", heading tombstones (Wiktionary); later *D. M. S.*, with *sacrum* "sacred".
- *H. S. E.* *hīc situs est* "here he lies" or *hīc sepultus est* "here is buried": English Wikipedia's List of classical Latin abbreviations gives both, Wiktionary only the second.
- *S. T. T. L.* *sit tibi terra levis* "may the earth lie light on you" (Wiktionary has the phrase).
- *V. A.* *vīxit annōs* "he lived … years"; *ann.* *annōrum*.
- *F.* *fīlius*, *fīlia*, or *fēcit*; *L.* *lībertus* "freedman" after a name.
- *F. C.* *faciendum cūrāvit* "saw to its making" (lesson 2).
- *V. S. L. M.* *vōtum solvit libēns meritō* "paid his vow gladly and deservedly", on dedications.
- *D. D.* *dōnum dedit* "gave as a gift".
- *S. P. Q. R.* *senātus populusque Rōmānus* (Wiktionary).
- *IMP.* *imperātor*, *COS.* *cōnsul*, *AVG.* *Augustus*, *LEG.* *legiō*, *TR. POT.* *tribūniciā potestāte*.

English Wikipedia's List of classical Latin abbreviations gives *V. A.*, *F. C.*, *V. S. L. M.* and *S. T. T. L.* as above, and also other readings for two of them: *V. A.* can be *veterānō assignātus*, and *F. C.* *fierī cūrāvit*, with the same meaning. On a tombstone the context decides. This guide has not checked them against the CIL indexes.

**Common manuscript abbreviations** (Wiktionary):

- a bar over a vowel for a following *m* or *n*: *dñs* for *dominus*, *factū* for *factum*;
- *ꝑ* (p with a stroke through the descender) for *per*; *ꝓ* (p with a loop) for *pro*;
- *ꝙ* for *quod*, *quī* or *quae*; *ꝗ* for *quam* or *quan-*;
- *⁊*, the Tironian sign, for *et*; the ampersand *&* is also a ligature of *et* (Wiktionary);
- contractions keeping the first and last letters: *dns* *dominus*, *eps* *episcopus*.

## Where it applies

- **Tombstones**: *D. M.*, the dead in the dative or nominative, age (*vīxit annōs*), the person who set the stone up (*fēcit*, *posuit*), *H. S. E.*, *S. T. T. L.*
- **Dedications to gods**: the god in the dative, the dedicator in the nominative, *V. S. L. M.*, *D. D.*
- **Building inscriptions and honours**: emperors' titles in long abbreviated strings, *IMP. CAESAR … AVG. … TR. POT. … COS.*
- **Military records**: *LEG.*, legion numbers, *O.* or *ORD.* for rank, as on the Caelius stone.
- **Walls and objects**: painted notices and graffiti (part 8, reading 1), often in colloquial spelling.
- **Manuscripts of classical authors**: medieval copies with nasal bars, *ꝑ*, *ꝓ*, *⁊*; editions expand them silently, so you meet them only in facsimiles.
- **Medieval documents**: charters and letters in the same abbreviations, with medieval spelling (lesson 13).
- **Editions of papyri** use the same system; the Leiden meeting covered papyrology as well as epigraphy.

## Watch out

**Do not read the editor's additions as the stone's text.** *[Ce]cidit* means the first two letters are lost. If you are building an argument on a word, check whether it is in brackets.

**Do not confuse ( ) with [ ].** Parentheses expand an abbreviation the stone really has; square brackets restore letters that are gone.

**Do not expand an abbreviation without checking the grammar.** *F.* can be *fīlius*, *fīliō* or *fēcit*; the case of the name it goes with decides. On the Caelius stone the dead man's *f(ilio)* is dative, his brother's *f(ilius)* nominative.

**Do not read inscriptional numerals as modern ones.** *XIIX* is 18, *IIX* is 8: subtraction can be by two (lesson 11).

**Do not expect classical spelling on stone.** *Vivos*, *heic*, *deico*, *pulcrai* are older or popular spellings (lesson 12), not errors of the editor.

**Do not "correct" what the editor marked with { } or ⟨ ⟩ back again.** Those brackets record a mistake by the ancient writer and the editor's judgement; read the corrected form, but know it is a correction.

**Do not take a manuscript bar for a macron.** In *dñs* or *factū* the stroke means a missing *m* or *n*; it says nothing about vowel length.

## Connections

English still writes Latin abbreviations without expanding them: *e.g.* (*exemplī grātiā*), *i.e.* (*id est*), *etc.* (*et cētera*, Wiktionary), *R.I.P.* (*requiēscat in pāce*). The ampersand *&* is a written *et*.

## Exercises

1. Expand and translate *D. M. S.*
2. Expand *M. Caelius M. l.* and say what *l.* means.
3. In *[Ce]cidit*, what do the brackets tell you?
4. Write *aedificassem* as an editor would if the carver had written *aedificasem*.
5. Translate *V. S. L. M.* in a dedication to Jupiter.
6. Expand *dñs* and *ꝓ patre*.
7. On the Caelius stone, why is it *f(ilio)* the first time and *f(ilius)* the second?
8. Expand and translate *H. S. E.*
9. Read *leg. XIIX* and *ann. LIII s.*

## Worked answers

1. *Dīs Mānibus sacrum*: "sacred to the spirits of the dead" (Wiktionary gives *D. M.*; *sacrum* is added on many later stones).
2. *Mārcus Caelius Mārcī lībertus*: "Marcus Caelius, freedman of Marcus". *L.* after a name is *lībertus*. The two freedmen named beside Caelius's portrait are both *M. Caelius*, one of them *M. l.*
3. The letters *CE* are lost on the stone and restored by the editor; only *CIDIT* is legible.
4. *Aedifica⟨s⟩sem*: angle brackets add a letter the carver left out. Curly brackets would mean the opposite, a letter cut by mistake.
5. "He paid his vow willingly and deservedly" (to Jupiter, who would be in the dative).
6. *Dominus*; *prō patre* "for (his) father". The bar stands for the missing *-ominu-*, and *ꝓ* for *pro* (Wiktionary).
7. The dead man is in the dative ("to Marcus Caelius, son of Titus"), so his *fīliō* agrees; his brother is the subject of *fēcit*, so *fīlius* (lesson 11; part 3).
8. *Hīc situs est* (or, as Wiktionary gives it, *hīc sepultus est*): "here he lies (is buried)".
9. *Legiōnis duodēvīcēsimae* "of the Eighteenth Legion"; *annōrum LIII sēmissis* "aged 53 and a half".
