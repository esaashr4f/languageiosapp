## About these texts

Pompeii was buried by the eruption of Vesuvius in AD 79, and its walls kept thousands of painted notices and scratched messages. They are the nearest thing we have to the voices of ordinary Romans: election posters, jokes, insults, love notes, dates and accounts. Their Latin is the spoken Latin of a small Italian town, written by people who had learned to write but not to spell like Cicero.

The texts below are as printed by The Latin Library in its selection *Inscriptiones parietariae*, with its numbering; one number from the *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum* (CIL IV, the Pompeii volume) is added where two sources agree. They keep the writers' spellings. As with the inscriptions of part 7, lesson 14, they are printed without macrons, and the morphemes follow the spelling on the wall. Each note gives the classical form.

Use what you learned in part 7, lessons 12 and 14. Expect:

- final *-t* and *-m* dropped: *ama* for *amat*;
- *e* for *ae* and *i*: *que* for *quae*, *vedes* for *vendis*;
- one letter for two: *bela* for *bella*;
- *cum* with the accusative instead of the ablative;
- abbreviations in election notices, expanded in parentheses.

morphemes: Gai-um Cuspi-um Pans-am aedil-em dign-um re-i public-ae or-o vos faci-a-tis Saturnin-us cum disc-ent-es rog-ant

translation: Please elect Gaius Cuspius Pansa aedile, a man worthy of the state. Saturninus and his pupils ask it.
note: An election poster painted by a schoolmaster and his class. The formula o. v. f. is orō vōs faciātis "I beg you to make (him)", a subjunctive without ut (part 6). cum discentes has the accusative where classical Latin needs cum discentibus, and the verb is plural because the meaning is "Saturninus and his pupils". [p] is restored.
source: The Latin Library, Inscriptiones parietariae, Pompeii no. 172
confidence: attested
:::

:::example
orig: Quisquis ama valia, peria qui nosci amare, bis tanti peria, quisquis amare vota.
morphemes: quisquis ama valia peria qui nosci amare bis tanti peria quisquis amare vota
gloss: whoever.NOM.SG love.3SG fare.well.SBJV.3SG perish.SBJV.3SG REL.NOM.SG.M not.know.3SG love.INF twice so.much.GEN perish.SBJV.3SG whoever.NOM.SG love.INF forbid.3SG
translation: Good luck to anyone who loves; death to anyone who doesn't know how; and twice over death to anyone who forbids love.
note: An elegiac couplet in street spelling. Classical: quisquis amat valeat, pereat quī nescit amāre; bis tantī pereat quisquis amāre vetat. Final -t has gone (ama, valia, peria, nosci, vota), and e before a vowel is written i (valia for valeat). bis tantī is a genitive of value, "at twice the price". The wall goes on with more lines, not given here.
source: The Latin Library, Inscriptiones parietariae, Pompeii no. 594; CIL IV 1173
confidence: attested
:::

:::example
orig: X k. Febrarias Ursa peperit diem Iovis.
morphemes: X Kalendas Febrari-as Urs-a peper-it di-em Iov-is
gloss: 10 Kalends.ACC.PL February-ACC.PL.F Ursa-NOM.SG give.birth.PRF-3SG day-ACC.SG Jupiter-GEN.SG
translation: On 23 January Ursa gave birth; it was a Thursday.
note: A household note. X k. Febrarias = ante diem decimum Kalendās Februāriās: January has 31 days, 31 + 2 − 10 = 23 (part 7, lesson 11). ## Reading notes

**The election poster** shows how much a Roman reader filled in. Nine of its words are abbreviations, and every voter could expand *O V F* at a glance. Read the expanded version first, then the abbreviated one, until the letters alone carry the words.

**The love couplet** is a hexameter and a pentameter, though the spelling hides it: restore the classical forms before you try to scan. The writers knew the shape of elegy (part 7, lesson 9) even if they spelled by ear. The dropped *-t* has a parallel in Spanish and Italian *ama* "he loves".

**The innkeeper couplet** has a regular hexameter once *medacia* is read as *mendācia* and *copo* as *caupō*. Its pentameter does not scan even in classical spelling: the second half must open with a long syllable, and *bibis* opens with two short ones. Verse on walls is often rough.

**The date note** mixes the Roman calendar with a named day of the week. Wiktionary derives French *jeudi* from *Iovis diēs*. How the seven-day week came into Roman use is not explained here.

**What to take away.** None of these writers is careless. They write the Latin they spoke, and that Latin is already moving towards Italian and Spanish: final consonants weakening, *e* and *i* merging before vowels, cases replaced by prepositions with one all-purpose case. Classical spelling, which you have learned, is the school standard they were half aware of.

## Questions

1. Write the election poster in full classical form without abbreviations.
2. Which three spellings in *quisquis ama valia* show the loss of final *-t*?
3. In the election poster, what case would classical Latin use after *cum*?
4. Convert *X k. Febrarias* to a modern date and show the working.
5. In the innkeeper couplet, give the classical spelling of *medacia*, *copo*, *vedes* and *acuam*.

## Answers

1. *Gāium Cuspium Pansam aedīlem dignum reī pūblicae ōrō vōs faciātis. Saturnīnus cum discentibus rogant.*
2. *Ama* (*amat*), *valia* (*valeat*), *peria* (*pereat*); also *nosci* and *vota*.
3. The ablative: *cum discentibus* (part 3). The poster has *cum discentes*, an accusative.
4. 31 days in January; 31 + 2 − 10 = 23 January (part 7, lesson 11).
5. *Mendācia*, *caupō*, *vendis*, *aquam*.
