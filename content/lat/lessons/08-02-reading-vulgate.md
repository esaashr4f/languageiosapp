## About this text

The opening of Genesis in the Vulgate, the standard Latin Bible of the Western Church (part 7, lesson 13), is the right second reading because its sentences are short and its grammar plain. Its style is easy to hear: clauses joined by *et* and *-que*, the same words repeated, the main verb early.

The wording is from The Latin Library's text of the Vulgate, in this guide's spelling: the edition prints *cælum* and *tenebræ*, which become *caelum* and *tenebrae*, and macrons are added. One word has a disputed length: Wiktionary marks the *i* of *principium* as either long or short; it is printed short here.

Before you start, recall:

- word order: the Vulgate often puts the verb first (*creāvit Deus*, *dīxit Deus*); why is not explained here;
- the passive of *ferō*: *ferēbātur* "was being carried, was moving" (part 4, lesson 14);
- jussive *fīat* "let there be" (part 4, lesson 19);
- *quod* + subjunctive after a verb of seeing, where Cicero would use an accusative and infinitive (part 6, lesson 5; part 7, lesson 13);
- *factum est* "it happened, it came to be" (part 7, lesson 13).

## Reading notes

**Parataxis.** Almost every clause begins with *et* or ends in *-que*. **Parataxis** is placing clauses side by side, joined by "and" or by nothing, instead of subordinating one to another. Compare Caesar, who would have written something like "When God had said 'let there be light', light was made" with a *cum* clause (part 6, lesson 12). In the Vulgate "and … and … and" carries the story.

**Late features, but few.** The only construction here that Cicero would not use is *vīdit … quod esset bona*. Everything else, from the imperfects of description to the jussive *fīat*, is classical.

**Words worth noticing.** *Spīritus* is "breath", "wind" and "spirit" at once; the translation must choose. *Abyssus* is a Greek word for the primeval waters. *Caelum* is singular here; the Vulgate later uses the plural *caelī*, as in *Pater noster, quī es in caelīs* (part 7, lesson 13).

**Reading aloud.** In the ecclesiastical pronunciation (part 7, lesson 13), *caelum* is [ˈtʃɛːlum]; in the classical one, [ˈkae̯lũː] (Wiktionary).

**Building verse 4 step by step.** Start from the main verb, *vīdit* "he saw". Its subject is *Deus*; its object *lūcem*. Then *quod esset bona* adds what God saw about the light: *bona* is feminine to agree with *lūx*, and nominative because it is the complement of *esset*. Next comes a second main verb, *dīvīsit*, with the same subject and a new object, and *ā tenebrīs* gives the separation (part 5, lesson 8). Two short clauses, each complete, joined by *et*.

## Questions

1. Why is it *tenebrae erant* and not *tenebrae erat*?
2. Rewrite *vīdit Deus lūcem quod esset bona* in classical style.
3. What mood and tense is *fīat*, and why is it used?
4. Find the two accusatives after *appellāvit*.
5. Translate *factumque est vespere et māne, diēs secundus* (Genesis 1.8).

## Answers

1. *Tenebrae* has no singular (part 2, lesson 4); a plural subject takes a plural verb.
2. *Vīdit Deus lūcem bonam esse* (part 6, lesson 5).
3. Present subjunctive of *fīō*, used as a command in the third person, "let there be" (part 4, lesson 19).
4. *Lūcem … Diem* and *tenebrās … Noctem*: the thing named and the name (part 3).
5. "And there was evening and morning, the second day."
