## About this text

Caesar wrote the *Commentāriī dē Bellō Gallicō*, his own account of his campaigns in Gaul, in the third person and in deliberately plain Latin. Its first chapter is a short geography lesson: who lives where, and why the Belgians and the Helvetii are the toughest. It is the passage generations of learners began with, and you can now read all of it.

The wording is from The Latin Library, in this guide's spelling, with macrons. Section numbers follow The Latin Library. This reading covers sections 1 to 4; sections 5 to 7 give the boundaries of each region and are left for you to read on your own.

Allen and Greenough use this chapter to show Latin word order: the important word first, the verb last, and a chiasmus near the end (ag1903 §597a; part 7, lesson 6).

## Reading notes

**Topic first.** Each sentence starts with what it is about: *Gallia*, *hī omnēs*, *Gallōs*, *hōrum omnium*, *quā dē causā*. Allen and Greenough render the opening with stress on "Gaul", "divided" and "parts" to show this (ag1903 §597a).

**One long sentence.** Sections 3 and 4 are a single sentence in The Latin Library's punctuation, held together by *-que* and relatives: the Belgians are bravest *because* they are far from civilisation, *and* traders seldom come, *and* they are next to the Germans, *with whom* they fight. Read it in the four pieces above, then all at once.

**Chiasmus.** In section 3, *longissimē absunt … minimē … commeant* puts the superlative adverbs at the edges; Allen and Greenough point out the crossed order of *minimē* and the following clause (ag1903 §597a, notes 16–17).

**Caesar's plainness.** There is not one rare word in the passage except the names. The difficulty is all in the structure, and the structure is the grammar of parts 3 to 6. If any sentence stopped you, go back to the lesson its note names.

**How to tackle the long sentence.** Find the main clause first: *fortissimī sunt Belgae*. Then ask what each following piece hangs on. *Proptereā quod* opens a reason, and its verb is *absunt*. The next *-que* adds a second verb to the same reason clause (*commeant*), with its own subject *mercātōrēs*; *atque* then adds a third (*important*). The last *-que*, on *proximī*, adds a further reason with the indicative *sunt*: "and (because) they are nearest". The relative *quī* and then *quibuscum* attach to *Germānīs*. Mark each verb and its subject, and the sentence falls into place.

## Questions

1. What does *omnis* add in the first sentence?
2. In *quārum ūnam incolunt Belgae*, what is the case of *quārum* and why?
3. Why is *dīvidit* singular in the third sentence?
4. Explain *ad effēminandōs animōs*.
5. Translate sections 1.1.5–7 on your own with a dictionary for the place names.

## Answers

1. It marks Gaul "as a whole", the wide sense of *Gallia*, as opposed to the part where the Gauls proper live (ag1903 §597a, note 2).
2. Genitive plural, partitive: "of which (parts) one" (part 5, lesson 9).
3. It stands next to *Sēquana* and agrees with that nearest noun; in the first half it is understood with *Garumna flūmen*, also singular.
4. The gerundive with *ad* for purpose, "for weakening minds" (part 7, lesson 2).
5. Open answer; check each relative clause and each ablative against parts 5 and 6.
