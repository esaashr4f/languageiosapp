## About these texts

Catullus wrote short poems in the generation of Cicero and Caesar. Many are addressed to a woman he calls Lesbia. Poem 85 is a single elegiac couplet (part 7, lesson 9); poem 5 is in **hendecasyllables**, a lyric line of eleven syllables that Allen and Greenough call the Phalaecean (ag1903 §625.11). The wording is from The Latin Library, in this guide's spelling, with macrons.

**The hendecasyllable** is not built from repeated feet like the hexameter. It is a fixed sequence of eleven syllables. In every line of poem 5 that is scanned below, the pattern is:

— — — ∪ ∪ — ∪ — ∪ — —

Read it as a run of three heavy syllables, two light, then heavy and light alternating to the end; the last syllable, as always, is anceps (part 7, lesson 8). Elision works as in the hexameter.

**Reading 85.** Every verb is first person except *requīris*: the poem is about the speaker's own state. The only verbs that are not active are *fierī*, which has no active, and *excrucior*, "I am tortured": the last word is passive because the feeling happens to him.

## Reading notes

**Two moods, two moments.** Poem 5 begins in the subjunctive (*vīvāmus*, *amēmus*, *aestimēmus*: let us), turns to the indicative for a fact (*sōlēs … possunt*), then to an imperative (*dā*), then to the future (*conturbābimus*). Track the moods and you track the argument: exhortation, reason, demand, plan.

**Numbers.** *Mīlle* is indeclinable in the singular; *mīlia*, a neuter plural noun, is used for thousands (part 5). Catullus piles numerals without verbs: *dā* governs every one of them.

**Why muddle the count?** The last lines connect knowing the number with the power of an envious person; the poem does not say more, and this guide does not explain the belief behind it.

**Scanning poem 5 for yourself.** Lines 2, 4 to 6, 8 and 9, and 11 to 13 follow the same pattern. Mark long vowels, apply position, make the elisions (*occidere et*, *perpetua ūna*, *mīlle altera*, *deinde usque altera*), and check against — — — ∪ ∪ — ∪ — ∪ — —.

**Line 5 scanned.** *Nōbīs cum semel occidit brevis lūx* divides as *nō·bīs·cum·se·me·loc·ci·dit·bre·vis·lūx*. *Nō*, *bīs* have long vowels; *cum* is closed by *m* + *s*; *se* and *me* are open with short vowels, since the *l* of *semel* moves on to *occidit*; *loc* is closed; *ci* is light; *dit* is closed by *t* + *b*; *bre* is light, because *b* + *r* can both start the next syllable; *vis* is closed by *s* + *l*. The result is the same eleven-syllable shape as line 1.

## Questions

1. Why is *ōdī* translated as a present?
2. In 85.2, which word is elided and before what?
3. Name the construction of *vīvāmus* and *amēmus*.
4. Explain *nōbīs … dormienda*.
5. Why are *sciāmus* and *possit* subjunctive?
6. Scan line 2 of poem 5, *rūmōrēsque senum sevēriōrum*.

## Answers

1. *Ōdī* is a perfect in form but present in meaning, like *meminī* (part 4).
2. *Sentiō*, before *et*: *senti' et*.
3. Hortatory subjunctives, "let us" (part 4, lesson 19).
4. *Nox … dormienda* is a gerundive of obligation, "a night to be slept"; *nōbīs* is the dative of the agent (part 7, lesson 2).
5. They are in purpose clauses introduced by *nē* (part 6, lesson 7).
6. *rū·mō·rēs·que·se·num·se·vē·ri·ō·rum*: — — — ∪ ∪ — ∪ — ∪ — —. *Num* is heavy because *m* + *s* follow; *ri* is light, a vowel before a vowel.
