## About this text

Phaedrus put Aesop's fables into Latin verse. In his prologue he says so himself: *Aesōpus auctor quam māteriam repperit, hanc ego polīvī versibus sēnāriīs* "the material that Aesop the author found, I have polished in senarii". The **senarius**, or iambic trimeter, is a line of six iambic feet, ∪ — (bennett1918 §370), with many substitutions allowed; this reading does not scan it, because its freedoms need more than this guide teaches.

The first fable, *Lupus et Agnus*, is fifteen lines: a story in thirteen, a moral in two. Its Latin is plain and its grammar classical: direct speech with *inquit*, a few participles, and a relative clause in the moral. The wording is from The Latin Library, in this guide's spelling, with macrons; line divisions follow the edition.

## Reading notes

**The shape of a fable.** Scene (lines 1–3), accusation (4–6), reply (6–8), second accusation (9–10), reply (11), third accusation and the end (12–13), moral (14–15). The wolf's charges grow less plausible each time; the lamb's replies are always true; truth makes no difference.

**Speech in verse.** *Inquit* and *ait* are tucked inside the quoted words, after the first word or phrase (ag1903 §599c). Find the words of the speaker first, then the narrative frame.

**Names that are descriptions.** The wolf is *latrō*, the lamb *lāniger*. Phaedrus names each animal by a quality instead of repeating the noun.

**Sounds of the senarius.** Even without scanning, read the lines aloud and listen for the rising pattern, light then heavy, that the iambic line is built on (bennett1918 §370).

**Building line 8 from meaning to form.** The lamb wants to say "the water runs down from you to where I drink". The verb is *dēcurrō* "run down", a compound of *currō* with *dē-* "down from" (part 4, lesson 16); present, third person singular, *dēcurrit*. The subject, "the water", is *liquor*, a poetic word for any liquid; it stands last for emphasis. "From you": *ā tē*. "To my drinkings": *ad meōs haustūs*, a fourth-declension noun from *hauriō* "draw (water)", in the accusative plural after *ad*. The plural is poetic (part 7, lesson 9): it means the place where he drinks.

**Verb tenses as storytelling.** The narrative runs in perfects (*intulit*, *respondit*) and one vivid present at the climax (*lacerat*). The characters' speeches use the perfect for what happened (*fēcistī*, *dīxistī*) and the imperfect *eram* for a state in the past. Each tense does exactly what parts 4 and 6 taught.

## Questions

1. Why is *vēnerant* pluperfect?
2. What is the case and reason of *sitī*?
3. In *turbulentam fēcistī … aquam*, what is the function of *turbulentam*?
4. What does the lamb's answer in lines 7–8 depend on?
5. Why does the wolf switch to the lamb's father?
6. Translate the moral.

## Answers

1. The arrival is already complete when the story begins (part 4, lesson 8).
2. Ablative of cause, with *compulsī*: "driven by thirst" (part 5, lesson 8).
3. A predicate adjective after *faciō*: "made the water muddy" (part 3).
4. On lines 2–3: the wolf stands upstream, so water cannot flow from the lamb to him.
5. The lamb's reply, "I was not yet born", has refuted the second charge, so the wolf invents a third; the moral explains why.
6. "This fable was written for those who crush the innocent on invented pretexts."
