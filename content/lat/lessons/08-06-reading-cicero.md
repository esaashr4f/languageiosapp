## About this text

In 63 BC Cicero was consul (ag1903 §630) and Lucius Sergius Catilina, as the speech itself says, was leading a conspiracy (*coniūrātiō*). Cicero spoke against him in the senate with Catiline present; the speech opens without any of the usual courtesies, with a volley of questions. It is the most famous opening in Roman oratory, and it shows a style very different from Caesar's: questions, repetitions, exclamations, short clauses hammered one after another.

The wording is from The Latin Library, in this guide's spelling, with macrons; *voltus* is modernised to *vultūs* (dossier §4). Square brackets in the last sentence are the edition's: it marks *omnēs iam diū* as words it doubts.

You met the first sentence in part 4. Now read it in context.

## Reading notes

**Questions as attack.** The first section contains six questions and no statements. None of them expects an answer; each states an accusation. The second section switches to exclamation and short statements. Read the passage aloud and the change of tempo is obvious.

**Lists with one verb.** The sentence with six *nihil*s holds back its verb, *mōvērunt*, until every subject has been named, a period in the sense of part 7, lesson 6. The sentence of indirect questions does the same with *ignōrāre*.

**Pronouns as weapons.** *Iste*, *istīus* for Catiline, *hic* for the senators present: Cicero points with his pronouns. *Hic tamen vīvit* uses *hic* of Catiline only to make the contrast with the consul's knowledge.

## Questions

1. What is the form *abūtēre*, and why does *patientiā* follow in the ablative?
2. In the *nihil* sentence, what is the subject of *mōvērunt*?
3. Why are *ēgeris*, *fueris*, *convocāveris* and *cēperis* subjunctive?
4. Translate *Ō tempora, ō mōrēs!* and name the case.
5. Why is *oportēbat* imperfect, though the English says "ought to have"?

## Answers

1. Future indicative, second person singular of the deponent *abūtor*, with *-re* for *-ris*; *abūtor* takes the ablative (part 4, lesson 13; part 7, lesson 9).
2. All six nouns together: *praesidium*, *vigiliae*, *timor*, *concursus*, *locus*, *ōra vultūsque*; the plural verb agrees with the whole list.
3. They are indirect questions depending on *ignōrāre* (part 6, lesson 11).
4. "Oh the times, oh the morals!" Accusative of exclamation (part 2).
5. Latin puts the past on the verb of obligation and uses a present infinitive (ag1903 §486a; part 7, lesson 4).
