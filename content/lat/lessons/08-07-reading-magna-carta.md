## About this text

Magna Carta is the charter of King John of England, which its heading calls an agreement (*concordia*) between the king and his barons; The Latin Library's heading gives the date as *15° Junii anno Domini 1215*. Its clauses 39 and 40 are the most quoted: they promise that no free man will be punished except by lawful judgement, and that justice will not be sold, denied or delayed. They are a good first medieval document because the grammar is classical and the sentences short; the difficulty is in the vocabulary of medieval law and in the spelling.

The wording is from The Latin Library, which follows the Lincoln copy and adds the clause numbers (the original has none, as the edition notes). The text keeps its medieval spelling and is printed without macrons (part 7, lesson 13): *terre* for *terrae*, *justiciam* for *iūstitiam*, *j* for consonantal *i*.

Three verbs in clause 39 are medieval legal words not found in classical Latin, and Wiktionary has no Latin entries for them: *imprisonare*, *disseisire*, *utlagare*. Their meanings are given below from the English words that continue them (*imprison*, *disseise* "dispossess", *outlaw*); this guide has not checked them in a dictionary of medieval Latin.

:::example

## Reading notes

**Legal style.** The charter piles up near-synonyms: six verbs for punishment in clause 39, three for denying justice in clause 40. The grammar that holds them together is classical: one subject, a row of subjunctives joined by *vel* and *aut*, and a closing *nisi*.

**Jussive and future.** Clause 39 uses the jussive subjunctive for a general rule (*nūllus … capiātur*) and then switches to the future indicative for the king's own promise (*ibimus*, *mittēmus*). Clause 40 keeps the future. The change of mood marks the change from rule to undertaking.

**Spelling.** Every word ending in classical *-ae* here ends in *-e*: *terre*, *Anglie*, *Hibernie*. Read them as genitives.

**Building clause 40 from meaning to form.** The king wants to promise three things about justice. "We will sell": future of *vendō*, a third-conjugation verb, so *-ē-* + *-mus*: *vendēmus*. "We will deny": *negō*, first conjugation, *-bi-*: *negābimus*. "We will delay": *differō*, an irregular compound of *ferō*, but its future is regular: *differēmus*. "To no one": the dative *nūllī*, because selling and denying are done *to* someone (part 3). The objects, "right or justice", are accusatives shared by all three verbs, so they come once, after the last of them.

## Questions

1. What mood is *capiatur*, and why?
2. Which word is the object of *vendemus*, *negabimus* and *differemus*?
3. Give the classical spellings of *terre*, *justiciam* and *gracia*.
4. What does *nisi per legale judicium parium suorum* add?
5. Convert the date in the dating clause to day and month, and compare it with the edition's heading.

## Answers

1. Present subjunctive passive, jussive: "let no free man be taken" (part 4, lesson 19).
2. *Rectum aut justiciam*, shared by all three.
3. *Terrae*, *iūstitiam*, *grātia* (part 7, lesson 13).
4. The only exception: punishment is allowed only by the judgement of his equals or by the law of the land.
5. *Quinto decimo die junii*: 15 June. The heading gives *15° Junii*, the same day.
