## About this text

The Aeneid tells how Aeneas, a Trojan prince, escaped the fall of Troy and after years of wandering and war founded the line that led to Rome. Its first eleven lines are two sentences. The first (lines 1 to 7) states the subject: a man, his sufferings, and what came of them, ending on the walls of Rome. The second (lines 8 to 11) asks the Muse why a goddess, Juno, persecuted him.

You have already met most of these lines one at a time: line 1 in part 4, line 3 in part 4 and part 7, line 4 in part 7, lesson 6, and lines 1, 2, 3, 7 and 11 scanned in part 7, lesson 8. Here they come together.

The wording and the macrons are those of The Latin Library's edition of Aeneid 1, which prints macrons itself. Three words differ from this guide's usual spelling: the edition has *Trōiae*, *inferretque* and *īnsīgnem*, where Wiktionary, which the guide follows, has *Troia* (its *Trōia* is a form of the adjective *Trōius*), *īnferō* and *īnsignis* (part 7, lessons 2 and 8); the guide's forms are used below. Each note gives the scansion; — is a heavy syllable, ∪ a light one, ‖ the main caesura, which for lines 1 to 7 is placed where Allen and Greenough place it (ag1903 §615d).

## Reading the whole

**The architecture of lines 1 to 7.** *Arma virumque canō* announces the theme. Everything after is one relative clause about the man: *quī … vēnit* (lines 1–3), *multum ille … iactātus* (line 3), *multa … passus* (line 5), each "much" balancing the other, then *dum conderet … īnferretque* (lines 5–6), the goal. *Unde* (line 6) opens the result: the Latin race, the Alban fathers, the walls of Rome. The verb of the main clause, *canō*, came in the first line; the sentence then grows by adding what it sings.

**The second sentence.** The poet turns to the Muse with an imperative, *memorā*, and two indirect questions, *quō nūmine laesō* and *quid dolēns*, which ask the same thing twice: what offended Juno? Their verb, *impulerit*, is held back to the first word of line 11, and its object, *virum*, sits in line 10. Then a direct question, short and complete, closes the passage.

**Sound and metre.** Count the spondees: lines 3 and 5 each have three in a row. Line 4 is evenly balanced between dactyls and spondees. Whether such effects are intended is a matter of reading, not of grammar; the scansion itself is not in doubt.

**What you have learned to do.** To read these lines you needed the cases (part 3), the perfect and the participle (part 4), adjectives as nouns and the ablative of respect (part 5), the relative clause and the indirect question (part 6), and the metre, word order and poetic forms of part 7. That is the whole guide, in eleven lines.

## Questions

1. What is the object of *canō*?
2. Where does the relative clause that begins with *quī* end?
3. Find two places where a place to which has no preposition.
4. What do *superum* and *deum* stand for?
5. What governs *volvere* and *adīre*?
6. Why is *impulerit* subjunctive?
7. Scan line 6 without looking at the note.

## Answers

1. *Arma virumque*: "arms and the man".
2. At *Rōmae* (line 7): the clause includes *unde* and its three nominatives.
3. *Ītaliam* (line 2), *Lāvīnia … lītora* (lines 2–3); *Latiō* in line 6 is a dative of direction (part 7, lesson 9).
4. *Superōrum* and *deōrum*, genitive plurals with the poetic *-um* (part 7, lesson 9).
5. *Impulerit* (line 11): "drove the man to roll through … and to undergo …".
6. It is the verb of an indirect question depending on *memorā*, in primary sequence, so perfect subjunctive for a past action (part 6, lessons 6 and 11).
