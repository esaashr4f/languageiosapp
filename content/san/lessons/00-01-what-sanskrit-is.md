## A name that means "perfected"

The word Sanskrit is the Sanskrit adjective *saṃskṛta*. Monier-Williams gives its meanings as "put together", "well or completely formed", "perfected", and, of speech, "refined" or "highly elaborated", as opposed to the everyday languages. The name already tells you the language's history. It is the speech that was worked over, fixed by rule and kept that way.

Sanskrit is an Indo-European language. Glottolog places it in Indo-European, within Indo-Iranian and its Indo-Aryan branch. The Indo-Iranian branch holds the old languages of Iran (Avestan, Old Persian) and of northern India. So Sanskrit is a distant relative of Latin, Greek, the Germanic languages and the Slavonic languages, and a much closer one of Persian.

## Three thousand years, in three layers

The oldest Sanskrit is called **Vedic**, after the Vedas, the sacred collections of hymns and ritual texts. The first and greatest is the *Ṛgveda*, over a thousand hymns and ten thousand verses. Nobody can date it closely. Whitney, writing in 1889, said the hymns "may have been as early as 2000 B.C."; Wiktionary's dated quotations put the Ṛgveda at about 1500 to 1000 BC. Either way it is among the oldest Indo-European texts that survive.

The Vedas were not written down for a long time. They were learnt by heart and recited, school by school, with such care that Whitney calls each school's text "virtually without various readings". Prose explanations of the ritual followed, the *Brāhmaṇas*, and after them the philosophical *Upaniṣads*. Whitney calls the Brāhmaṇa prose "the oldest and most primitive Indo-European prose".

Then came the grammarians. The Vedic schools studied their texts sound by sound, and that study grew into a science of language. Its peak is **Pāṇini**, whose grammar, the *Aṣṭādhyāyī* ("eight chapters"), states the whole language in nearly 4,000 rules, each usually two or three words long. Macdonell calls it "at once the shortest and fullest grammar in the world". Macdonell counts sixty-four earlier grammarians mentioned by name; none of their grammars survives.

Pāṇini's date is not known. Whitney puts him two to four centuries before the Christian era. Macdonell argues from one word: Pāṇini teaches how to form *yavanānī*, which a later commentator explains as "the writing of the Yavanas", the Greeks. Indians in the far north-west would have met Greek writing after Alexander's invasion of 327 BC, so Macdonell finds it "hardly possible" to place Pāṇini earlier than about 300 BC. Both estimates allow the late 4th century BC, the date this guide uses. Treat every date in early Indian history as an estimate: Whitney says such dates are "pins set up to be bowled down again".

The third layer is **Classical Sanskrit**: everything written to Pāṇini's rules. Whitney says the whole later literature was "Paninized", pressed into the mould of the grammar. In sound it is almost the same as Vedic. In grammar it is simpler: Macdonell notes that it lost the whole subjunctive mood and all but one of the many Vedic infinitives. In vocabulary it grew enormously. Macdonell compares the gap between Vedic and Classical to the gap between Homer's Greek and the Greek of classical Athens.

## A language of learning, not of the street

While the grammar held Sanskrit still, the spoken languages moved on. Macdonell traces the popular dialects called **Prākrits** ("derived", "vulgar") back to the Vedic language. The oldest that survive are in the rock inscriptions of the emperor Aśoka, in the third century BC. One of them, **Pāli**, became the sacred language of southern Buddhism. From the Prākrits come most of the languages of northern India today: Hindi and Urdu, Bengali, Marathi, Gujarati, Punjabi, Sindhi.

Sanskrit stayed above them as the language of religion, scholarship and the court. The plays show the arrangement. In the Sanskrit drama, Whitney notes, the higher and educated characters speak Sanskrit and the lower and uneducated ones speak Prākrit. Whitney compares Sanskrit's long "artificial life" to that of Latin in Europe: written, and spoken among the learned, for two thousand years. Whether Classical Sanskrit was ever anyone's first language has no settled answer; Whitney judged that its sound form "never can have been a truly vernacular" one.

Its reach went far beyond the Indo-Aryan north. Macdonell points out that the Dravidian languages of the south, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada and Malayalam, are unrelated to Sanskrit but full of Sanskrit words. Whitney adds that the Indian alphabets travelled with Indian religion and culture to Tibet and to South-East Asia.

## What was written in it

First come the two epics. The *Mahābhārata*, which Monier-Williams describes as some 215,000 lines in eighteen books, tells of the contest between the sons of two royal brothers. Whitney calls it a core of very early date into which material of many periods was woven, until it became "a kind of cyclopedia". Whitney names two of its most noted episodes: the love story of **Nala** and Damayantī, and the **Bhagavad Gītā**, a philosophical dialogue between the warrior Arjuna and the god Kṛṣṇa. The *Rāmāyaṇa*, ascribed to the poet Vālmīki, tells the story of Rāma and Sītā.

After the epics come court poetry and drama. Whitney calls Kālidāsa "incomparably the chief" of the dramatists; he is the author of the play *Śakuntalā* and the poem *Raghuvaṃśa*. The animal fables of the *Pañcatantra* reached the West through Persian and Semitic versions; Whitney notes that they left their mark on the fable literature of every European language. The *Hitopadeśa* ("good advice") is a later collection partly founded on it.

Alongside the literature runs a vast technical library: law (the code of Manu), philosophy, grammar, mathematics, astronomy and medicine. Most of it, Whitney notes, is in verse, even the textbooks.

## Which Sanskrit this guide teaches

This guide teaches Classical Sanskrit, the language Pāṇini describes. There are three reasons.

First, it is the norm. For two thousand years Sanskrit was written to Pāṇini's rules, and every standard grammar and dictionary describes this variety, so every statement here can be checked.

Second, it reads the famous texts. The epics are slightly freer than Pāṇini allows, and the guide marks their extra forms where they occur. Kālidāsa and the Hitopadeśa are Classical.

Third, it is the door to the rest. Vedic is easier to approach once you know the Classical system, and one late lesson (san-07-10) shows its main differences.

The readings in part 8 run from the Hitopadeśa to the opening of the Nala story, the first verse of the Rāmāyaṇa, Kālidāsa, and, as the last reading, the opening of the Bhagavad Gītā with its best-known teaching.

## What you already know

If you know Spanish or French you already know some Sanskrit words, because Latin and Sanskrit inherited them from the same parent. Wiktionary traces *pitṛ* "father" and Latin *pater* (whence *padre*, *père*) to one Indo-European word; *mātṛ* "mother" goes with *māter*; *nāman* "name" with *nōmen* (*nombre*, *nom*); *deva* "god" with *deus* (*dios*, *dieu*); *asti* "is" with Latin *est*; the stem *tri-* "three" with *trēs*. The grammar is related too: Sanskrit has cases like Latin, and more of them.

Do not lean on this too far. Three thousand years separate Sanskrit from Spanish, and the grammar has to be learnt on its own terms. The next lesson gives its organising idea.
