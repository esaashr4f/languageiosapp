## The idea

A Sanskrit word is built like a small machine, in three layers. Learn the layers and almost every word you meet can be taken apart.

The first layer is the **root**: a single syllable that carries a bare meaning and is not itself a word you would say. The Indian grammarians called it *dhātu*. This guide writes a root with the sign √: √bhū "be, become", √vid "know", √kṛ "do, make".

The second layer is the **stem**: the root with a suffix added, and often with its vowel changed. The stem says what kind of word you have: a noun, an adjective, a verb in a given tense.

The third layer is the **ending**. On a noun it says the word's job in the sentence and how many there are. On a verb it says who is acting and how many.

Then a fourth thing happens, which is not a layer but a process. Where two pieces meet, inside a word or between words, their sounds adjust to each other. This is **sandhi** (Sanskrit *saṃdhi*, "junction, combination"). It is the reason a line of Sanskrit can look like one long word.

Macdonell sees this analysis as the great discovery of the Indian grammarians: that words "for the most part consist on the one hand of roots, and on the other of affixes". He adds that it was "aided by the great transparency of the Sanskrit language". The joins are regular, so the pieces can be found.

## One root, many words

Here are three roots and some words built on them. The words are given in their dictionary form. Do not try to learn them yet: watch the vowel of the root.

:::example
orig: भू, भव, भाव
translit: bhū, bhava, bhāva
morphemes: bhū bhava bhāva
gloss: be birth being
translation: √bhū "be, become"; bhava "coming into existence, birth"; bhāva "being, state"
note: The root vowel ū becomes av, then āv. The av and āv are o and au before a vowel (san-03-11 shows why).
source: mw1899
confidence: verified
:::

:::example
orig: विद्, वेद, वैद्य
translit: vid, veda, vaidya
morphemes: vid veda vaidya
gloss: know knowledge learned
translation: √vid "know"; veda "knowledge", and the name of the sacred texts; vaidya "learned; a physician"
note: The root vowel i becomes e, then ai.
source: mw1899
confidence: verified
:::

:::example
orig: कृ, कृत, कर्तृ, कार्य
translit: kṛ, kṛta, kartṛ, kārya
morphemes: kṛ kṛta kartṛ kārya
gloss: do done doer to.be.done
translation: √kṛ "do, make"; kṛta "done, made"; kartṛ "doer, maker"; kārya "to be done"
note: The root vowel ṛ stays, or becomes ar, or becomes ār.
source: mw1899
confidence: verified
:::

Each root vowel has three settings. The plain one is the root's own (*i*, *u*, *ṛ*). The second, called **guṇa** (the word means "quality"; Monier-Williams gives the grammatical sense "the first gradation of a vowel"), puts an *a* in front of it: *i* becomes *e*, *u* becomes *o*, *ṛ* becomes *ar*. The third, called **vṛddhi** ("growth", in grammar "the second modification or increase of vowels"), puts in a long *ā*: *i* becomes *ai*, *u* becomes *au*, *ṛ* becomes *ār*. Whitney describes *e* and *o* as the guṇa of *i* and *u*, and *ai* and *au* as their vṛddhi. Which setting a word uses depends on the suffix, and part 3 teaches the rules.

Why does the vowel change at all? Macdonell credits Pāṇini with finding the pattern, and notes that it is the same alternation that Grimm, studying German, called *ablaut*, and which "comparative grammar traces to the original Indo-European language". The Indian grammarians describe it as a raising of the plain vowel. Historically it ran the other way: Whitney says *ṛ* comes "almost always" from an older *ar* or *ra*, so the full grade is the older one. The raising picture still predicts the forms, which is why this guide uses it.

## Endings carry the grammar

Now the third layer. These examples are previews: the forms are taught in part 2.

orig: भवति
translit: bhavati
morphemes: bhava-ti
gloss: be-3SG
translation: He is. / She becomes. / It is.
note: Preview. Root √bhū, stem bhava- (the guṇa o, as av), ending -ti "one other person".

:::

:::example
orig: गच्छामि
translit: gacchāmi
morphemes: gacchā-mi
gloss: go-1SG
translation: I go.
note: Preview. No word for "I" is needed: the ending -mi says it.
source: constructed
confidence: constructed

:::

A gloss line labels each piece. Lower-case words are meanings; capitals are grammatical labels (3SG "he, she or it", NOM "the doer", PL "more than two"). The next lesson explains how to read the whole block.

Notice that one ending packs several facts together. The *-āḥ* of *devāḥ* says "doer" and "several" at once, and you cannot cut it into a piece for each. Languages built like this are called **fusional**. Japanese and Korean do the same jobs with separate particles, one meaning each, placed after the noun. Sanskrit fuses them into the end of the word, as Latin does.

## The joins

Here is the most quoted line of the Bhagavad Gītā, as editions print it in roman letters.

:::example
orig: कर्मण्य् एवाधिकारस् ते मा फलेषु कदा चन
translit: karmaṇy evādhikāras te mā phaleṣu kadā cana
morphemes: karmaṇ-i eva adhikār-aḥ te mā phal-eṣu kadā cana
gloss: action-LOC.SG EMPH right-NOM.SG 2SG.GEN PROH fruit-LOC.PL when INDF
literal: In action alone (is) your right; not in fruits, ever.
translation: Your right is to the work alone, never to its fruits.
note: Preview, to show the joins. Unjoined, the first words are karmaṇi eva adhikāraḥ te.
source: Bhagavad Gītā 2.47a (= Mahābhārata 6.24.47a, BORI critical edition, GRETIL e-text bhgce__u.htm)
confidence: attested
preview: LOC, SG, EMPH, NOM, 2, GEN, PROH, PL, INDF
:::

Compare the first line with the third. Three joins have changed the sounds.

1. *karmaṇi* "in action" stands before a vowel, so its final *i* turns into the consonant *y*: *karmaṇy eva*.
2. *eva* "alone" ends in *a*, and *adhikāraḥ* "right" begins with *a*. The two vowels merge into one long *ā*: *evādhikāraḥ*.
3. *adhikāraḥ* stands before *t*, so its final breath *ḥ* becomes *s*: *adhikāras te*.

Sandhi works inside words too. The stem of "action" is *karman*, but before the ending *-i* its *n* has become *ṇ*, because an *r* earlier in the same word pulls it back in the mouth (Whitney §46). Part 1 explains the sounds involved.

Indian manuscripts go one step further and write a whole line without spaces, joining every final consonant to what follows (Whitney §9). Where the guide's line has a space after *karmaṇy*, a manuscript has none.

## What this means for you

Three habits follow.

Read every word in two directions. The front tells you the root and so the meaning. The end tells you the job.

Expect the edges of words to move. A word on the page may not look like the word in the dictionary. Part 2 begins with the commonest joins, and later lessons add the rest in stages.

Look for the root behind the vowel. *Veda*, *vaidya* and *vid* are one family. Once you can strip the guṇa or vṛddhi off a vowel, the dictionary opens up.

The price of the system is that you must learn the joins as well as the endings. The reward is that a vast vocabulary turns out to rest on a limited stock of roots. Pāṇini's list has about 2,000, and Macdonell notes that only about 800 of them have been found in the literature.
