## The route

The guide has nine parts after this one. Read them in order. Each lesson uses only what earlier lessons have taught, so skipping ahead will show you forms nobody has explained yet.

Part 1 teaches the sounds and the Devanagari script. Part 2 gets you to short, complete sentences and the first joins between words. Parts 3 and 4 are the core: nouns with their eight cases, then verbs with their tenses, moods and voices. Part 5 adds adjectives, numbers, indeclinable words and compounds. Part 6 builds complex sentences. Part 7 covers special topics, among them metre, the epic language and Vedic. Part 8 is real texts, read line by line. Part 9 is reference: tables, vocabulary, a glossary and the book list.

Each lesson has the same shape. First the idea and the reason for it. Then how the form is built, step by step. Then examples, the places where the construction applies, and the traps. A table comes only after the pattern has been explained, as a place to look things up. The lesson ends with exercises and worked answers. Do the exercises before reading the answers: building a sentence yourself is the test of whether you understood it.

## How to read an example

Here is the first line of the Nala story in the Mahābhārata. It is a preview: its forms are taught in parts 2 to 4.

:::example
orig: आसीद् राजा नलो नाम वीरसेनसुतो बली
translit: āsīd rājā nalo nāma vīrasenasuto balī
morphemes: āsīt rājā nal-aḥ nāma vīrasena-sut-aḥ balī
gloss: be.PST.3SG king.NOM.SG Nala-NOM.SG by.name Vīrasena-son-NOM.SG strong.NOM.SG.M
literal: There was a king, Nala by name, son of Vīrasena, strong.
translation: There was once a king called Nala, the mighty son of Vīrasena.
note: Preview. Two joins: āsīt is written āsīd before r, and nalaḥ is written nalo before n. vīrasenasutaḥ is a compound, "Vīrasena's son".
source: Mahābhārata 3.50.1a (BORI critical edition, GRETIL e-text mbh_03_u.htm)
confidence: attested
preview: PST, 3, SG, NOM, M
:::

The block has up to eight lines. Here is what each one is for.

The first line is the Sanskrit in Devanagari, the script Sanskrit is normally printed in.

The second line is the same text in roman letters. This is called **transliteration**: each Devanagari letter is replaced by a fixed roman letter or letter with a mark. The scheme used here, IAST, is the one in the grammars, the dictionaries and the online texts. Part 1 teaches it with the script. In the app you can hide this line once you read Devanagari comfortably.

The third line undoes the joins. Every word stands in the form it would have alone, at the end of an utterance, and a hyphen separates its pieces. The Vedic schools had a name for a text recited or written this way: the *padapāṭha*, which Monier-Williams defines as arranging "each word ... separately in its original form ... without regard to the rules of Saṃdhi". Here *āsīd* is back to *āsīt*, and *nalo* is back to *nalaḥ*. When you meet a line you cannot parse, compare these two lines first.

The fourth line is the **gloss**. It labels each piece, word under word and piece under piece. Meanings are in lower case. Grammatical labels are in capitals: NOM, SG. A full stop joins labels that share a single piece: the ending *-aḥ* of *nalaḥ* means "doer" and "one" at once, so it is glossed NOM.SG. When a whole word cannot be cut, the full stops join its meaning to its labels, as in *rājā* "king.NOM.SG". Tap any label in the app to see what it stands for. Each label is explained in plain words in the lesson that introduces it, and all are listed in part 9. The labels follow the Leipzig Glossing Rules, the standard in linguistics.

Then come a literal translation where it helps to show the Sanskrit order, a natural English translation, and a note saying what the example is there to show.

The last lines say where the example comes from and how sure the guide is of it.

## Spelling and spacing

The Devanagari line is spaced exactly as the roman line: a space wherever one word ends and the next begins, except where two vowels have melted into one. A word that ends in a consonant before a space carries a small stroke under its last letter (the **virāma**, taught in san-01-07). This is a teaching convention, which Whitney (§9) notes in texts prepared for beginners. Indian editions run such words together, and manuscripts write a whole line solid. Lesson san-01-09 shows you how to read that printing too.

Dictionaries list words in a form you will rarely see in a sentence. Nouns and adjectives are listed by their **stem**, the form before any ending: *deva* "god", not *devaḥ*. Verbs are listed by their root: √gam "go", not *gacchati* "he goes". The guide does the same. When part 1 shows single words, they are in this dictionary form, glossed by their meaning only, like this:

:::example
orig: देव
translit: deva
morphemes: deva
gloss: god
translation: god
note: The dictionary form (stem). In a sentence the word takes an ending: devaḥ, devam, devena and so on.
source: mw1899; wiktionary
confidence: verified
:::

## Real sentences and made-up ones

Classical Sanskrit has no community of native speakers to ask. Everything known about it comes from texts and from the grammarians. This guide therefore tells you where each example comes from. In the app, each example and table has a small coloured dot. Tap it to see the label and the source.

- **attested**: a real line from a real text, cited by work and passage, as edited in a critical edition. Translations are always this guide's own.
- **constructed**: a sentence built for this guide from rules and words that have each been checked. Early lessons use mostly constructed examples, because real lines use too much grammar at once. As the guide goes on, attested lines take over.
- **verified**: a form or table checked against a named source.
- **uncertain**: the sources disagree, or the form is a reconstruction. The lesson says so in words as well.
- **unverified**: written but not yet checked. You should not see this label in a finished guide.

## Where the facts come from

The grammar follows two standard reference works that are free to read: William Dwight Whitney's *Sanskrit Grammar* (2nd edition, 1889) and Arthur Macdonell's *Sanskrit Grammar for Students* (3rd edition, 1927). Section numbers such as "Whitney §9" point to Whitney. Syntax, the uses of the cases and moods, follows J. S. Speijer's *Sanskrit Syntax* (1886).

Meanings follow Monier-Williams's *Sanskrit-English Dictionary* (1899), with Apte's *Practical Sanskrit-English Dictionary* (1890) as a second opinion. Individual inflected forms were checked against the Sanskrit tables of Wiktionary, and where needed against the declension engines of the Sanskrit Heritage Site.

Attested lines come from GRETIL, the Göttingen register of Sanskrit e-texts, which transcribes the critical editions: the Poona edition of the Mahābhārata (which includes the Gītā and Nala), the Baroda edition of the Rāmāyaṇa, and others. The Digital Corpus of Sanskrit, which splits texts into words and counts them, guides the choice of vocabulary towards the most frequent words.

## How much to memorise

Less than a school course would ask. The aim of this guide is that you understand why each form is what it is, so that you can rebuild it. Tables are there to be consulted.

Some things must still become automatic through reading: the commonest endings, the commonest joins, and a few hundred words. The joins are taught in four stages (san-02-06, san-03-07, san-04-07, san-05-07). Until a stage is reached, constructed examples are built to avoid the joins it covers, and attested lines that contain them say so in the note. Do the exercises, and come back to earlier lessons when something later depends on them.
