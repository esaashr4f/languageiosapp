## The idea

The English alphabet is in no order at all. Nobody can say why *b* follows *a* or why *z* comes last. The Sanskrit alphabet is the opposite: it is a map of the mouth.

The phoneticians of the Vedic schools sorted every sound by two questions. Where in the mouth is it made? And how is it made? Whitney (§7) notes that their order of description became the alphabetic order, and European scholars adopted it for dictionaries and indexes. When you look a word up in a Sanskrit dictionary, you are walking through their classification.

The heart of the system is a grid of twenty-five consonants. The Indian name for them is *sparśa*, "contact", because the tongue or lips close the passage completely (Whitney §32). In English these are called **stops**: sounds made by stopping the air and letting it go, like *p*, *t*, *k*. Sanskrit has five rows of them, one for each place in the mouth, and five columns, one for each way of making the sound.

## How it is built

Build the grid in two steps, then add the sounds that sit around it.

**Step 1: the places, from the back of the mouth to the front** (Whitney §33). The Indian names are adjectives made from the parts of the body involved:

1. **Velar** (*kaṇṭhya*, "of the throat"): the back of the tongue against the soft palate, as in English *k*, *g*.
2. **Palatal** (*tālavya*, "of the palate"): the middle of the tongue against the hard palate.
3. **Retroflex** (*mūrdhanya*, "of the head", meaning the roof of the mouth): the tip of the tongue curled back against the roof of the mouth. Older English grammars call these "cerebral" or "lingual".
4. **Dental** (*dantya*, "of the teeth"): the tip of the tongue against the upper teeth.
5. **Labial** (*oṣṭhya*, "of the lips"): the two lips.

**Step 2: the manners, five in each row** (Whitney §34). The grammarians call them the first, second, third, fourth and fifth:

1. **Voiceless**: the vocal cords do not vibrate, as in English *k*, *t*, *p*. The Indian term is *aghoṣa*, "toneless".
2. **Voiceless aspirate**: the same, followed by a clear puff of breath: *kh*, *th*, *ph*. Pāṇini himself has no name for aspirates; the scheme in the commentary on his grammar calls them *mahāprāṇa*, "great expiration" (Whitney §37d).
3. **Voiced**: the vocal cords vibrate, as in English *g*, *d*, *b*.
4. **Voiced aspirate**: voiced, with a breathy release: *gh*, *dh*, *bh*.
5. **Nasal**: the mouth closed at the same place, the air going out through the nose: *ṅ*, *ñ*, *ṇ*, *n*, *m*.

Every stop is the meeting of one row and one column. *k* is velar and first. *dh* is dental and fourth. *m* is labial and fifth. That is all a sound's "name" is.

**Step 3: what surrounds the grid.** Before it stand the vowels, *svara* (§30). After it come four **semivowels**, *y r l v*, which the grammarians called *antaḥsthā*, "standing between", probably because they stand between the stops and the next group in the list (§51). Then come three hissing sounds, *ś ṣ s*, and the breathing *h*, together called *ūṣman*, "heat, steam" (§59). Two more sounds, anusvāra *ṃ* and visarga *ḥ*, occur only at the end of a syllable. Lesson san-01-04 explains them.

The semivowels and sibilants fit the same places. *y* and *ś* are palatal, *r* and *ṣ* are retroflex, *l* and *s* are dental, *v* is labial (§§51–64). The vowels fit too: *i* is palatal and *u* is labial (§20), so *i* goes with *y* and *u* with *v*.

**Why the grid matters.** The grid is not decoration. The rules of sandhi are stated in terms of it. A sound at the edge of a word often takes on the row or the column of its neighbour. A final *m* before a stop is pronounced as the nasal of that stop's row: velar *ṅ* before *k*, dental *n* before *t* (Whitney §71). A voiceless stop becomes voiced before a voiced sound, moving across from the first column to the third (Whitney §159). And a vowel can turn into the semivowel of its own place: in the last lesson, *karmaṇi* before a vowel became *karmaṇy*, the palatal vowel *i* turning into the palatal semivowel *y*. If you know where a sound sits in the grid, you can predict most of what will happen to it.

## Watch out

**The order is not English order.** In a Sanskrit dictionary all words beginning with vowels come first, then *k*, *kh*, *g*, *gh*, then the palatals, and so on to *h*. A word in *d* comes after every word in *ṭ* and before every word in *p*. You will need the full order (Reference table) every time you use Monier-Williams.

**"Retroflex" is the modern name.** You will meet "cerebral" in Whitney and Macdonell and "lingual" in other older books. They mean the same row. This guide says retroflex.

**Aspirates are single sounds.** *kh* is written with two roman letters but it is one consonant, one cell of the grid, and one letter in Devanagari. Never split it into *k* + *h* when you count consonants or sort words.

**h is not where you expect.** *h* stands at the very end of the alphabet, after the sibilants, not next to the aspirates.

## Exercises

1. Give the row and column of each sound: *g*, *ph*, *ṇ*, *t*, *jh*.
2. Name the sound that is palatal and voiceless aspirate, and the one that is labial and voiced.
3. Put these words in dictionary order: *danta*, *kaṇṭha*, *tālu*, *oṣṭha*.
4. Why is *n* written before *t* in *danta* and not *ṅ* or *m*? Use the grid.
5. Which semivowel and which sibilant belong to the same place as the vowel *i*?
6. A book calls *ḍ* a "cerebral". What does this guide call it, and where is it made?
7. A final *m* stands before a word beginning with *p*. Which nasal is it pronounced as, and why?

## Worked answers

1. *g*: velar row, third (voiced). *ph*: labial row, second (voiceless aspirate). *ṇ*: retroflex row, fifth (nasal). *t*: dental row, first (voiceless). *jh*: palatal row, fourth (voiced aspirate).
2. Palatal and voiceless aspirate is *ch*. Labial and voiced is *b*.
3. *oṣṭha*, *kaṇṭha*, *tālu*, *danta*. Vowels come first, so *o* leads. Then the velar *k*. Then the dental row, where *t* (first) precedes *d* (third).
4. A nasal before a stop takes the stop's row. *t* is dental, so the nasal before it is the dental *n*. Velar *ṅ* would need a following *k*, and labial *m* a *p*.
5. *i* is palatal. The palatal semivowel is *y* and the palatal sibilant is *ś*.
6. Retroflex. It is made with the tip of the tongue curled back against the roof of the mouth.
7. As [m], the labial nasal, because *p* is a labial stop and a nasal before a stop takes the stop's row. The sound is [m] itself, although this guide writes a final *m* before a consonant as anusvāra *ṃ* (san-01-04).
