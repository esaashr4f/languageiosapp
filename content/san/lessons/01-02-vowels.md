## The idea

Sanskrit has three basic vowels, *a*, *i* and *u*, each short or long. Whitney (§19) calls them "the three earliest and most universal vowels" of the Indo-European languages. To these it adds two vowels made from *r* and *l*, and four vowels that were once pairs of vowels, the diphthongs *e*, *ai*, *o*, *au*. That is the whole first block of the alphabet: *a ā i ī u ū ṛ ṝ ḷ e ai o au*.

Length is part of the vowel. A long vowel is held for twice the time of a short one, and the grammarians measured it so (Whitney §77). Length changes meaning. *dina* is "day"; *dīna* is "wretched". The roman letters show length with a bar, the **macron**: *ā ī ū*. Devanagari has a separate letter for each long vowel (san-01-06).

The *a*-vowels dominate the language. Whitney (§22) counted them as about twice as frequent as all the other vowels together. So the commonest sound in Sanskrit is short *a*, and it is not the *a* you expect.

## How it is built

Take the vowels in four groups.

**1. The simple vowels.** Say *i ī u ū* as in Spanish or Japanese, pure and steady: *i* as in *pin*, *ī* as in *pique*, *u* as in *pull*, *ū* as in *rule* (Whitney §19). Long *ā* is the *a* of *father* [aː].

Short *a* is different. It is not a short version of *ā* but a duller vowel, close to the vowel of English *but*: in phonetic letters [ɐ]. Whitney (§21) notes that Pāṇini already describes it as *saṃvṛta*, "covered up, dimmed", and Macdonell (§15) dates the sound to at least 300 BC. So *mati* "thought" is [mɐt̪i], with a dull first vowel and a clear second one.

**2. The vocalic ṛ and ḷ.** Sanskrit can use *r* as the centre of a syllable, as English uses *l* in *bottle*. This sound is written *ṛ*, with a dot below. Whitney (§24) describes it as "a smooth or untrilled *r*-sound, assuming a vocalic office". Say *ṛṣi* "seer" as [r̩ʂi]: two syllables, the first made of *r* alone.

Modern Indian reciters say *ṛ* as [ɾɪ], like *ri*, and Whitney already notes this habit (§24a). That is why you will see the spelling *rishi*. This guide teaches the vowel [r̩], and you may use [ɾɪ] if the vowel is too hard; the spelling *ṛ* stays either way.

Long *ṝ* is rare: it occurs only in a few plural forms of nouns like *pitṛ* "father" (§26). *ḷ* occurs only in forms of one root, √kḷp "be fit" (§26). Long *ḹ* is listed by some grammarians for symmetry, but Whitney (§23a) says it "does not occur in a single genuine word".

**3. e and o.** These look like plain vowels and sound like them: *e* as in *they* [eː], *o* as in *go* without the glide [oː] (Whitney §28). Both are **always long**. There is no short *e* or *o* in Sanskrit. They count as diphthongs because they began as *a* + *i* and *a* + *u*, and the rules of sandhi still treat them that way (§28a).

**4. ai and au.** These are real diphthongs, two vowels in one syllable: *ai* glides from *a* to *i*, *au* from *a* to *u*. Whitney (§28a) reconstructs them as *ā* + *i* and *ā* + *u*, with a long first part. This guide writes them [ɐi̯] and [ɐu̯]. They are the vṛddhi grade from san-00-02: *tila* "sesame", *taila* "sesame oil".

Last, a rule that depends on length: **syllable weight**. A syllable is **heavy** (*guru*) if its vowel is long, or if two consonants follow its vowel, or if *ṃ* or *ḥ* follows it. Otherwise it is **light** (*laghu*) (Whitney §79). An aspirate such as *kh* or *gh* counts as one consonant. So *putra* "son" is heavy-light (two consonants after the *u*), and *laghu* is light-light. Weight is what Sanskrit metre counts (san-07-07). Notice the difference in terms: a *vowel* is long or short, a *syllable* is heavy or light (§79a).

## Watch out

**Short a is not ā.** English speakers tend to say every *a* as in *father*. Doing so blurs *a* and *ā*, which the grammar keeps apart everywhere: endings, roots and words differ by this length alone.

**e and o are never short.** Spanish and Italian habits suggest a short *e* in *deśa*. Sanskrit has none, and the first syllable of *deśa* is heavy.

**ṛ is a vowel, not "ri".** The transliterations "Krishna" and "rishi" are English spellings of the modern pronunciation. In this guide they are *kṛṣṇa* and *ṛṣi*, and *ṛ* counts as one vowel for weight and for sandhi.

**Long and heavy are different things.** The first vowel of *putra* is short, but its syllable is heavy. Keep "long" for vowels and "heavy" for syllables.

**Length is not stress.** Classical Sanskrit was written without accent marks, and the grammar does not depend on stress. Give each vowel its length and do not stretch or weaken vowels for emphasis.

## Connections

Arabic, like Sanskrit, builds on three vowel qualities, *a*, *i*, *u*, each short or long. Japanese keeps a length contrast of the Sanskrit kind (*obasan* "aunt", *obāsan* "grandmother"). Latin *pater* is the same word as *pitṛ*: Wiktionary traces both, and Spanish *padre* and French *père*, to one Indo-European word for "father".

## Exercises

1. Write the sounds of *dina* and *dīna* in brackets and say what differs.
2. Which of these first syllables are heavy: *mati*, *loka*, *putra*, *ṛṣi*, *taila*? Give the reason for each.
3. A book prints "rishi". Give the IAST spelling and say how many syllables the word has.
4. Why do the rules of sandhi treat *e* and *o* as diphthongs, when they sound like single long vowels?
5. Give the vṛddhi of *i* and of *u*, with one word pair from this lesson for each.
6. An English speaker says *mati* with the *a* of *father*. What is wrong, and what is the right sound?
7. Is *laghu* light-light or heavy-light? Explain the role of *gh*.

## Worked answers

1. [d̪in̪ɐ] and [d̪iːn̪ɐ]. The first vowel is short in *dina* and long in *dīna*. The meanings are "day" and "wretched".
2. *mati*: light (short *a*, one consonant follows). *loka*: heavy (*o* is always long). *putra*: heavy (two consonants, *t* and *r*, follow the short *u*). *ṛṣi*: light (short *ṛ*, one consonant *ṣ* follows). *taila*: heavy (*ai* is a diphthong, so long).
3. *ṛṣi*. Two syllables: *ṛ* and *ṣi*. The first syllable has no other vowel: *ṛ* is the vowel.
4. Because they were diphthongs when the rules took shape: *e* from *a* + *i*, *o* from *a* + *u*. Whitney (§28a) argues this from the way sandhi handles them.
5. The vṛddhi of *i* is *ai*: *tila*, *taila*. The vṛddhi of *u* is *au*: *putra*, *pautra*.
6. Sanskrit short *a* is the dull vowel [ɐ], as in English *but*. The *a* of *father* is long *ā*. So the word is [mɐt̪i], not [maːt̪i], which would sound like a different word.
7. Light-light. Both vowels are short. The *gh* after the first *a* is one consonant, an aspirate, so it does not make the syllable heavy.
