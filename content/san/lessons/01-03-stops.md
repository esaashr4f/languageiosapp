## The idea

The grid of san-01-01 has five rows of stops. Two of its five columns are familiar from English: the plain voiceless stops *k t p* and the voiced ones *g d b*. The nasal column is familiar too. What English lacks is the two aspirate columns, and two whole rows: the palatals and the retroflexes. Those are the sounds to practise.

Sanskrit keeps these differences because they separate words. *kara* is "hand" and *khara* is "donkey". *aṇu* is "minute, atomic" and *anu* is "after". If you say *kh* as *k*, or *ṇ* as *n*, you are saying a different word.

The aspirates have a history. Whitney (§38) says the voiced aspirates *gh dh bh* go back to Indo-European, the parent language, while the voiceless aspirates *kh th ph* are "a special Indian development". The retroflexes, too, are an Indian development: mostly dentals that changed next to *ṣ* or *r*, and partly found in words with no known Indo-European source, which Whitney (§46) suggests may come from other languages of India. That second part is a suggestion, not a certainty.

## How it is built

Work column by column first, then row by row.

**The aspirates.** An aspirate is a stop with a puff of breath after it, all one sound. *kh* is [kʰ], as in the middle of English *backhand* said fast; *th* as in *boathook*, *ph* as in *haphazard* (Whitney §37e). They are never the fricatives of English *thin* or *photo*: Whitney (§37a) says it is "beyond question" that they are true stops. So *phala* "fruit" is [pʰɐlɐ], not "fala".

The voiced aspirates *gh jh ḍh dh bh* are voiced stops released with a breathy, murmured sound: *dh* as in *madhouse*, *bh* as in *abhor*. Whitney (§37c) admits that their exact nature is disputed. They are still used in Hindi and other Indian languages today, so you can hear them in modern speech.

**The nasals.** Each row has its own nasal, made at the same place as the stops of that row. *m* and *n* are as in English. *ṅ* is the *ng* of *singing* [ŋ]. *ñ* is the *ñ* of Spanish *año* [ɲ]. *ṇ* is a retroflex *n* [ɳ]. *ṅ* and *ñ* hardly ever stand alone: they appear before a stop of their own row, as in *aṅga* "limb" and *pañca* "five" (Whitney §§40, 42).

**Row 1, velar: k kh g gh ṅ** [k kʰ ɡ ɡʱ ŋ]. As in English, with *g* always hard.

**Row 2, palatal: c ch j jh ñ.** The old phoneticians describe these as simple stops made with the middle of the tongue on the hard palate. Today, in India and in Europe, they are said as affricates, a stop gliding into a hiss: *c* close to the *ch* of *church*, *j* to the *j* of *judge* (Whitney §44). This guide writes them [t͡ɕ d͡ʑ], says them that way, and reminds you that the older value was probably [c ɟ]. IAST *c* is never [k] or [s]. *jh* is very rare (§42).

The palatals are also a history lesson. Whitney (§42) says the whole row comes from older velars, and Macdonell (§6, note) adds that the change happened largely under the influence of palatal vowels. That is why *c* and *k* sometimes alternate within one root: the old velar "reverts" in some forms (Whitney §43).

**Row 3, retroflex: ṭ ṭh ḍ ḍh ṇ** [ʈ ʈʰ ɖ ɖʱ ɳ]. Turn the tip of the tongue up and draw it back into the dome of the palate, and make the stop there (Whitney §45). Whitney compares the tongue position to that of the smooth English *r*.

**Row 4, dental: t th d dh n** [t̪ t̪ʰ d̪ d̪ʱ n̪]. Put the tip of the tongue against the back of the upper teeth (§47), as for Spanish or French *t* and *d*. The small mark under [t̪] in phonetic writing means "dental".

**Row 5, labial: p ph b bh m** [p pʰ b bʱ m]. As in English, plus the two aspirates. *b* is rare, and *bh* far more common (Whitney §50).

## Watch out

**th and ph are not English th and f.** This is the commonest mistake of English speakers. *tathā* has no *th* of *thin* in it, and *phala* no *f*.

**English t and d are neither row.** English makes *t* and *d* on the gum ridge, between the Sanskrit dental and retroflex places. Whitney (§47a) notes that Indian listeners hear English *t* and *d* as retroflex. Aim for the teeth with the dentals and well back with the retroflexes.

**Do not skip the retroflexes.** Whitney (§45) reports that European Sanskritists of his day said *ṭ* as *t* and *ḍ* as *d*. Sanskrit spelling and sandhi keep them apart, and the n-to-ṇ rule (san-03-01) depends on hearing the difference.

**c is one sound.** In IAST *c* is always the palatal [t͡ɕ], never [k] as in English *cat* or [s] as in *city*. *ch* is its aspirate, not a second spelling of the same sound.

**Aspirates count as one consonant.** For syllable weight, *sukha* has one consonant between its vowels, so its first syllable is light.

## Connections

Korean keeps a contrast between plain and aspirated stops much like Sanskrit's first two columns: ㄱ against ㅋ, ㄷ against ㅌ, ㅂ against ㅍ. Hindi, a descendant of the Prākrits, keeps all four columns, including the voiced aspirates. Spanish and French *t* and *d* are dental, so they are already the Sanskrit dental row.

## Exercises

1. Write the sounds of *kara* and *khara* and say what separates them.
2. Name the row and column of each stop in *daṇḍa*.
3. An English speaker reads *phala* as "fala" and *tathā* with the *th* of *thin*. Correct both.
4. Why can *ṅ* appear in *aṅga* but not at the start of a word like *ṅala*?
5. Which row came from older velars, according to Whitney? What do you expect to find inside some roots as a result?
6. Is the first syllable of *cakra* heavy or light? And of *sukha*?
7. Sort these into dictionary order: *phala*, *jana*, *dhana*, *khara*, *daṇḍa*.

## Worked answers

1. [kɐɾɐ] "hand" and [kʰɐɾɐ] "donkey". The second begins with the aspirate *kh*: the same *k* followed by a puff of breath.
2. *d*: dental, voiced (third). *ṇ*: retroflex, nasal (fifth). *ḍ*: retroflex, voiced (third).
3. *ph* is *p* with a puff of breath, [pʰɐlɐ]. *th* is a dental *t* with a puff of breath: [t̪ɐt̪ʰaː]. Neither is a fricative.
4. *ṅ* occurs before a velar stop, as here before *g*, and in a handful of word-final forms (Whitney §40). It does not begin words, so *ṅala* is not a possible Sanskrit word.
5. The palatal row. Expect *c* and *k* (and *j* and *g*) to alternate in forms of one root.
6. *cakra*: heavy, because two consonants, *k* and *r*, follow the short *a*. *sukha*: light, because *kh* is one consonant.
7. *khara*, *jana*, *daṇḍa*, *dhana*, *phala*. Velar *kh* first; then the palatal *j*; then *daṇḍa* and *dhana* in the dental row, *d* before *dh*; then labial *ph*.
