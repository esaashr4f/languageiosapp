## The idea

After the grid come the last nine consonants of the alphabet: four semivowels, three sibilants and *h*. Two more sounds, anusvāra and visarga, complete the system. All of them fit the places of the grid, and most are tied to other sounds by regular changes.

A **semivowel** is a consonant that is close to a vowel: the tongue or lips narrow the passage but do not close it. Sanskrit's four, *y r l v*, are the consonant partners of the vowels *i ṛ ḷ u*. Whitney (§55) says *y* and *i* "exchange with one another in cases innumerable", and *v* stands to *u* exactly as *y* to *i* (§57a). You saw it in san-00-02: *karmaṇi* before a vowel became *karmaṇy*.

A **sibilant** is a hissing sound, made by forcing air through a narrow gap. Sanskrit has three, one each in the palatal, retroflex and dental places: *ś ṣ s*.

Anusvāra and visarga occur only after a vowel, at the end of a syllable. They are what the nasals and the sounds *s* and *r* become in that position.

## How it is built

**1. The semivowels.**

- ***y*** [j] is the *y* of *yes*: *yoga* [joːɡɐ]. It is palatal, the partner of *i*.
- ***r*** [ɾ] is a light tap of the tongue tip. Whitney (§52) argues from sandhi that it was made in the retroflex position, and that it was "untrilled", unlike Spanish *rr*. Its partner is the vowel *ṛ*.
- ***l*** [l] is a dental *l* (§53). Make it clear, as in French or Spanish, not the dark *l* of English *full*. Its partner is *ḷ*.
- ***v*** [ʋ] was originally a *w*-sound, the partner of *u* (§57a). The grammarians already describe a sound made "between the upper teeth and the lower lip", and today it is usually a soft *v*, or *w* after a consonant in the same syllable (§57). Say [ʋ], halfway between *v* and *w*: *vana* "forest" [ʋɐn̪ɐ].

**2. The sibilants.**

- ***s*** [s] is the *s* of *sea*, never the *z* of *rose* (§60).
- ***ś*** [ɕ] is palatal: the flat of the tongue against the hard palate, the ordinary English *sh* (§63). *śānti* "peace" [ɕaːn̪t̪i].
- ***ṣ*** [ʂ] is retroflex: a *sh* made with the tongue tip turned back (§61). *puruṣa* "man" [puɾuʂɐ].

*ś* and *ṣ* sound alike to an English ear. Whitney (§63a) compares the difference to that between *t* and *ṭ*, and says they "are doubtless not more unlike". Each has its own history. *ṣ* is nearly always *s* changed by a neighbouring sound, under rules taught in part 5 (§62). *ś*, like the palatal stops, comes from an older velar (§64).

**3. h.** Sanskrit *h* is voiced [ɦ], a breathy *h* with the vocal cords vibrating, as in English *ahead* said with a murmur. Whitney (§65) says all the native authorities define it "as not a surd element, but a sonant". It comes in nearly every case from an older *gh* (§66). *hasta* "hand" [ɦɐst̪ɐ].

**4. Anusvāra ṃ.** The name means "after-sound". It is a nasal that follows a vowel without a full closure of the mouth (Whitney §70). How it sounds depends on what follows (§71):

1. Before a stop it becomes the nasal of that stop's row: *saṃkalpa* "resolve" is [sɐŋkɐlpɐ], with velar [ŋ] before *k*.
2. Before *y r l v*, the sibilants and *h*, it is a nasal sound without contact. The old authorities disagreed whether this was a nasalised vowel or a separate nasal element; Whitney (§71) calls it a "discordance of opinion". This guide writes it as a nasalised vowel: *siṃha* "lion" [sĩɦɐ], *saṃsāra* "the round of existence" [sɐ̃saːɾɐ].

Inside a word, a nasal before a stop is normally written with the stop's own nasal letter, as in *aṅga* and *daṇḍa*. Anusvāra is written before the semivowels, sibilants and *h*, and in a few words such as *saṃkalpa*, where the prefix *saṃ-* keeps it. At the end of a word before a consonant, final *m* is written *ṃ*. Part 2 starts using that rule.

**5. Visarga ḥ.** A voiceless breath, an *h* "uttered in the articulating position of the preceding vowel" (Whitney §67). It replaces *s* or *r* at the end of a word before a pause (§68). So the dictionary word *punar* "again" is said and written *punaḥ* at the end of a sentence. Visarga also appears inside a few words, as in *duḥkha* "pain". Many sandhi rules of part 2 are rules about visarga.

## Watch out

**ś, ṣ and s are three sounds.** Spellings such as *Shiva* and *Krishna* write both *ś* and *ṣ* as *sh*. In IAST they are always kept apart, and the rules of sandhi treat them differently.

**h is voiced.** It is not the voiceless English *h* of *hat*, and it is not the visarga *ḥ*. Sanskrit *h* begins syllables and is voiced; visarga ends syllables and is voiceless.

**Anusvāra is not always [m].** Readers often say every *ṃ* as [m]. Before *k* it is [ŋ], before *t* [n̪], and before *s* or *h* it nasalises the vowel. Only before *p*, *b* and *m*, and at a pause, is it [m].

**Visarga is a sound, not a silent mark.** Say a short breath after the vowel: *punaḥ* is not *puna*. It also makes the syllable heavy (san-01-02).

**r is a tap.** Do not use the English *r* of *red*, and do not roll it long as in Spanish *perro*.

## Exercises

1. Write the sounds of *śānti*, *puruṣa* and *sūrya*, and name the place of each sibilant.
2. How is the anusvāra pronounced in *saṃkalpa*, and how in *saṃsāra*? Why the difference?
3. Which vowel is the partner of *v*, and which of *y*? Give the change you saw in san-00-02 that shows one of these pairs at work.
4. A reader says *hasta* with the *h* of English *hat* and *punaḥ* as *puna*. Correct both.
5. Is the first syllable of *duḥkha* heavy or light? And of *siṃha*?
6. Why does Whitney call *r* a retroflex sound, when English speakers make it elsewhere?
7. Put in dictionary order: *hasta*, *vana*, *śānti*, *yoga*, *sūrya*, *rūpa*.

## Worked answers

1. [ɕaːn̪t̪i], [puɾuʂɐ], [suːɾjɐ]. *ś* is palatal, *ṣ* retroflex, *s* dental.
2. In *saṃkalpa* it is [ŋ], the velar nasal, because *k* follows and a nasal before a stop takes the stop's row. In *saṃsāra* it is a nasalised vowel [ɐ̃], because *s* is not a stop and the mouth does not close.
3. *v* goes with *u*, *y* with *i*. In *karmaṇy eva*, the final *i* of *karmaṇi* turned into *y* before a vowel.
4. Sanskrit *h* is voiced and breathy, [ɦɐst̪ɐ]. Visarga is a real breath after the vowel: [pun̪ɐh].
5. Both are heavy. In *duḥkha* the visarga follows the vowel; in *siṃha* the anusvāra does. Both count as consonants for weight (Whitney §79).
6. Because of how it behaves. In the sandhi rules *r* acts like the retroflexes: for example it turns a following *n* in the same word into *ṇ* (Whitney §52). That is evidence for its place of articulation.
7. *yoga*, *rūpa*, *vana*, *śānti*, *sūrya*, *hasta*. The semivowels come in the order *y r l v*, then the sibilants *ś ṣ s*, then *h*.
