## The idea

Sanskrit has been written in many scripts. Whitney (§1) notes that Indians wrote it, in each region, in the alphabet of their own vernacular. All of these scripts go back to one ancestor. Macdonell (§3) explains that a Semitic script reached northern India, probably through Mesopotamia, and that its Indian adaptation, called **Brāhmī**, is known from coins and inscriptions of the third century BC. The oldest Indian inscriptions of known date are the edicts of the emperor Aśoka, from about the middle of that century (Whitney §2). From Brāhmī descend all the later Indian scripts, including the one used in this guide: **Devanagari**, *devanāgarī*. The name is obscure. *Nāgarī* is "of the city" (from *nagara* "city"), and *deva-nāgarī* perhaps "Nāgarī of the gods" (Whitney §1a; Macdonell §3).

Devanagari is not an alphabet in the European sense. Whitney (§8) calls it "syllabic and consonantal". Its unit is the written syllable, *akṣara*: one or more consonants and the vowel after them. The consonant is the main part of the sign. The vowel is added to it as a small mark, or, if it is short *a*, not written at all.

That is the one rule of this lesson. **Every consonant letter, written alone, stands for that consonant followed by a.** The letter क is not *k* but *ka*. This *a* is called the **inherent a**. To write *ki* or *ku* you add a vowel sign (san-01-06); to write *k* with no vowel you add a stroke below (san-01-07).

The convention is economical. Short *a* is by far the commonest vowel (san-01-02), so leaving it unwritten saves more marks than leaving out any other vowel would.

## How it is built

**Step 1: learn the letters in grid order.** The letters follow the order of san-01-01, so you already know where each belongs. Read the Reference table row by row, saying each letter with its *a*: *ka kha ga gha ṅa*, *ca cha ja jha ña*, and so on.

**Step 2: see how a letter is shaped.** Most letters have three parts: a distinctive shape, a vertical stroke on the right, and a horizontal line along the top. Macdonell (§8) gives the writing order: first the distinctive part, then the vertical stroke, then the top line. The top line joins the letters of a word, so a printed word hangs from one continuous line. Macdonell adds that the line was originally not part of the letters at all but the ruled line below which they were written.

**Step 3: read a word letter by letter.** Take each letter as consonant + *a* and run them together. न *na*, ग *ga*, र *ra*: नगर *nagara* "city". Nothing else is needed for words whose vowels are all short *a*.

**Step 4: name the letters as the grammarians did.** Whitney (§18) explains that a consonant is named by its syllable with *a*, optionally followed by *-kāra* "maker": *ka* or *kakāra*. The one exception is *r*, which is called *ra* or *repha* "snarl".

**Look-alikes.** Several letters differ by one stroke. Learn these pairs side by side:

- ब *ba* and व *va*: ब has a small diagonal stroke inside the loop.
- घ *gha* and ध *dha*, म *ma* and भ *bha*: ध and भ have a gap in the top line over their left part; घ and म do not.
- प *pa* and ष *ṣa*: ष has a diagonal stroke across it.
- ड *ḍa* and ङ *ṅa*: ङ has a dot to its right.
- ट *ṭa* and ठ *ṭha*: ट is an open hook, ठ a closed circle.

## Watch out

**Never drop the final a.** Hindi is written in the same script, and Hindi speakers leave out many inherent *a*s, especially at the end of a word. Wiktionary gives Hindi *kamal* [kɐ.mɐl] for the same letters कमल. In Sanskrit every inherent *a* is pronounced: *kamala*, three syllables.

**The inherent a is short.** It is the dull short [ɐ] of san-01-02, never long *ā*. Long *ā* always has its own sign (next lesson).

**Do not read the top line as a letter.** It is shared by the whole word. Find each letter by its vertical stroke and its distinctive shape.

**Letters are not sounds plus a.** Do not try to "add" the *a* in your head. Learn क as *ka*, as a Japanese learner learns か as *ka*. The removal of the *a* is the special case, with its own sign.

## Connections

Japanese kana work on a similar principle: か is *ka* as a unit, and a separate device is needed to write a bare consonant. Hindi, Marathi and Nepali use Devanagari today. Hindi reads the same letters with many final *a*s dropped, so a Hindi speaker reading Sanskrit has to add them back.

## Exercises

1. Transliterate: रथ, नगर, कमल, गमन.
2. Write in Devanagari: *nara*, *vana*, *jala*, *dhana*.
3. How many syllables does मरण have, and why is its third letter ण and not न?
4. Which letter is *bha* and which is *ma*: भ, म? Which is *ba* and which *va*: ब, व?
5. A Hindi speaker reads कमल as *kamal*. What does a Sanskrit reader say, and why?
6. What did the grammarians call the letter र, and what did they call क?
7. Transliterate and translate: खर, गज, वचन.

## Worked answers

1. *ratha*, *nagara*, *kamala*, *gamana*. Each letter is a consonant plus *a*.
2. नर, वन, जल, धन.
3. Three: *ma-ra-ṇa*. The *n* follows an *r* in the same word, which turns it retroflex (Whitney §46); so the letter is ण *ṇa*. *gamana*, with no *r*, keeps न.
4. भ is *bha* and म is *ma* (म is closed along the top). ब is *ba* and व is *va* (ब has the inner stroke).
5. *kamala*, with the final *a*. In Sanskrit every consonant letter without another vowel sign or a virāma is read with its *a*.
6. र is *ra* or *repha*; क is *ka* or *kakāra* (Whitney §18).
7. *khara* "donkey", *gaja* "elephant", *vacana* "speech".
