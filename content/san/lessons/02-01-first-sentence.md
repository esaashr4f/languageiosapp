## The idea

One Sanskrit word can be a whole sentence. *gacchati* means "he goes", "she goes" or "it goes". You need no separate word for "he": the end of the verb says it.

A **verb** is a word that names an action or a state: go, see, live. Its ending tells you who is doing it. The ending *-ti* says two things at once. The doer is one, not several: this is the **singular**. And the doer is neither the speaker nor the person spoken to, but someone or something else: this is the **third person**. The gloss writes these two facts as 3SG.

The verbs in this lesson are in the **present tense**, the set of forms for what is happening now or is generally true. Speijer (§323) puts it in one line: the Sanskrit present is what it is everywhere, the expression of facts that are present or represented as present. *gacchati* is "he goes" and "he is going"; Sanskrit has one form for both.

Now give the verb a doer with a noun. The doer of a sentence is its **subject**. Sanskrit marks the subject with an ending on the noun. The case of the subject is the **nominative**: a **case** is a form of a noun that shows its job in the sentence. Take *deva* "god". Its nominative singular is *devaḥ*. So *devaḥ paśyati* is "the god sees".

That *-ḥ* is the second piece of logic in this lesson. The nominative singular ending of words like *deva* is an *s*. But Sanskrit allows no *s* at the end of a word before a pause: it becomes the breath *ḥ*, the visarga of san-01-04 (Whitney §145, §170a). A word "before a pause", at the end of what is said, stands in its **pausa** form (Latin *pausa*, "pause"): the form it has with nothing after it. The dictionary lists words as stems, but texts and this guide's morphemes line show every word in its pausa form. Lesson san-02-06 shows what happens to the *-ḥ* when a word follows.

Sanskrit has no word for "the" or "a". *devaḥ* is "the god" or "a god", as context requires.

## How it is built

To say "the boy plays", walk through five steps.

1. **Find the verb's root.** The dictionary gives √krīḍ "play". A root is not a word you can say (san-00-02).
2. **Find the present stem.** The present is built on a stem made from the root. In the commonest type, which the Indian grammarians list first and which Whitney (§744) counts as nearly half of all present stems, the stem is the root plus *a*, with the root's vowel raised to guṇa where it can be: √bhū gives *bhava*, √vad stays *vada*, √krīḍ stays *krīḍa* (Whitney §734). Some common verbs make the stem in other ways. √gam makes *gaccha* (Whitney §747), √sthā makes *tiṣṭha* and √pā makes *piba* (§749a), and √paś adds *ya*: *paśya*. Whitney (§601) notes that the way a root makes its present stem is so varied that it is the basis on which verbs are classified. So the stem must be learned with each verb, and the vocabulary always gives it, as the 3SG form.
3. **Add the ending.** One other person, now: *-ti*. *krīḍa-* + *-ti* = *krīḍati*.
4. **Put the subject in the nominative.** The noun is *bāla* "boy". Its stem ends in *a*. The nominative singular is *bālaḥ*. The gloss cuts it *bāl-aḥ*, because the stem's final *a* changes from case to case and is best counted as part of the ending: think of the *a* of the stem as sitting inside the ending.
5. **Order the words.** The usual order puts the subject first and the verb last (Speijer §16): *bālaḥ krīḍati*. But the endings, not the order, say who acts, so *krīḍati bālaḥ* means the same.

The same steps give *devaḥ paśyati* "the god sees": √paś, stem *paśya*, ending *-ti*; *deva*, nominative singular *devaḥ*.

## Where it applies

The nominative and the third person singular together make the smallest full sentence with a named doer. They apply in these places.

- **What is happening now.** *bālaḥ krīḍati* "the boy is playing".
- **What always or habitually happens.** Speijer (§323) says that things represented as happening always and everywhere are put in the present: *sadā hasati bālaḥ*.
- **What is about to happen.** The present can stand for a near future, as English "I am leaving tomorrow" (Speijer §324). With a word like *adya* "today" context shows it: *adya gacchati nṛpaḥ* "the king goes today".
- **What has just happened**, in a reply such as "I'm here" from someone who has arrived (Speijer §325). You will meet this in dialogue.
- **Narrative of the past.** Stories often tell past events in the present, as English does in "so he walks in and sees the king" (Speijer §§326–327). This "historical present" is common in the fables of part 8.
- **A state rather than an action.** *jīvati* "is alive", *vasati* "lives (somewhere)", *tiṣṭhati* "stands, is standing": the same endings serve.
- **Animals and things as subjects.** *vṛkṣaḥ patati*. Sanskrit makes no difference between a person and a thing here.
- **No subject word at all.** When context has already said who, *gacchati* alone is the sentence. This is the normal state of affairs, not a shortened style.

The nominative has a second use, after verbs meaning "be" and "become": "the boy is a pupil". That is the subject of san-02-07. Other kinds of noun (with stems in *-i*, *-u* and consonants) also have a nominative; their endings come in part 3.

## Watch out

**A root is not a verb.** *\*gam devaḥ* is no sentence: √gam is a dictionary entry, not a word. Use the present stem with its ending: *gacchati devaḥ*.

**Do not build the present stem by analogy.** √gam does not give *\*gamati* in Classical Sanskrit (Monier-Williams lists *gamati* only as a Vedic form): the stem is *gaccha-*. √sthā does not give *\*sthāti* but *tiṣṭhati*, and √pā not *\*pāti* but *pibati*. Monier-Williams gives *pāti* "drinks" only for the Vedic hymns; in Classical Sanskrit *pāti* is the present of a different root *pā*, "protect", so it would say something else. Learn each verb with its 3SG.

**√paś and √dṛś are one verb.** Monier-Williams says √paś is used only in the present: *paśyati*. Most other forms of "see" come from √dṛś, which part 4 teaches. Do not make *\*dṛśati* for "sees".

**The bare stem is not a subject.** *\*deva gacchati* is wrong for "the god goes": *deva* without *-ḥ* is the form used to call out to someone, "O god!" (san-02-09). The subject needs its ending: *gacchati devaḥ*.

**Do not double the vowel.** The stem's *a* and the ending's *a* are one sound: *devaḥ*, not *\*devāḥ* (which is a plural, san-02-03) or *\*devaaḥ*.

**Do not add "he".** English needs "he goes"; Sanskrit does not. *gacchati* is complete. Adding a word for "that one" (san-03-09) is possible but adds emphasis, as Spanish *él va* does beside *va*.

**Do not translate "is going" with "is".** English "the god is going" uses "is", but Sanskrit has no such helper. *\*devaḥ asti gacchati*, with *asti* "is" (san-02-07), is not a Sanskrit sentence. Say *devaḥ gacchati*.

**The verb shows no sex.** *gacchati* is "he goes", "she goes" or "it goes". Only the subject noun, when there is one, says which.

**Why this guide puts some subjects last.** You may wonder why so many examples here have the subject at the end. When a word in *-aḥ* stands before most sounds, texts change its ending (san-02-06). Until you have learned that, the examples keep *-aḥ* at the end or before *k*, *kh*, *p* or *ph*, where it does not change (Whitney §170d). Both orders are good Sanskrit.

## Connections

The ending *-ti* is inherited: Wiktionary traces it to the Indo-European third person singular *\*-ti*, the same ending that Latin wore down to *-t* (*amat*). Spanish and French have since lost even that *t* (*ama*, *aime*). Japanese and Korean also leave the doer unsaid, but there the verb does not show who it is; in Sanskrit the ending states it.

## Exercises

1. Build "the deer runs" from *mṛga* and √dhāv. Show each step.
2. Build "the pupil speaks" in two orders, and say which is the usual one.
3. Take apart *tiṣṭhati*: root, present stem, ending, meaning.
4. Translate *tatra carati aśvaḥ*, and say how you know which word is the subject.
5. What is wrong with *\*gamati nṛpaḥ* for "the king goes"? Correct it.
6. Translate *atra krīḍati bālaḥ*, then replace the boy with the crow.
7. Someone writes *\*kāka khādati* for "the crow eats". What does the missing *ḥ* do to the sentence?
8. Why does *devaḥ* end in *ḥ* and not in *s*?

## Worked answers

1. The present stem of √dhāv is *dhāva-*, and one other person takes *-ti*: *dhāvati*. *mṛga* in the nominative singular is *mṛgaḥ*. *dhāvati mṛgaḥ*, "the deer runs". The usual order would put *mṛgaḥ* first, but before *d* texts change its ending (san-02-06), so for now put the verb first or write the subject last.
2. √vad has the present stem *vada-*: *vadati*; *śiṣya* gives *śiṣyaḥ*. *śiṣyaḥ vadati* puts the subject first, the usual order (Speijer §16), but texts will write its ending differently before *v* (san-02-06). *vadati śiṣyaḥ*, verb first, avoids that and means the same.
3. Root √sthā "stand"; present stem *tiṣṭha-* (Whitney §749a); ending *-ti*, third person singular: "he, she or it stands".
4. "The horse is wandering there" (or "grazing there"). *aśvaḥ* has the nominative singular ending *-aḥ*; *tatra* is an adverb with no ending, and *carati* is the verb.
5. *gamati* is not the Classical present of √gam (Monier-Williams gives it only as Vedic). The stem is *gaccha-*: *gacchati nṛpaḥ*.
6. "The boy is playing here." With the crow: *atra krīḍati kākaḥ*. Only the noun changes; the verb already says "one other person".
7. Without its ending, *kāka* is not a nominative: it is the form for calling out, so the line reads "O crow, (he) eats". The subject needs *-ḥ*: *kākaḥ khādati*.
8. The ending is *-s*, but Sanskrit allows no *s* at the end of a word before a pause: it turns into the breath visarga (Whitney §145). *devaḥ* is the pausa form.
