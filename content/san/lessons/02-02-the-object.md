## The idea

Many actions have a doer and a second party: something eaten, someone seen, a book read. That second party is the **direct object**. A verb that takes one, like "see" or "eat", is called **transitive**. A verb that takes none, like "fall" or "laugh", is **intransitive**.

Sanskrit marks the object with its own case, the **accusative**. For nouns like *deva*, the accusative singular ends in *-am*: *devam*. Now there are two endings to tell the roles apart. *-aḥ* says "the doer", *-am* says "the thing done to".

Because the endings carry the roles, the order of the words does not. *mṛgaṃ paśyati sarpaḥ* and *sarpaḥ paśyati mṛgam* both say "the snake sees the deer". Swap the endings and the meaning flips: *sarpaṃ paśyati mṛgaḥ* is "the deer sees the snake". Speijer (§15) puts this down to the rich inflection: like other languages with many endings, Sanskrit allows great freedom of order. It still has a usual order (Speijer §16): subject first, verb last, and the object immediately before the verb. English fixes its order because it has lost these endings; Sanskrit never needed to.

The accusative does a second, very common job: it names where you go to. *grāmaṃ gacchati* is "he goes to the village". There is no word for "to". Whitney (§274a–b) calls this the goal of motion and says that with verbs meaning "go" it is an extremely common construction. The logic is that the place gone to is the target of the going, just as the thing seen is the target of the seeing.

## How it is built

**The ending.** Take the stem *grāma* "village". As with the nominative, cut off the final *a* and add the ending, here *-am*: *grām-am*. The gloss is ACC.SG. Many nouns you already know, such as *phala* "fruit", *jala* "water" and *vana* "forest", also have *-am* in the accusative. Use them freely as objects; lesson san-02-05 explains why they look different as subjects.

**The spelling of final m.** An *m* at the end of a word is not stable. Before a vowel, or at a pause, it stays *m*. Before a consonant it takes the place of mouth of the next consonant (Whitney §213: before *g* it sounds like *ṅ*, before *t* like *n*), and texts write it with the anusvāra sign ं, transliterated *ṃ* (§213i). So *grāmam* "the village" but *grāmaṃ gacchati* "he goes to the village". This is the first join between words that the guide's examples allow. Lesson san-02-06 sets it beside the joins of *-ḥ*.

**From meaning to form.** To say "the king protects the son":

1. The verb: √rakṣ "protect", present stem *rakṣa-*, one other person: *rakṣati*.
2. The doer is the king: nominative singular *nṛpaḥ*.
3. The thing protected is the son: *putra* in the accusative, *putram*.
4. Usual order, subject, object, verb: *nṛpaḥ putram rakṣati*.
5. Spell the join: *putram* stands before the consonant *r*, so it is written *putraṃ*. *nṛpaḥ* stands before *p*, where its *-ḥ* does not change. Result: *nṛpaḥ putraṃ rakṣati*.

To say "the deer runs to the forest", the forest is the goal: *vanam* in the accusative, before *dh*: *vanaṃ dhāvati mṛgaḥ*.

## Where it applies

- **The direct object of a transitive verb.** *phalaṃ khādati*, *pustakaṃ paṭhati*, *putraṃ rakṣati* (Whitney §§269–270).
- **The goal of motion**, with any verb of going, running, leading, carrying or bringing: *grāmaṃ gacchati*, *vanaṃ dhāvati*, *aśvaṃ grāmaṃ nayati* (Whitney §274a).
- **The person spoken to.** Verbs of speaking take the person addressed in the accusative (Whitney §274c; Monier-Williams gives √vad "speak to" with the accusative): *nṛpaṃ vadati* "he speaks to the king".
- **The thing said.** *satyaṃ vadati* "he speaks the truth". With √vad both can appear at once.
- **Two objects with verbs of asking and leading.** *nṛpaṃ mārgaṃ pṛcchati* "he asks the king the way"; *aśvaṃ grāmaṃ nayati* "he leads the horse to the village". Whitney (§277) says such double accusatives are quite frequent.
- **How long.** *māsaṃ vasati* "he stays for a month" (Whitney §276b). The accusative also measures distance (§276a), once you have words for distances.
- **How something is done.** The accusative of some neuter words serves as an adverb (Whitney §276d): *sukham* "ease" gives *sukhaṃ vasati* "he lives happily". Part 5 collects these adverbs.
- **Verbs that are not transitive in English.** Sanskrit gives an accusative object easily, even to verbs that look intransitive (Whitney §274d). Follow the dictionary, which notes "(acc.)" after a meaning.
- **Later:** after a few prepositions, such as *prati* "towards, about" (Whitney §273, taught in part 5), and with "go to" plus an abstract noun meaning "become" (§274b, met in the readings).

## Watch out

**Not every *-am* is an accusative.** Nouns like *phala*, *vana* and *jala* end in *-am* as subjects too: *phalaṃ patati* is "the fruit falls", not "he drops the fruit". Lesson san-02-05 explains this group. Until then, a noun in *-aḥ* is a subject, and a noun in *-am* is an object if the sentence has another subject.

**An intransitive verb has no object.** *\*phalaṃ patati bālaḥ* does not mean "the boy drops the fruit". √pat means "fall", and a falling has no object, so the sentence has two subjects and makes no sense. "Drop" needs another verb (a causative, part 7).

**No word for "to" with a goal.** Spanish *voy al pueblo*, French *je vais au village* and Japanese *mura ni iku* all mark the goal with a small word. Sanskrit marks it with the ending. Do not look for a preposition; do not leave the goal in the nominative either. *\*grāmaḥ gacchati* is "the village goes". Say *grāmaṃ gacchati*.

**Do not read the first noun as the subject.** In *mṛgaṃ paśyati sarpaḥ* the deer comes first but is seen. English habit says "the deer sees the snake". That is wrong: *-am* marks the one seen.

**Write *ṃ* before a consonant.** *\*grāmam gacchati* is not how texts spell it: final *m* before a consonant is written *ṃ* (Whitney §213i). Before a vowel it stays *m*: *grāmam atra paśyati* "he sees the village here". At the end of a sentence it is *m*: *paśyati grāmam* "he sees the village".

**Do not confuse *ṃ* with the stem's own nasal.** The *ṃ* of *grāmaṃ* is the ending's *m* in disguise. A dictionary has no word *\*grāmaṃ*: look up *grāma*.

**The goal does not need "to" in the translation either.** *gṛhaṃ gacchati* is "he goes home" as well as "he goes to the house"; translate the meaning, not the words.

## Connections

Japanese marks the object with *o* and the goal with *ni* or *e*; Korean with *-eul/-reul* and *-e*. Sanskrit uses one case, the accusative, for both, and Latin does the same with the names of towns: *Rōmam eō* "I go to Rome".

## Exercises

1. Build "the tiger sees the deer" in the usual order. Show each step and each spelling.
2. Build "the thief carries off the book", putting the object first.
3. Translate *gṛhaṃ tyajati putraḥ*. Which word is the subject, and how do you know?
4. Translate *kṣīraṃ pibati bālaḥ*, then make the crow the drinker.
5. Say "he goes to the mountain", and then "he leads the horse to the mountain".
6. Why is *\*phalaṃ patati bālaḥ* nonsense?
7. Write *mārgam* correctly in *mārgam smarati* and in *paśyati mārgam*.
8. Translate *māsaṃ tatra vasati śiṣyaḥ*, and name the job of each accusative.
9. Translate *nṛpaṃ satyaṃ vadati bālaḥ*.

## Worked answers

1. √paś gives *paśyati*. The tiger sees: *vyāghraḥ*. The deer is seen: *mṛgam*. Usual order: *vyāghraḥ mṛgaṃ paśyati*. But *vyāghraḥ* stands before *m*, where texts change *-aḥ* (san-02-06), so for now use *mṛgaṃ paśyati vyāghraḥ*, with *mṛgam* written *mṛgaṃ* before *p*. Same meaning.
2. √hṛ gives *harati*; *pustaka* in the accusative is *pustakam*, before *h* written *pustakaṃ*; the thief is *coraḥ*, last: *pustakaṃ harati coraḥ*. *coraḥ pustakaṃ harati* (subject first, *-ḥ* before *p*) is also good.
3. "The son leaves home." *putraḥ* has the nominative ending *-aḥ*; *gṛham* is the object. (*gṛha* is one of the nouns of san-02-05, but with *putraḥ* as subject it can only be the object.)
4. "The boy drinks milk." *kṣīraṃ pibati kākaḥ* "the crow drinks milk".
5. *parvataṃ gacchati*: the goal in the accusative. *aśvaṃ parvataṃ nayati*: one accusative for what is led, one for where (Whitney §274a).
6. √pat "fall" is intransitive. *phalaṃ patati* means "the fruit falls" (*phalam* can be a subject, san-02-05), and *bālaḥ* is a second subject with nothing to do.
7. Before the consonant *s*: *mārgaṃ smarati* "he remembers the way". At the end, before a pause: *paśyati mārgam*.
8. "The pupil stays there for a month." *māsam* is an accusative of time, saying how long (Whitney §276b). There is no other accusative: *tatra* is an adverb.
9. "The boy tells the king the truth." *nṛpam* is the person spoken to, *satyam* the thing said; both are accusatives with √vad.
