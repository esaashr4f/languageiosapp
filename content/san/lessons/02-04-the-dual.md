## The idea

English counts "one" and "more than one". Sanskrit counts one, two, and more than two. The forms for exactly two are the **dual** (gloss DU). They exist for verbs, nouns and pronouns alike. Whitney (§264) lists the three numbers side by side: singular, dual, plural.

The dual is not decoration. Speijer (§26) says that in every period of the language it is the proper and only way to speak of two, and that polished Sanskrit observes it strictly, while the spoken dialects and Pāli had lost it. Whitney (§265) agrees: the dual is used in all cases where two things are meant. A Sanskrit speaker cannot say "the two boys play" with a plural; he has to choose the dual.

Why should a language keep a separate number for pairs? Many things come in twos: hands, feet, eyes, ears, the two parents, a married couple. Whatever its origin, Sanskrit is not alone: ancient Greek had a dual too, though Speijer (§26) notes that Greek was already confusing it with the plural in Homer. Arabic, which you know a little, still has one: *kitābāni* "two books". So does Slovene. The dual is old, not exotic.

## How it is built

**Verbs.** Three new endings, one for each person, all in pausa with a visarga:

- 1st dual, "we two": *-vaḥ*. The stem's *a* becomes long before *v*, as it does before *m* (Whitney §733): *gacchā-vaḥ*.
- 2nd dual, "you two": *-thaḥ*: *gaccha-thaḥ*.
- 3rd dual, "they two": *-taḥ*: *gaccha-taḥ*.

Compare the singular and plural endings you know. The dual ones look like them with a *-ḥ* (that is, *-s*) at the end: *-tha* "you (several)" beside *-thaḥ* "you two". Read the visarga.

**Nouns.** The a-stem masculine dual has one form for subject and object: *-au*. *devau* is "two gods" doing, *devau* "two gods" done to (Wiktionary table for देव; Whitney §328b). Context decides which. Older Vedic texts often have *-ā* instead (*devā*); Whitney says the Ṛgveda prefers it, but Classical Sanskrit always has *-au*.

**Pronouns.** *āvām* "we two" and *yuvām* "you two", glossed `1DU.NOM` and `2DU.NOM`. Like the other pronouns, they are written *āvāṃ*, *yuvāṃ* before a consonant.

**"Two" and "both".** You do not need a word for "two": the dual already says it. Whitney (§265a) says the dual stands alone when it is clear that two are meant, as with the Aśvins, the twin gods, who are called *aśvinau* with no number word. When the number itself matters, add *dvau* "two", which is itself dual: *dvau putrau* "two sons". *ubhau* "both" works the same way.

**From meaning to form.** "The two warriors bow to the king."

1. The doers are two warriors: *yodha* in the nominative dual, *yodhau*.
2. The verb agrees: third person dual, *-taḥ*: *namataḥ*.
3. The king, bowed to, is the object (Monier-Williams gives √nam with the accusative): *nṛpam*, written *nṛpaṃ* before *n*.
4. Order: *yodhau nṛpaṃ namataḥ*. *yodhau* ends in a vowel and stands before a consonant, so it does not change.

## Where it applies

- **Any two people or things, always.** *kumārau krīḍataḥ*. There is no choice: two takes the dual (Speijer §26; Whitney §265).
- **Natural pairs, with no word for "two".** Hands, feet, ears: *hastau*, *pādau*, *karṇau*. Whitney (§265a) adds that the dual alone is used whenever it is clear that two are meant.
- **With *dvau* "two"** when the number matters or is new information: *dvau putrau*. Whitney's example is "he has two horses", where the count is the point.
- **With *ubhau* "both"**: *ubhau yodhau*. *ubha* has no singular or plural.
- **"We two" and "you two"**: *āvāṃ gacchāvaḥ*, *yuvāṃ pibathaḥ*; the pronoun can be dropped exactly as in the plural.
- **Two different subjects counted together.** Whitney (§265) notes that the dual is used where two are meant "by combination of two individuals", such as heaven and earth. "The king and the pupil go" takes a dual verb (Speijer §28). Joining nouns with "and" comes in san-02-08.
- **A pair named in one word.** *haṃsakākau* "swan and crow" in the reading above is a compound that names two things and takes one dual ending; part 5 teaches it.
- **Dual pronouns as objects.** *āvām* and *yuvām* are also the accusative forms (Wiktionary अहम्, त्वम्), so *āvāṃ paśyati* is "he sees us two". Part 3 gives the other forms.

## Watch out

**Do not use the plural for two.** English, Spanish and French have only "more than one", so *\*dvau devāḥ* or *\*kumārāḥ krīḍataḥ* feels natural. Both are wrong: the noun, the number word and the verb must all be dual. Say *dvau devau*, *kumārau krīḍataḥ*. Speijer (§25) notes one licence: the first person plural "we" may be used for two speakers (or even one). Do not extend it to nouns.

**Do not leave "both" with a plural verb.** *\*ubhau gacchanti*, on the pattern of *ambos van*, *tous les deux vont*, is wrong. "Both go" is *ubhau gacchataḥ*.

**Read the visarga.** *gacchatha* is "you (several) go", *gacchathaḥ* "you two go". *gacchataḥ* is "they two go", and *gacchanti* "they (several) go". A missing *ḥ* changes the number.

**Long *ā* before *v*.** *\*gacchavaḥ* is wrong: *gacchāvaḥ*, as *gacchāmi*, *gacchāmaḥ*.

**Subject and object duals look alike.** *devau* is both. In *vyāghrau paśyāmaḥ* the first-person verb shows that "we" see and the tigers are seen. Where both nouns could be either, the rest of the sentence or the context decides, as with *-am* in san-02-02.

**Do not put *-au* before a vowel yet.** Before a vowel the *-au* changes (*-āv*), a join taught in san-03-07. Until then the examples put a consonant after it.

**Vedic duals in *-ā*.** In the Veda you will meet *devā* for *devau* (Whitney §328b). It is not a plural and not a mistake; it is the older form. Do not use it in Classical sentences.

**Do not look for gender in *dvau*.** *dvau* is the form that goes with nouns like *deva*. Nouns of other kinds take another form of "two" (san-02-05 and san-05-05).

## Connections

Arabic is the nearest parallel for this reader: it too has a dual in nouns and verbs (*kitābāni* "two books", *kataba* "he wrote" beside *katabā* "they two wrote"). Ancient Greek had a dual and was losing it in Homer's day; Speijer (§26) contrasts this with Sanskrit, which kept it strictly. Spanish *ambos* and French *les deux* are words, not grammar.

## Exercises

1. Build "you two are laughing" with √has, with and without the pronoun.
2. Build "the two tigers wander everywhere".
3. Take apart *jighrāvaḥ* and *karṣataḥ*.
4. Say "two horses run" with the word *dvau*. What goes wrong at the join, and what can you write for now?
5. Correct *\*ubhau śiṣyau paṭhanti*.
6. Translate *pādau spṛśati putraḥ*.
7. Turn *jalaṃ pibati haṃsaḥ* into "the two swans drink water", then into "the swans drink water" (several).
8. Someone reads *gacchatha* as "you two go". What has he missed?

## Worked answers

1. √has, stem *hasa-*; second person dual *-thaḥ*: *hasathaḥ*. With the pronoun for stress: *yuvāṃ hasathaḥ* (*yuvām* before *h* is written *yuvāṃ*).
2. Two tigers: *vyāghrau*. Third person dual: *bhramataḥ*. *sarvatra* is an adverb: *vyāghrau sarvatra bhramataḥ*. *-au* and *-a* stand before consonants and *-ḥ* at the end, so nothing changes. (*sarvatra bhramataḥ vyāghrau* would put *-ḥ* before *v*, where it changes: san-02-06.)
3. *jighrā-vaḥ*: stem *jighra-* of √ghrā (Whitney §749a), long *ā* before *v*, first person dual: "we two smell". *karṣa-taḥ*: stem *karṣa-* of √kṛṣ (guṇa *ar*), third person dual: "they two pull" or "they two plough".
4. *dvau* and *aśvau* are both dual, and *dvau* comes before its noun. But then *-au* stands before the vowel *a*, where texts change it: they write *dvāv aśvau* (san-03-07). For now use the dual alone, which already says "two": *aśvau dhāvataḥ*.
5. *ubhau śiṣyau* is dual, so the verb must be dual: *ubhau śiṣyau paṭhataḥ* "both pupils are reading aloud".
6. "The son touches (his father's or his own) feet." *pādau* is accusative dual; touching the feet is a gesture of respect, but the sentence does not say whose.
7. *haṃsau jalaṃ pibataḥ*; *jalaṃ pibanti haṃsāḥ* (with *haṃsāḥ* last, since *-āḥ* before *j* changes, san-02-06).
8. The visarga: "you two go" is *gacchathaḥ*. *gacchatha* without *-ḥ* is the plural, "you (three or more) go".
