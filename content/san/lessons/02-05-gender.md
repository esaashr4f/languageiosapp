## The idea

Every Sanskrit noun belongs to one of three classes, called **genders**: **masculine**, **feminine** and **neuter** (gloss M, F, N). Whitney (§263) says the three genders are those of the other old Indo-European languages and are distributed by the same laws as in Greek and Latin. You know two-gender systems from Spanish and French. Sanskrit adds the neuter, as Latin and German do.

Gender is a grammatical class, not a fact about the world. Nouns for men and women usually follow sex: *putra* "son" is masculine, *kanyā* "girl" feminine. Nouns for things are spread over all three: *vṛkṣa* "tree" is masculine, *latā* "creeper" feminine, *phala* "fruit" neuter. And a few words for people ignore sex altogether. Monier-Williams notes that *mitra* "friend", masculine in the Veda, is mostly neuter in the later language. Whitney (§264a) gives *dārāḥ* "wife", a masculine noun used only in the plural.

Why should this matter? Because gender decides the endings. Whitney (§262) puts it this way: declension shows case and number, but it also shows gender, partly in the stem and partly in the endings. There are three patterns in this lesson:

- **Masculine a-stems** like *deva*, which you know: *devaḥ, devam*.
- **Neuter a-stems** like *phala*. The stem ends in *a* too, but the subject form is *phalam*, the same as the object form. A neuter's nominative and accusative are always alike.
- **Feminine ā-stems** like *kanyā*: *kanyā, kanyām*.

The second thing gender decides is agreement. An **adjective** is a word that describes a noun: "dear", "new", "old". In Sanskrit it takes the gender, number and case of its noun. Speijer (§27) calls this concord: words in the same sentence that point at the same thing agree. *priyaḥ putraḥ* "the dear son", *priyā kanyā* "the dear girl", *priyaṃ phalam* "the dear fruit". The adjective has no gender of its own; it copies the noun's.

## How it is built

**The neuter.** Take the masculine endings and change only the subject and object forms. Singular *-am* for both (*phal-am*). Dual *-e* for both (*phal-e*); Whitney (§328b) explains it as the stem's *a* fused with an old dual ending *ī*. Plural *-āni* for both (*phal-āni*; Whitney §329c). So *phalaṃ patati* is "the fruit falls": a neuter subject ends in *-am*.

**The feminine.** The stem ends in long *ā*: *kanyā*. Subject singular: the stem unchanged, *kany-ā*. Object singular: *kany-ām*. Dual, subject and object: *kany-e*. Plural, subject and object: *kany-āḥ* (Wiktionary table for सेना).

**Spotting the gender.** A dictionary marks it: *phala* n., *kanyā* f. A stem in *-ā* is almost always feminine. A stem in *-a* is masculine or neuter, and only the dictionary or a form like *phalam* as a subject tells you which.

**Making an adjective agree.** Adjectives are listed by their masculine stem with the feminine beside it: *priya*, f. *priyā*. To describe a noun:

1. Find the noun's gender, number and case.
2. Pick the adjective's stem for that gender: *priya* for masculine and neuter, *priyā* for feminine.
3. Give it the ending that the noun's pattern has in that case and number.

"He sees the dear girl": *kanyā* is feminine, here singular object, *kanyām*. The adjective's feminine stem is *priyā*, and its object form *priyām*: *priyāṃ kanyāṃ paśyati*. The endings rhyme because both words follow the *ā*-pattern. They do not always rhyme: in part 3, nouns of other stem types take adjectives whose endings look different.

**Where the adjective stands.** An adjective that only describes comes before its noun (Speijer §16.3). One that says something new about it, a predicate, usually follows (san-02-07).

## Where it applies

- **Every noun has one gender**, fixed in the dictionary: *vṛkṣa* m., *latā* f., *puṣpa* n. "flower".
- **People usually by sex**: *putra*, *kumāra* m.; *kanyā*, *bhāryā* f. But not always: *mitram* "friend" is neuter in Classical Sanskrit (Monier-Williams), and *dārāḥ* "wife" is masculine plural (Whitney §264a).
- **Neuter nominative and accusative are always alike**, in all three numbers. Only the sentence shows which it is.
- **Adjectives beside their nouns**, agreeing in gender, number and case (Speijer §27): *priyā kanyā*, *madhuraṃ phalam*.
- **Adjectives standing for a person or thing**: *vṛddhaḥ* "an old man", *vṛddhā* "an old woman", *bālaḥ* "a boy" (from *bāla* "young"). Many nouns you know began this way.
- **Adjectives as predicates**, "the girl is dear": san-02-07. They agree there too.
- **Two nouns of different gender described together.** Speijer (§28) gives the rule: for persons of different sex the shared adjective is masculine; for things, or things and persons mixed, neuter. You will use this once you can join nouns with *ca* (san-02-08).
- **Number words agree as well.** *dvi* "two" has *dvau* with masculines and *dve* with feminines and neuters: *dve kanye* "two girls", *dve phale* "two fruits" (Monier-Williams under *dva*; Wiktionary द्व). *ubha* "both" works the same way: *ubhe*.

## Watch out

**Do not assume *-am* is an object.** In *phalaṃ patati* the neuter *phalam* is the subject. Earlier lessons kept neuters as objects; from now on, check the gender.

**Do not give a neuter a masculine subject form.** *\*phalaḥ patati* is wrong, by analogy with *devaḥ*. Say *phalaṃ patati*. Equally, *\*mitraḥ* for "friend" follows the Spanish *amigo* or the Vedic usage; the Classical form is *mitram*.

**Do not guess gender from the meaning, or from Spanish.** *senā* "army" is feminine, Spanish *ejército* masculine. *vana* "forest" is neuter, French *forêt* feminine. Gender belongs to the Sanskrit word.

**The adjective copies the noun, not the person.** For "the friend (a man) is dear", the adjective agrees with neuter *mitram*: *priyaṃ mitram*, not *\*priyaḥ mitram*.

**Matching endings are a help, not a rule.** *priyā kanyā* rhymes, but in part 3 a feminine noun like *mati* "thought" takes *priyā* too: agreement is in gender, number and case, not in sound.

**Feminine plural and singular differ only in *ḥ*.** *kanyā* "the girl", *kanyāḥ* "the girls". And *kanyāḥ* is subject or object. Read the visarga and the verb: *kanyāḥ paśyati* is "he sees the girls" (singular verb, so *kanyāḥ* cannot be the subject).

**Not every feminine adjective ends in *-ā*.** Some take *-ī* (*aihika*, f. *aihikī*, san-01-06). The vocabulary always gives the feminine; *ī*-feminines are declined in san-03-13.

**Gender in glosses.** This lesson marks gender on every noun as well. From the next lesson on, glosses mark it only on adjectives and pronouns; a noun's gender is in its vocabulary entry.

## Connections

Latin has the same three genders and the same pattern for neuters: *templum* is subject and object alike, as *phalam* is. Spanish and French lost the neuter and sorted its nouns into the other two. Japanese and Korean have no grammatical gender, and Arabic has two.

## Exercises

1. Give the gender of *vastra*, *latā*, *sarpa*, and the subject singular of each.
2. Build "the dear wife is laughing".
3. Build "he sees the new garments" (*vastra*, plural).
4. Build "the two girls see the sweet fruit".
5. Correct *\*mukhaḥ śubhaḥ* "a beautiful face" and *\*priyaḥ kanyā*.
6. Translate *kathāḥ smarati vṛddhaḥ*, and say how you know who remembers.
7. Translate *ramyaṃ vanaṃ gacchanti kumārāḥ*.
8. Make *alpaḥ vṛkṣaḥ* feminine (with *latā*) and neuter (with *puṣpa*).

## Worked answers

1. *vastra* n.: *vastram*. *latā* f.: *latā*. *sarpa* m.: *sarpaḥ*.
2. *bhāryā* f., subject: *bhāryā*. "Dear" agrees: *priyā*. Verb *hasati*. *priyā bhāryā hasati*.
3. *vastra* n., object plural: *vastrāṇi*. (The *n* becomes *ṇ* because of the *r* earlier in the word, a rule taught in san-03-01; *phalāni* has no *r*.) "New" agrees, neuter accusative plural: *navāni*. *navāni vastrāṇi paśyati*.
4. Two girls, subject: *kanye*; verb *paśyataḥ*. The fruit, neuter object singular: *phalam*; "sweet" agrees: *madhuram*. Usual order with the subject first: *kanye madhuraṃ phalaṃ paśyataḥ*. *kanye* ends in *e* before a consonant, so it does not change.
5. *mukha* is neuter: *śubhaṃ mukham* (adjective first, neuter nominative singular). *kanyā* is feminine: *priyā kanyā*.
6. "The old man remembers the stories." *vṛddhaḥ* is masculine nominative singular, and the verb is singular. *kathāḥ* could be subject or object, but a plural subject would need *smaranti*.
7. "The boys go to the pleasant forest." *vanam* is neuter, here the goal (san-02-02); *ramyam* agrees with it.
8. Feminine: *alpā latā* "a small creeper". Neuter: *alpaṃ puṣpam* "a small flower".
