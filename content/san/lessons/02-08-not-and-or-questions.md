## The idea

This lesson adds six small words that never change their form. They are **particles**: words with no endings, which shape the meaning of a sentence rather than naming anything. Sanskrit grammar calls such words *avyaya*, "unchanging", and Monier-Williams lists each of them as "ind." (indeclinable).

- *na* "not" denies.
- *ca* "and" adds; *vā* "or" offers a choice.
- *eva* stresses: "just this, nothing else". The gloss writes it EMPH, for **emphatic** particle.
- *api* means "also, even"; at the head of a sentence it asks a question.
- *kim* at the head of a sentence asks a yes/no question. The gloss writes the asking use of *kim* and *api* as Q, for **question particle**.

Two of them, *ca* and *vā*, have a fixed place: they come after the word they join, never first. Words that lean on the word before them like this are **enclitics**. Monier-Williams compares *ca* with Latin *-que* and Greek *te*, placed "as an enclitic after the word which it connects"; Latin *senātus populusque* is the same pattern as *bālaḥ kanyā ca*. The others are freer, and their place carries meaning.

## How it is built

**na "not".** Speijer (§401) gives the rule. To deny one word, put *na* immediately before it. To deny the whole statement, put *na* either first in the sentence or just before the verb; before the verb is the plain choice, first is for contrast or emphasis. *bālo na krīḍati* "the boy is not playing". *na bālaḥ krīḍati* puts the denial up front: "it is not the boy who plays".

**ca "and".** Put *ca* after the word it adds (Speijer §422): "the boy and the girl" is *bālaḥ kanyā ca*. Often each member gets one: *bālaś ca kanyā ca*, "both the boy and the girl". With three or more, one *ca* after the last is usual (Speijer §422, Rem. 1). *ca* begins with *c*, so a preceding *-aḥ* becomes *-aś* (san-02-06): *bālaś ca*.

**Two subjects joined by *ca* take a dual or plural verb.** Two people are two: *bālaś ca kanyā ca krīḍataḥ*, dual (Speijer §28). Three or more take the plural.

**vā "or".** It also follows its word (Speijer §428): *jalaṃ kṣīraṃ vā* "water or milk". *A vā B vā* is "either A or B".

**eva.** Speijer (§398) calls it a restrictive: it marks the word before it as "this, and not anything else", and it is "hardly ever omitted when any stress, however slight, is to be laid on a word". It often needs no word in translation; English uses tone of voice, or "only", "just", "the very", "it is ... who".

**api "also, even".** After a word: *aham api* "I too", *kāko 'pi* "even a crow". Monier-Williams gives "also, even, very" for this use.

**Questions.** A yes/no question asks whether a whole statement is true. Speijer (§412) describes two ways:

1. No particle, and the verb moves to the head of the sentence: *gacchasi grāmam* "are you going to the village?" Only context and tone show that it is a question.
2. A particle at the head: *api* or *kim*. *api gacchasi* "are you going?", *kiṃ gacchasi* "are you going?". Speijer (§412*) notes that *kim* often suggests the answer "no", like Latin *num*: "surely you are not going?".

Sanskrit writing has no question mark. The particle, the order or the context marks the question; the translation adds the "?".

**Joins.** *na*, *ca*, *vā* begin with consonants. *eva* and *api* begin with vowels: after *-aḥ*, *api* gives *-o 'pi* (before *a*) and *eva* gives *-a eva* (before *e*), both rules of san-02-06. After *m* nothing changes: *aham eva*, *aham api*. After a final vowel they would merge with it (san-03-07), so this lesson avoids that order.

**From meaning to form.** "Are the king and the messenger not coming to the village?"

1. Statement: the king and the messenger go to the village. *nṛpaḥ dūtaḥ ca grāmam gacchataḥ*: two subjects, dual verb.
2. Negate the verb: *na* before it: *... grāmaṃ na gacchataḥ*.
3. Make it a question: *api* first.
4. Joins: *nṛpaḥ* before *d*: *nṛpo*. *dūtaḥ* before *c*: *dūtaś*. *grāmam* before *n*: *grāmaṃ*.
5. *api nṛpo dūtaś ca grāmaṃ na gacchataḥ*. Speijer (§413) adds that a *na* inside a question expects the answer "yes": "aren't they coming?"

## Where it applies

- ***na* before a single word** denies that word: *na bālaḥ, kanyā krīḍati* "not the boy, the girl is playing" (Speijer §401).
- ***na* first or before the verb** denies the statement. Speijer notes that poets put it almost anywhere.
- ***na* with a noun predicate**: *na vṛddho nṛpaḥ* "the king is not old". With *asti*, "there is no ...", *na* and *asti* merge into *nāsti* (san-03-07).
- ***na* before *ca*, *vā*, *api***: Speijer (§402) says *na* precedes the connective particles: *na ca* "and not", *na vā* "or not".
- ***ca* after each member, or after the last only**: *bālaś ca kanyā ca*, *nṛpo bhṛtyaś ca*. Speijer (§422) adds that *ca* ... *ca* is used even in prose when one thing has two qualities at once: "he is both a sacrificer and a grammarian".
- ***ca* joining sentences**: put it after the first word of the second sentence: *bālaḥ krīḍati kanyā ca hasati* "the boy plays and the girl laughs" (Speijer §422).
- ***vā* ... *vā***: "either ... or" (Speijer §428).
- ***eva* after any word** you want to stress, including verbs and pronouns. After a pronoun it can mean "the same, the very" (Speijer §398).
- ***api* after any word**: "also, too, even". With *na*, *api* gives "not even": *kāko 'pi na jīvati* "not even a crow lives".
- **Questions**: verb first with no particle; *api* or *kim* first. With *na* inside, the expected answer is "yes" (Speijer §413).

## Watch out

**Do not put *ca* or *vā* first.** English "and the girl" leads to *\*ca kanyā*, Spanish *y la niña* the same. Sanskrit puts *ca* after its word: *kanyā ca*. The same for *vā*: *\*vā kṣīram* is wrong, *kṣīraṃ vā* right.

**Two subjects need a dual or plural verb.** *\*bālaś ca kanyā ca krīḍati*, with a singular verb, copies the English feeling that "the boy and the girl" is one phrase. Count the people: two, dual: *krīḍataḥ*.

**Do not join *ca* to the wrong word.** In *nṛpo bhṛtyaś ca*, the *ca* adds *bhṛtyaḥ*. Read it backwards: whatever stands before *ca* is the new member.

**Do not use *kim* for every question.** *kim* first often hints that the answer is "no". For a neutral question, use *api* first, or no particle with the verb first (Speijer §412).

**Do not read *api* as a question everywhere.** Only at the head of the sentence is it Q. In *vayam api gacchāmaḥ* it means "too".

**Do not confuse *na* with *mā*.** Commands such as "don't go" take a different word, *mā* (part 4). *na* is for statements and questions.

**Do not leave *-aḥ* unchanged before *ca*.** *\*bālaḥ ca* is wrong: before *c* the visarga becomes *ś*: *bālaś ca* (san-02-06).

**Do not double negatives for emphasis.** Spanish *no veo nada* uses two negatives for one. Speijer (§406) says two negations in one Sanskrit sentence amount to a strong affirmation: *na na gacchati* is "he does go".

**Do not translate every *eva*.** It is very frequent (Speijer §398). Often stress in English or nothing at all renders it. Translating each one with "only" distorts the sentence.

## Connections

*ca* is placed exactly like Latin *-que* and Greek *te*, which Monier-Williams sets beside it. Japanese asks yes/no questions with a particle too, but at the end: *ikimasu ka*. Sanskrit puts *api* or *kim* at the front, as Arabic does with *hal* and *a-*.

## Exercises

1. Negate *dūto grāmaṃ gacchati* in the plain way, and then so as to say "not the messenger (but someone else)".
2. Say "the two warriors and the king are going" (three people).
3. Say "the boy or the girl is singing", with *vā*.
4. Translate *kṣīram eva pibāmaḥ* and *kṣīram api pibāmaḥ*.
5. Turn *nṛpo 'tra vasati* into a neutral question in two ways, and into a question expecting "no".
6. Correct *\*ca kanyā bālaḥ krīḍataḥ* and *\*bālaś ca kanyā ca hasati*.
7. Translate *api na krudhyati nṛpaḥ*, and say what answer it expects.
8. Translate *vṛkṣo jvalati sahasā vanaṃ ca dahati*.
9. What does *na na tapati sūryaḥ* say?

## Worked answers

1. Plain: *na* before the verb: *dūto grāmaṃ na gacchati*. Contrast: *na* before the word denied: *na dūto grāmaṃ gacchati* "it is not the messenger who goes to the village".
2. Three people take the plural: *yodhau nṛpaś ca gacchanti*. *yodhau* is dual, but the whole subject is three persons. *nṛpaḥ* before *c* is *nṛpaś*.
3. *bālaḥ kanyā vā gāyati*. "Or" offers one of the two, so the verb stays singular. *bālaḥ* before *k* keeps its visarga; *vā* follows the second choice.
4. "We drink only milk" (*eva*: milk and nothing else). "We drink milk too" (*api*: milk as well as something else).
5. Neutral: *api nṛpo 'tra vasati*, or with the verb first and no particle, *vasati nṛpo 'tra*. Expecting "no": *kiṃ nṛpo 'tra vasati*.
6. *ca* follows its word, and two subjects take the dual: *bālaḥ kanyā ca krīḍataḥ*. In the second, two subjects need the dual: *bālaś ca kanyā ca hasataḥ*.
7. "Isn't the king angry?" *api* first marks a question; the *na* inside it expects the answer "yes" (Speijer §413).
8. "The tree suddenly blazes and burns the forest." *ca* joins the second sentence and stands after its first word, *vanam* (written *vanaṃ* before *c*).
9. Two negatives make a strong positive (Speijer §406): "the sun does shine".
