## The idea

Two things happen in almost every Sanskrit story: people speak to each other, and their words are reported. This lesson gives you both.

**Calling.** The form of a noun used to call or address someone is the **vocative** (gloss VOC). "Child, you run fast": "child" is not the subject of the sentence, it is the person spoken to. Sanskrit gives it its own form. For *deva* it is the bare stem: *deva* "O god!", or, to a king, "Your Majesty!" (Monier-Williams notes this use of the vocative as a title). For *kanyā* it is *kanye* "girl!".

Whitney (§266a) points out that the Indian grammarians do not count the vocative as a case like the others, and that it differs from the nominative only in the singular. This guide lists it as an eighth row in tables, as Whitney does, for convenience.

**Quoting.** Sanskrit reports speech and thought word for word, and marks the end of the quotation with *iti* "thus" (gloss QUOT, for **quotative**). Speijer (§491) is clear that the indirect form of English ("he said that he would come") is not idiomatic Sanskrit. A Sanskrit speaker says: "'I will come', thus he said." Speijer (§492) adds that *iti* is in origin a pointing adverb, "thus, so", the same word as Latin *ita*.

There is no word for "that" introducing a report. There are no quotation marks either: *iti* is the closing quotation mark, spoken aloud.

## How it is built

**The vocative of a-stems and ā-stems.** Start from the forms you know.

- Masculine and neuter a-stems, singular: the bare stem, *deva*, *putra*, *vatsa*. Glossed `dev-a` `god-VOC.SG`.
- Feminine ā-stems, singular: *-e*: *kanye*, *bhārye* (Wiktionary table for सेना: *sene*).
- Dual: as the nominative dual: *devau*, *kanye*.
- Plural: as the nominative plural: *devāḥ*, *kanyāḥ*.

**Where the vocative stands.** Speijer (§420) says it generally heads the sentence, at least in prose; poets put it anywhere. The verb that goes with it is in the second person, because the vocative names "you": *deva gacchāmi* "Your Majesty, I am going"; *vatsa śīghraṃ dhāvasi* "child, you run fast".

**Calling words.** An interjection often comes first. Speijer (§419) notes that interjections readily go with vocatives. *he* "O!, ho!" and *bhoḥ* "hey, ho" are common. Monier-Williams explains *bhoḥ* as an old vocative of *bhavat* "sir", and gives its joins: *bhoḥ* before a voiceless consonant and at a pause, *bho* before vowels and voiced consonants (also Whitney §174b). *re* is the rude one, "you there!" (Monier-Williams: "generally used contemptuously").

**Quoting with *iti*.** Build the quotation exactly as it was spoken or thought, then put *iti* immediately after it (Speijer §495), then the verb of saying or thinking with its subject.

1. The words spoken: "We are going." *gacchāmaḥ*.
2. Close the quotation with *iti*: *gacchāmaḥ iti*.
3. Add who says it: *vadanti* "they say".
4. Apply the joins: *-aḥ* before a vowel other than *a* becomes *-a*, and the hiatus stays (san-02-06): *gacchāma iti vadanti*. "'We are going,' they say."

Persons inside the quotation are the speaker's own. If the messenger says "the king lives here", the quotation keeps "lives" in the third person, whoever reports it; if he says "I am going", it keeps "I".

**Joins with *iti*.** *iti* begins with a vowel. After *-aḥ* it gives *-a iti*, after *-āḥ* *-ā iti*, after *-m* *-m iti* (san-02-06). After a word ending in a vowel, the two vowels merge (san-03-07: *gacchāmi* + *iti* = *gacchāmīti*). Until that lesson, the quotations in this guide end in a word with *-ḥ* or *-m*.

**Iti with one word.** A quotation can be a single noun, which is then in the nominative (Speijer §498). This is how Sanskrit names things: "he is famed as 'hare-marked'", "they call him 'king'". English often drops the quotation in translation and writes "as", "called" or "that".

## Where it applies

**The vocative:**

- **Calling or addressing anyone**, usually at the head of the sentence (Speijer §420): *deva gacchāmi*.
- **With an interjection**: *he*, *bhoḥ*, *re* (Speijer §419).
- **As a title**: *deva* "Your Majesty"; *vatsa* "my dear child" (mw1899).
- **Several vocatives**, one after another, or a vocative with epithets in high style (Speijer §420).
- **Dual and plural**: the same forms as the nominative: *kumārau*, *bālāḥ*. The singular is the only distinct form (Whitney §266a).

***iti*:**

- **Words spoken**, closing the quotation: *gacchāma iti vadanti*.
- **Thoughts, knowledge, belief, doubt, fear**: *atra vyāghra iti cintayati* (Speijer §493). English uses "that" or nothing.
- **Questions reported**: the question is quoted as asked: *api grāmaṃ gacchatha iti pṛcchati*.
- **Reasons and purposes**: Speijer (§493) lists the cause or motive of an action and the object of a wish as further uses ("he does it, thinking 'X'"). You will meet them in the readings.
- **Names and descriptions**: a single nominative with *iti*: *śaśāṅka iti prasiddhiḥ*. Whitney (§268b) notes that such a word stays nominative even when, in English, it describes an object: "what they call 'heaven'".
- **The end of a text or chapter**: *iti* closes whole works: "thus (ends) the first act" (Speijer §492, note).
- ***iti* can be absent.** Speijer (§496) says quoting without it, or with another word such as *evam* "thus", is allowed. Expect it, but do not depend on it.

## Watch out

**Do not use the nominative to call someone.** *\*devaḥ gacchāmi* would make "the god" the subject of "I go", which cannot be. To address, use the vocative: *deva gacchāmi*.

**Do not give the vocative a third-person verb.** *\*vatsa dhāvati* is "child, he runs", which talks to one person about another. Talking to the child: *vatsa dhāvasi*.

**The vocative is not a lost *-aḥ*.** Before a consonant, *-aḥ* never shows as *-a*. So *deva gacchāmi* can only be a vocative. Before a vowel it is ambiguous: *deva iti* could be the vocative *deva* or *devaḥ* with its *ḥ* lost (san-02-06). Then read the sense.

**Do not change persons in a quotation.** English reports "he said he was going"; Sanskrit quotes "'I am going,' he said". *\*gacchati iti vadati* for "he says that he is going" means "'He is going,' he says", about someone else. For the speaker himself the quotation keeps the first person.

**Do not put *iti* before the words.** Spanish *dice que...* and French *il dit que...* put the reporting word first. *iti* comes after the quotation (Speijer §495). The verb of saying may stand before the quotation or after *iti*, but *iti* itself follows what is quoted.

**Do not translate every *iti* as "thus".** "'We are going,' thus they say" is stiff. Usually it becomes quotation marks, "that", or nothing (Speijer §492).

**Do not forget the joins before *iti*.** *\*gacchāmaḥ iti* is the unjoined form; texts write *gacchāma iti*. And a vowel before *iti* merges with it (*gacchāmīti*), which san-03-07 teaches.

**Do not confuse *bho* and *-o*.** *bho* is *bhoḥ* before a voiced sound, an interjection, not a noun ending in *-aḥ*.

**Do not look for a dual or plural vocative ending.** Whitney (§266a) notes that outside the singular the vocative differs from the nominative only by accent, which Classical texts do not mark. *kanyāḥ* may be "the girls" or "girls!"; the second-person verb or the head position shows which.

## Connections

Japanese quotes almost exactly like Sanskrit: the words, then a quotative particle, then the verb: *ikimasu to iimashita* "'I'll go,' (he) said". Korean does the same with *-ra-go* or *-da-go*. Spanish and French put *que* first and shift the persons; Sanskrit, like Japanese, quotes word for word. Latin and Greek, which Speijer contrasts with Sanskrit here, prefer indirect speech.

## Exercises

1. Address a king and say "I am going to the forest".
2. Say "Girls, you are singing" (several girls).
3. Correct *\*putraḥ kiṃ śocasi* "son, are you grieving?".
4. Quote: "'We two are going to the village,' the boys say" (two boys).
5. A text has *mṛṣā vadasīti krudhyati nṛpaḥ*. Find the quotation, translate, and explain the long *ī*.
6. Translate *ācāryo vīrāḥ stha iti vadati*: who are the heroes?
7. Say "the pupil thinks there is a snake here" with *iti*.
8. Undo the joins in *bho deva* and *dūta iti*.

## Worked answers

1. The vocative heads the sentence: *deva vanaṃ gacchāmi*. *deva* before *v* is unambiguous; *vanam* before *g* is written *vanaṃ*.
2. The plural vocative is the nominative plural: *kanyāḥ*. Verb second plural: *gāyatha*. *kanyā gāyatha*: *-āḥ* before the voiced *g* loses its *ḥ* (san-02-06).
3. The son is being addressed, so the vocative: *putra kiṃ śocasi*. (*kim* first makes it a question, perhaps hoping for "no".)
4. The boys' own words: "we two are going to the village": *grāmaṃ gacchāvaḥ*. Then *iti*, then the verb with a dual subject: *vadataḥ kumārau*. Joins: *gacchāvaḥ* before *i* is *gacchāva*. *grāmaṃ gacchāva iti vadataḥ kumārau* (*vadataḥ* before *k* keeps its visarga).
5. The quotation is *mṛṣā vadasi* "you speak falsely", closed by *iti*; *krudhyati nṛpaḥ* "the king is angry". "'You are lying!' The king is angry." *vadasi* ends in *i* and *iti* begins with *i*: the two vowels have merged into one long *ī*, *vadasīti*, a join taught in san-03-07.
6. "'You are heroes,' says the teacher." The heroes are the people the teacher is talking to: "you" (*stha*, second plural) belongs to the teacher's words.
7. The thought, as the pupil thinks it: "here (is) a snake", *atra sarpaḥ*. Close it with *iti*, then the verb of thinking and its subject: *atra sarpa iti cintayati śiṣyaḥ*. *sarpaḥ* before *i* becomes *sarpa* (san-02-06). The thought ends in *-ḥ*, so no vowel join arises.
8. *bhoḥ deva* (the interjection *bhoḥ* before the voiced *d*); *dūtaḥ iti* (*-aḥ* before *i* becomes *-a*).
