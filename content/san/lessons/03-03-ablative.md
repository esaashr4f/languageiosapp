## The idea

The dative of san-03-02 points toward something. The **ablative** (gloss ABL) points away. Whitney (§289) calls it the "from-case", used "to express removal, separation, distinction, issue". The fruit falls *from* the tree; the deer is freed *from* the snare; anger arises *from* greed.

From that one image come four families of use:

- **Separation and movement away**: falling from, coming from, freeing from, protecting from.
- **Source and origin**: arising from, being born from, hearing from.
- **Cause**: "from greed", meaning "because of greed". Whitney (§291a) notes that this is "especially frequent in the later language"; it is the commonest way to say "because" with a noun.
- **Distinction and comparison**: "other than", "better than". Comparison waits for san-05-06, but the logic is the same: what you are measured *from*.

Fear belongs here too. Sanskrit pictures being afraid as recoiling *from* something (Whitney §292a): the tiger is in the ablative.

## How it is built

**The endings.** Only masculine and neuter a-stems have an ablative singular of their own: *-āt*. Feminine ā-stems use *-āyāḥ*, which is also their genitive singular (san-03-04). Dual *-ābhyām* and plural *-ebhyaḥ*, *-ābhyaḥ* you already know from the dative: the two cases share them (Wiktionary देव, सेना).

- *dev-āt*, *dev-ābhyām*, *dev-ebhyaḥ*
- *sen-āyāḥ*, *sen-ābhyām*, *sen-ābhyaḥ*

So the ablative is easy to spot only in one place, the a-stem singular. Everywhere else a form is "ablative or dative" (plural, dual) or "ablative or genitive" (feminine singular), and the sentence decides. This is normal in Sanskrit: outside the a-stems and the pronouns, every noun in this part has one form for the ablative and genitive singular (you will see it from san-03-12 on).

**Joining *-āt*.** The ablative singular is the first ending in this guide that ends in a stop consonant. Final stops change before many sounds (*vinayāt* + *yāti* becomes *vinayād yāti*), and those joins are taught in san-04-07. They do not change before *k, kh, p, ph, t, th, s*, or at the end of a sentence. Until san-04-07 the constructed examples put *-āt* only there, and the attested lines that have other joins say so in the note.

**From meaning to form.** "Out of greed the thief steals the gold."

1. Verb: √hṛ "take, steal", *harati*.
2. Subject: *coraḥ*. Object: *suvarṇam*.
3. Cause: *lobha* "greed", ablative singular *lobhāt*.
4. Order: subject, cause, object, verb: *coraḥ lobhāt suvarṇam harati*.
5. Joins: *coraḥ* before *l*, voiced: *coro*. *lobhāt* before *s*: no change. *suvarṇam* before *h*: *suvarṇaṃ*. Result: *coro lobhāt suvarṇaṃ harati*.

## Where it applies

- **Movement away from a place or thing**: *vṛkṣāt patati*, *svargāt patati*.
- **Separation, release and removal**: *pāśebhyo muñcati*; *vinā* "without" can take the ablative, though it more often takes the instrumental or accusative (mw1899): *vinā bandhanāt* "without a fetter".
- **Protection and defence from**: *corebhyo rakṣati* (Whitney §290).
- **Fear of**: √tras "tremble, fear"; the noun *bhaya* "fear" also takes the ablative of what is feared: *bhayaṃ vyāghrāt* "fear of the tiger" (Whitney §292a quotes "of whom the world is not afraid").
- **Source and origin**: *lobhāt krodhaḥ prabhavati*; "born from", "hear from" (Whitney §291).
- **Cause, "because of"**: *lobhāt*, *krodhāt*, *bhayāt* "out of fear". In the commentaries and the śāstras this is a fixed way of giving reasons, "because of X" (Whitney §291a).
- **"Other than" and comparison**: Whitney (§292b) quotes the Hitopadeśa's "who else than a friend?" with the friend in the ablative. Comparatives come in san-05-06.
- **Distance**: *dūra* "far" takes the ablative or the genitive of the place one is far from (mw1899).
- **"From ... up to"**: the particle *ā* before an ablative means "until": "until the sixteenth year" (Whitney §293c). It comes back with the adverbs of san-05-03.
- **One out of several**: occasionally the ablative does the genitive's work, "one of them" (Whitney §292d).

## Watch out

**Hearing or receiving from a person: expect two cases.** Verbs of hearing and receiving take the ablative (Whitney §291), but texts also use the genitive (Whitney §297d). When you read, accept both; when you build, use the ablative.

**The ablative is not the instrumental.** "By greed" and "from greed" overlap in English, and the two cases overlap in Sanskrit too (Whitney §291a says the causal ablative "borders on instrumental constructions"). But for a tool, a companion or a road you need the instrumental: *\*khaḍgāt tāḍayati* means "strikes from the sword". Correct: *khaḍgena tāḍayati* (san-03-01).

**Do not use the ablative for the goal.** "He goes to the village" is accusative (san-02-02). *\*grāmāt gacchati* says the opposite: "he goes from the village".

**Feminine ā-stems have no *\*-āt*.** *\*latāt* applies the a-stem ending to a feminine. The ablative of *latā* is *latāyāḥ*.

**Do not read every *-āyāḥ* as an ablative.** It is also the genitive. *latāyāḥ puṣpam* alone is "the creeper's flower"; with *patati* it is ambiguous, and only sense decides. When you write, choose an order that makes your meaning clear.

**Do not read every *-ebhyaḥ* as a dative.** *corebhyo rakṣati* is "protects from thieves", not "for thieves". The verb tells you which way the action points.

**Do not leave *-āt* unchanged before a voiced sound in a real text.** *vinayāt yāti* is wrong; editions print *vinayād yāti*. When you read *-ād*, *-ān*, *-āc* or *-āj* at the end of a word, undo it to *-āt* and look for an ablative (san-04-07 gives the rules).

**Do not confuse *-āt* with the neuter *tat*.** Both end in *t*, but *tat* "that" (san-03-09) is not an ablative. The ablative of *tad* is *tasmāt*.

## Connections

Latin's ablative is named after this same "from" use (*ab-latus*, "carried away"), but it also absorbed the instrumental and the locative, so it does work that Sanskrit spreads over three cases. Japanese *kara* and Korean *-eseo* (from a place) or *-buteo* (from a time) mark the starting point; Japanese also uses *kara* for "because", as Sanskrit uses the ablative.

## Exercises

1. Give the ablative singular and plural of *kūpa*, *prāsāda*, *mālā* and *kṣetra*.
2. Build "The boy falls from the peak", with *śikhara*.
3. Build "The girls are afraid of the snakes."
4. Translate *bālāḥ prāsādāt paśyanti*, and say what the ablative does here.
5. Translate *nṛpo bhayāt tatra na gacchati*, and explain why *bhayāt* is unchanged.
6. Find and fix the error: *\*mṛgo vyāghreṇa trasyati* for "the deer is afraid of the tiger".
7. Find and fix the error: *\*kanyā latāt puṣpaṃ harati* for "the girl takes a flower from the creeper".
8. In *lobhān mohaś ca*, undo the joins and name the case of each noun.

## Worked answers

1. *kūpāt*, *kūpebhyaḥ*; *prāsādāt*, *prāsādebhyaḥ*; *mālāyāḥ*, *mālābhyaḥ*; *kṣetrāt*, *kṣetrebhyaḥ* (this lesson).
2. *bālaḥ śikharāt patati*. *śikhara* is neuter, but its ablative is the same as a masculine's. *bālaḥ* keeps the visarga before *ś*; *śikharāt* before *p* is unchanged (san-02-06, this lesson).
3. *kanyāḥ sarpebhyas trasyanti*. Fear takes the ablative; *-ebhyaḥ* before *t* becomes *-ebhyas* (san-02-06); *kanyāḥ* before *s* keeps its visarga.
4. "The boys look out from the palace." The ablative gives the point the seeing starts from: they are in the palace, looking out. English says "from" too.
5. "Out of fear the king does not go there." *bhayāt* is a causal ablative; it stands before *t*, where a final *t* does not change (this lesson, san-04-07).
6. Fear takes the ablative, not the instrumental: *mṛgo vyāghrāt trasyati* (Whitney §292a).
7. *latā* is feminine: its ablative is *latāyāḥ*. *kanyā latāyāḥ puṣpaṃ harati*. *latāyāḥ* before *p* keeps its visarga (san-02-06).
8. *lobhāt mohaḥ ca*. *lobhāt* is ablative singular (source: "from greed"), changed to *lobhān* before the nasal *m* (san-04-07). *mohaḥ* is nominative singular, and before *c* its *ḥ* becomes *ś* (san-02-06).
