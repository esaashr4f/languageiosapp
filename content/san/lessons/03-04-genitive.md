## The idea

Every case so far has linked a noun to the verb: who acts, what is acted on, with what, for whom, from where. The **genitive** (gloss GEN) is different. Its proper job, Whitney says (§294a), is "adjectival": it links a noun to another noun. *nṛpasya putraḥ* is "the king's son", "the son of the king". The genitive *nṛpasya* tells you which son, as an adjective would.

So the basic question for a genitive is not "what does the verb do to it?" but "which other noun does it belong to?". Usually the answer is close by, before or after it.

From that core the genitive spread. Whitney (§294b) says its use "has become much extended, especially in the later language", until it "often bears the aspect of being a substitute for other cases". In Classical texts you will find the genitive:

- **with nouns**: owner, part of a whole, the doer or object of an action noun ("fear *of the tiger*");
- **with adjectives**: "dear *to* the king", "best *of* heroes", "like *him*";
- **with verbs**: "remember *someone*", "rule *over*", and very often "give *to*", "tell *to*" in place of the dative.

The last one matters most for reading. When a Hitopadeśa sentence says "it was made known *of the king*", read "to the king".

## How it is built

**The endings.** Masculine and neuter a-stems: *-asya*, dual *-ayoḥ*, plural *-ānām*. Feminine ā-stems: *-āyāḥ* (the same form as the ablative, san-03-03), dual *-ayoḥ*, plural *-ānām* (Wiktionary देव, सेना, प्रजा).

- *dev-asya*, *dev-ayoḥ*, *dev-ānām*
- *sen-āyāḥ*, *sen-ayoḥ*, *sen-ānām*

The genitive plural has an *n* followed by a vowel, so the n-to-ṇ rule of san-03-01 applies: *nṛpāṇām*, *putrāṇām*, *vīrāṇām*, *vṛkṣāṇām*, but *rathānām* (the dental *th* blocks) and *prajānām* (the palatal *j* blocks). Run the test as before.

The dual *-ayoḥ* is shared with the locative dual (san-03-05). Its *-oḥ* is "another vowel" before a visarga, so before a voiced sound it becomes *-or* (san-02-06): *putrayor gṛham*.

**Where it stands.** In prose the genitive usually comes before the noun it belongs to: *nṛpasya putraḥ*. It can follow, and in verse it often does. Nothing marks which noun it goes with except sense and nearness, so look for the nearest noun that makes sense.

**From meaning to form.** "The protection of the subjects is the duty of kings."

1. No verb is needed: "X (is) Y" (san-02-07).
2. Subject: *rakṣaṇa* "protecting, protection", neuter, nominative *rakṣaṇam*. (The *ṇ* is the rule at work: *r*, then *kṣ*, then *a*, then *n*.)
3. Whose protection? The subjects are what is protected: genitive plural of *prajā*. Does the rule apply? *r*, then *j*, a palatal: blocked. *prajānām*.
4. Predicate: *dharmaḥ*, nominative.
5. Whose duty? Kings': genitive plural of *nṛpa*. *ṛ*, then *p*, then *ā*: nothing blocks. *nṛpāṇām*.
6. Order, genitive first: *prajānām rakṣaṇam nṛpāṇām dharmaḥ*.
7. Joins: *prajānām* before *r*: *prajānāṃ*; *rakṣaṇam* before *n*: *rakṣaṇaṃ*; *nṛpāṇām* before *dh*: *nṛpāṇāṃ*. Result: *prajānāṃ rakṣaṇaṃ nṛpāṇāṃ dharmaḥ*.

Notice that *prajānām* is an **objective genitive**: the subjects are protected, they do not protect. With an action noun the genitive can name either the doer or the one acted on (Whitney §295); context decides.

## Where it applies

**With a noun:**

- **Owner or belonging**: *nṛpasya putraḥ*, *kanyāyā mālā*. Whitney (§295) calls this "the commonest of all".
- **Part of a whole**: *vīrāṇām uttamaḥ* "best of heroes", (Whitney §296a).
- **Doer or object of an action noun**: *prajānāṃ rakṣaṇam* "protection of the subjects" (objective); *nṛpasya krodhaḥ* "the king's anger" (subjective).
- **Pronouns**: "my", "his" are genitives of the pronouns (*mama*, *tasya*, san-03-08, san-03-09). Sanskrit rarely uses a possessive adjective instead (Whitney §295a).

**With an adjective:**

- **Dear, hateful, known to**: *priyo nṛpasya* (Whitney §296b).
- **Like, equal to**: *tulya* and *sama* take the genitive as well as the instrumental (mw1899): *nṛpasya tulyaḥ*.
- **Good for**: *hita* (san-03-02) with the genitive or the dative.
- **Master of, lord of**: *īśvara* "lord" and *adhipa* "ruler" take the genitive of what is ruled: *adhipo rāṣṭrasya* "the ruler of the realm".

**With a verb:**

- **Giving, telling, announcing**, in place of the dative: *viprasya dadāti* (Whitney §297a).
- **Remembering, thinking of, caring for**: *mitrasya smarati* (Whitney §297b).
- **Ruling over**: verbs and nouns of power (Whitney §297c).
- **Fearing and receiving from**, in place of the ablative (Whitney §297d).
- **As predicate**: *rājyaṃ nṛpasya* "the kingdom is the king's" (Whitney §298).

**With words of place:** nouns used like prepositions take the genitive: "in the middle of", "for the sake of", "above". They come with the locative (san-03-05) and with the adverbs of san-05-03 (Whitney §299a).

**Time after which:** "after a long time" is sometimes a genitive in narrative (Whitney §300a).

**The genitive absolute**, "while X was ...", waits for participles (san-06-08).

## Watch out

**The genitive does not go with the verb in the usual way.** *\*nṛpasya gacchati* for "the king goes" makes the subject a genitive. The doer of an active verb is always nominative: *nṛpo gacchati*.

**"Of" in English is often not a genitive.** "Made of gold" is a compound or an adjective, not *\*suvarṇasya*: Whitney (§295) notes the genitive of material "hardly" occurs. "The city of Ayodhyā" (apposition) is two nouns in the same case, not *\*ayodhyāyāḥ nagaram*. "Afraid of" takes the ablative (san-03-03); "proud of" and "full of" will take the instrumental or locative.

**Do not forget the n-to-ṇ rule in *-ānām*.** *\*nṛpānām* and *\*putrānām* are wrong: *nṛpāṇām*, *putrāṇām*. And do not overapply it: *\*prajāṇām* is wrong, because the palatal *j* blocks.

**Do not read *-āyāḥ* as an ablative by habit.** It is either. *latāyāḥ puṣpam* "the creeper's flower"; *latāyāḥ patati* "falls from the creeper".

**Do not read *-ayoḥ* as a locative by habit.** It is also either (san-03-05). *putrayor gṛham* "the house of the two sons"; *putrayor* with "in" or "on" would be locative.

**Do not mix up *-asya* and *-āya*.** The genitive is *-asya*, the dative *-āya*: *nṛpasya* "of the king", *nṛpāya* "to the king". In later prose both can mean "to the king" with *dadāti*, but only *-asya* can mean "the king's".

**Do not put the genitive far from its noun in prose.** In verse it may stand anywhere; in your own prose keep it next to its noun, before it, so the reader can tell which noun owns it.

## Connections

French *de* and Spanish *de* do most of what the Sanskrit genitive does with nouns (*le fils du roi*, *el hijo del rey*), and Latin's genitive is a close match in its uses with adjectives and with verbs of remembering (*memini* takes the genitive, as √smṛ can). Japanese *no* and Korean *-ui* link noun to noun in the same way, owner first: *ō no musuko*, like *nṛpasya putraḥ*.

## Exercises

1. Give the genitive singular, dual and plural of *vṛkṣa*, *ratha*, *śākhā* and *kṣetra*, applying the n-to-ṇ rule.
2. Build "The leaves of the tree fall."
3. Build "The girls' garlands are beautiful", with *śubha*.
4. Translate *vipro nṛpāṇāṃ śreṣṭhaḥ*, and say what kind of genitive *nṛpāṇām* is.
5. Translate *dvāraṃ prāsādasya dīrgham*.
6. Find and fix the error: *\*nṛpasya vadati* for "the king speaks".
7. Find and fix the error: *\*śiṣyānāṃ ācāryaḥ* for "the pupils' teacher".
8. In later prose, what can *nṛpasya kathāṃ kathayati* mean, and why?

## Worked answers

1. *vṛkṣasya*, *vṛkṣayoḥ*, *vṛkṣāṇām* (ṛ and ṣ, no block); *rathasya*, *rathayoḥ*, *rathānām* (th blocks); *śākhāyāḥ*, *śākhayoḥ*, *śākhānām* (no trigger); *kṣetrasya*, *kṣetrayoḥ*, *kṣetrāṇām* (the *r* triggers) (this lesson; san-03-01).
2. *vṛkṣasya parṇāni patanti*. Genitive before its noun; *parṇa* is neuter, plural in *-āni*. Does the *r* turn that *n* into *ṇ*? No: the retroflex *ṇ* of the stem stands between them and blocks the rule, so the ending keeps dental *n* (Wiktionary पर्ण: *parṇāni*, *parṇānām*; san-03-01).
3. *kanyānāṃ mālāḥ śubhāḥ*. *kanyānām* before *m* is *kanyānāṃ*; *mālāḥ* before *ś* keeps its visarga; the adjective agrees, feminine plural (san-02-05, san-02-06).
4. "The brahmin is the best of kings." *nṛpāṇām* is a genitive of the whole with a word meaning "best"; *śreṣṭha* takes it (mw1899). *vipraḥ* before *n* is *vipro* (san-02-06).
5. "The door of the palace is long" (that is, tall or wide). *prāsādasya* belongs to *dvāram*; *dīrgham* agrees with the neuter *dvāram*. No verb is needed (san-02-07).
6. The subject of an active verb is nominative: *nṛpo vadati* (san-02-01).
7. *śiṣya* has *ṣ*, then *y*, then *ā*: nothing blocks, so *śiṣyāṇām*. Before the vowel *ā* the *m* stays: *śiṣyāṇām ācāryaḥ* (san-02-06).
8. "He tells the king a story", with the genitive standing for the dative (Whitney §297a). It could also be "he tells the king's story": *nṛpasya* might belong to *kathām*. Context decides.
