## The idea

English tells a sequence with "and": "the king went to the forest and saw a deer". Sanskrit usually puts every action except the last into a special form that does not change: *nṛpo vanaṃ gatvā mṛgam apaśyat* "the king, having gone to the forest, saw a deer". *gatvā* "having gone" is the **absolutive**.

A **converb** is a verb form that cannot end a sentence and links its action to the main verb, like English "having gone" or "after going". The absolutive is Sanskrit's converb. Indologists call it the absolutive; Whitney and older grammars call it the **gerund** (dossier §6.9). It is glossed `CVB`.

It is indeclinable: *gatvā* is the same for a man, a woman or a crowd, in any tense. Whitney (§989) calls it "a stereotyped case (doubtless instrumental) of a verbal noun" with "the virtual value of an indeclinable participle", denoting "an accompanying or (more often) a preceding action". It has no person or tense of its own: the main verb supplies them.

Japanese and Korean readers know this device: *itte* in *mori ni itte shika o mita* "went to the forest and saw a deer", Korean *-go* or *-seo*. Sanskrit narrative uses it on almost every line: two, three or more absolutives before one finite verb are normal. It is the "backbone of narrative" because it keeps the main verb for the main event.

## How it is built

There are two forms, chosen by one rule (Whitney §990): **-tvā** on a simple root, **-ya** on a root with a prefix.

**-tvā on an unprefixed root.** Add *-tvā* to the weak root, exactly as for the past participle in *-ta* (Whitney §991): if you know *gata*, *kṛta*, *ukta*, *dṛṣṭa*, you know *gatvā*, *kṛtvā*, *uktvā*, *dṛṣṭvā*.

- √gam *gatvā*, √kṛ *kṛtvā*, √bhū *bhūtvā*, √vac *uktvā*, √dṛś *dṛṣṭvā*, √śru *śrutvā*, √han *hatvā*, √tyaj *tyaktvā*, √labh *labdhvā*, √budh *buddhvā*, √ji *jitvā*, √bhuj *bhuktvā*;
- √dā *dattvā* (Whitney §991b), √sthā *sthitvā*, √dhā *hitvā*;
- roots and stems that take *-ita* take *-itvā*: √khād *khāditvā*; class 10 and other *-aya-* verbs keep *-aya-* and add *-itvā*: *pūjayitvā*, *kathayitvā*, *cintayitvā* (Monier-Williams under √pūj).

**-ya on a prefixed root.** Add *-ya* to the weak root behind the prefix (Whitney §992):

- *upa-viś* *upaviśya* "having sat down", *pra-viś* *praviśya*, *ā-gam* *āgamya*, *pra-nam* *praṇamya* "having bowed", *pra-āp* *prāpya* "having reached";
- after a root ending in a **short vowel**, *-tya*: *vi-ji* *vijitya*, *ā-gam* also *āgatya*, *upa-kṛ* *upakṛtya*;
- roots in *ā* keep *ā*: *ā-dā* *ādāya* "having taken", *vi-hā* *vihāya* "having left", *ut-sthā* *utthāya* "having stood up";

The *-tvā* form is never put on a prefixed root, and *-ya* never on a simple one, in Classical prose. The epics break the rule (Whitney §990a); the dossier lists this as an epic feature (san-07-08).

**Who does it.** The absolutive belongs to the **logical subject** of its clause, the one who does the main action (Whitney §989). With an active main verb that is the nominative: *nṛpo gatvā apaśyat*. With a passive main verb it is the **instrumental** agent: *nṛpeṇa vanaṃ gatvā mṛgo dṛṣṭaḥ* "the king went to the forest and saw a deer", literally "by the king, having gone, the deer was seen". Speijer (§§7–8) shows how much later prose likes this passive frame.

**Word order.** Each absolutive comes with its own object or place, placed before the main verb: *[vanaṃ gatvā] [mṛgaṃ dṛṣṭvā] [śaraṃ kṣiptvā] taṃ hatavān*. The order of the absolutives is the order of the events.

**How it is glossed.** `ga-tvā` `go-CVB`; `upa-viś-ya` `near-enter-CVB`; `ā-dā-ya` `hither-give-CVB`.

**The chain of thought.** "Having heard that, the king laughed."

1. Two actions, same doer: hear, then laugh. The first becomes an absolutive.
2. √śru, no prefix: weak root + *-tvā*: *śrutvā*.
3. Object of hearing: *tat*. Main verb: imperfect of √has, *ahasat*.
4. *tat śrutvā nṛpaḥ ahasat*. Joins: *tat* + *ś* → *tac ch* (san-04-07); *nṛpaḥ* + *a* → *nṛpo 'hasat* (san-03-07).
5. *tac chrutvā nṛpo 'hasat*.

## Where it applies

- **Sequence**: one action after another, same doer: *gatvā … apaśyat*. Several in a row are normal: *vanaṃ gatvā mṛgaṃ dṛṣṭvā śaraṃ kṣiptvā taṃ hatavān* "he went to the forest, saw a deer, shot an arrow and killed it".
- **Accompanying action or manner**: *saṅgaṃ tyaktvā kuru* "act, abandoning attachment" (Whitney §989: "accompanying or ... preceding").
- **Cause**: *tac chrutvā nṛpo 'hasat* "hearing that (and because of it), the king laughed".
- **With any main verb**: present (*bhuṅkte*), imperfect (*apaśyat*), past participle (*gataḥ*), imperative (*kuru*), optative (*gacchet*), and the future and perfect (san-04-17, san-04-18).
- **With a passive main verb**, belonging to the instrumental agent: *nṛpeṇa gatvā mṛgo dṛṣṭaḥ*.
- **With *iti***, to close speech or thought: *ity uktvā* "having said this", *iti matvā* "thinking so", *ity ālocya* "having considered this".
- **Some absolutives have become prepositions or adverbs**: *ādāya* "taking" → "with"; *uddiśya* "pointing at" → "towards, with regard to" (Monier-Williams). San-05-03 treats these.
- **Negated** with *a-*: *akṛtvā* "without doing" (Whitney §990b: the negative does not cause *-ya*).

## Watch out

**Do not use *-tvā* on a prefixed root.** *\*āgatvā*, *\*upaviṣṭvā* are wrong in Classical Sanskrit: *āgamya* (or *āgatya*), *upaviśya*. The epics sometimes do it (Whitney §990a), and the guide marks those (san-07-08).

**Do not use *-ya* on a simple root.** *\*gamya* for "having gone" is wrong (and *gamya* is a different word, "to be gone to", san-06-07): *gatvā*.

**Do not forget *-tya* after a short vowel.** *\*vijiya* is wrong: *vijitya*. *\*upakṛya* is wrong: *upakṛtya*.

**Do not attach the absolutive to the wrong person.** In *nṛpeṇa gatvā mṛgo dṛṣṭaḥ* the king went, not the deer. In a passive clause, the absolutive goes with the instrumental (Whitney §989).

**Do not use the absolutive when the subjects differ.** *\*nṛpe gatvā prajā aśocan* for "the king went and the subjects grieved" is wrong: the absolutive must share its doer with the main verb. Use two finite verbs (*nṛpo 'gacchat prajāś cāśocan*) or the locative absolute (*nṛpe gate*, san-06-08).

**Do not decline the absolutive.** *\*gatvāḥ*, *\*gatvau* do not exist: the form never changes.

**Do not translate it as a present participle every time.** *gatvā* is usually "having gone", "after going": prior action. English "going to the forest, he saw" is fine only if the actions overlap.

**Do not use the present stem.** *\*gacchitvā*, *\*kurutvā*: the absolutive is built on the root, *gatvā*, *kṛtvā*.

## Connections

The Japanese *-te* form (*itte*, *mite*) and Korean *-go*, *-seo* do the same job: an unchanging verb form that strings actions before one finite verb, which supplies tense and politeness. Spanish *habiendo ido* and French *ayant vu* are the European equivalents, used far less often. Hindi *jākar* "having gone" continues the Sanskrit pattern with a new suffix.

## Exercises

1. Give the absolutive of √bhū, √vac, √dṛś, √labh and √kath.
2. Give the absolutive of *ā-gam*, *pra-viś*, *ut-sthā*, *vi-ji* and *ā-dā*.
3. Join into one sentence with an absolutive, in the present: *bālo gṛham āgacchati. bālaḥ svapiti*.
4. Build "The hunter saw the deer and shot (√kṣip) an arrow" with *dṛṣṭvā*.
5. Build "The pupil took the book and read it" with *ādāya* and √paṭh.
6. Rewrite answer 4 with a passive main verb: "the arrow was shot by the hunter, having seen the deer".
7. Find and fix the error: *\*nṛpaḥ āgatvā upāviśat*.
8. Find and fix the error: *\*vyādhe mṛgaṃ dṛṣṭvā mṛgo 'dhāvat* for "when the hunter saw the deer, the deer ran".

## Worked answers

1. *bhūtvā*, *uktvā*, *dṛṣṭvā*, *labdhvā*, *kathayitvā* (this lesson: weak root as in the past participle; class 10 *-ayitvā*).
2. *āgamya* or *āgatya*, *praviśya*, *utthāya*, *vijitya* (short vowel: *-tya*), *ādāya* (*ā* kept) (Whitney §992).
3. *bālo gṛham āgamya svapiti* "the boy comes home and sleeps". *ā-gam* has a prefix: *-ya* (Whitney §990).
4. *vyādho mṛgaṃ dṛṣṭvā śaram akṣipat*. Same doer, so the absolutive is right.
5. *śiṣyaḥ pustakam ādāya paṭhati* (or *apaṭhat*). The object of *paṭhati* is understood from *pustakam*.
6. *vyādhena mṛgaṃ dṛṣṭvā śaraḥ kṣiptaḥ*. *dṛṣṭvā* now belongs to the instrumental *vyādhena* (Whitney §989; san-04-13).
7. *ā-gam* takes *-ya*: *nṛpa āgamyopāviśat* (*nṛpaḥ* before *ā* loses *-ḥ*; *āgamya* + *upa-* → *āgamyopa-*).
8. The doers differ (hunter, deer), so the absolutive cannot be used. Say *vyādho mṛgam apaśyat, mṛgaś cādhāvat* "the hunter saw the deer, and the deer ran"; the locative absolute of san-06-08 will give a shorter way.
