## A language of the coast

Swahili began on a strip of coast. Its home is the shore of the Indian Ocean in what is now Kenya and Tanzania, with the islands off it: Lamu, Pemba, Zanzibar. People there have spoken it as their mother tongue for centuries.

The name says so. *Swahili* comes from Arabic *sawāḥil*, "coasts", the plural of *sāḥil*. The Swahili call their language *Kiswahili*. The *ki-* at the front is a prefix that names languages, among other things. You will meet it properly in part 3.

From the coast the language travelled inland and became a **lingua franca**: a language that people with different mother tongues use to talk to each other. Today a few million people speak it as their first language, and tens of millions more as a second. Estimates of the total vary widely, from about 50 million in one teaching grammar to 150 million or more in surveys reported by Wikipedia. It is the national language of Tanzania and Kenya, and it is used across Uganda, Rwanda, Burundi and the east of the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

## Family: Bantu

Swahili is a **Bantu** language. The Bantu languages cover most of Africa south of a line from Cameroon to Kenya. Zulu in South Africa, Kikuyu in Kenya, Luganda in Uganda and Sotho in Lesotho belong to the same family. They are not mutually intelligible, but they descend from one ancestor, as the word for "person" shows.

The asterisk marks a reconstructed form, worked out by comparing the languages that inherited it. *Bantu* itself is the plural of this word in some of them: "people".

Bantu is one branch of a much larger family. Most books call that family **Niger-Congo**. Glottolog, the standard catalogue of the world's languages, uses the more cautious name **Atlantic-Congo** for the part it regards as proven. Inside Bantu, Swahili belongs to a small group on the coast of Kenya and Tanzania called **Sabaki**, together with the Mijikenda languages (Giryama is one) and the languages of the Comoros.

Most Bantu languages use **tone**: the pitch of a syllable can change the meaning of a word. Swahili has lost it, which is one reason it is easy to pronounce.

## How it took shape

Nobody knows when Swahili began. A Greek sailors' guide of the first century AD describes trade between Arabia and this coast, and says the Arab captains spoke the local language, but quotes none of it. An Arab geographer of the tenth century gives a royal title that may be the Swahili *mfalme* "king", but the reading is uncertain.

What is clear is the result. The coastal towns (Kilwa, Mombasa, Malindi, Lamu, Pate) grew rich on Indian Ocean trade and became Muslim. Their language kept a Bantu grammar and took in a large Arabic vocabulary. Older accounts, such as Polomé's handbook of 1967, tell the story mainly through Arab settlement and intermarriage, though Polomé too calls the language "strictly Bantu" in its structure. More recent work, summarised by Wikipedia, stresses that the grammar, the sounds and most everyday words are inherited from Sabaki. One count of basic vocabulary found 72 to 91 per cent inherited, and only 2 to 8 per cent from outside Africa. Counts over the whole dictionary give much higher shares of loans, from about 16 to 40 per cent. The figures depend on what is counted, so this guide gives none as fact.

Most loans are Arabic; others came from Persian, Hindi, Portuguese, English and German. Two centuries of Portuguese rule on the coast, from about 1500, left only words.

Swahili was written long before Europeans arrived, in Arabic script. The oldest known documents are letters from Kilwa dated 1711. A tradition of written poetry grew up in the north, centred on Lamu, and is still alive.

## How it spread

In the nineteenth century Zanzibar became the commercial capital of East Africa under its Omani sultans. Caravans left the mainland opposite for the great lakes and the Congo, and trading towns such as Tabora and Ujiji grew up along the routes. Swahili travelled with the caravans. The German and British colonial governments then used it to run their territories.

Christian missionaries wrote the language in the Latin alphabet. Johann Ludwig Krapf began around the middle of the century, and Bishop Edward Steere's handbook, begun in print in 1865 and published in 1870, became the model for later spelling; it introduced *ng'*. The Arabic script was never well suited to Swahili: it has no letters for *e*, *o*, *p* or *ch*. The Latin spelling replaced it in schools and print.

## Which Swahili this guide teaches

Swahili has many dialects. In 1928 a conference of the four British-ruled territories of East Africa, held in Mombasa, chose the dialect of Zanzibar town, called **Kiunguja**, as the basis of a standard. An Inter-Territorial Language Committee, founded in 1930, spread it through schoolbooks, grammars and dictionaries. Sources date the decision a little differently; one teaching grammar gives 1935.

This guide teaches that standard, **Kiswahili sanifu**, "standardised Swahili". It is the language of schools, newspapers and government in Tanzania and Kenya, and every grammar and dictionary describes it, so every form here can be checked.

Where everyday speech differs, the guide gives the common spoken form too, labelled colloquial, and labels forms typical of Kenya. Part 7 describes the old poetic dialects, Sheng (the mixed youth language of Nairobi) and the Swahili of the Congo.

## What kind of language it is

Three facts shape everything that follows.

Every noun belongs to a **class**, marked by a prefix, and words that refer to the noun repeat a matching prefix. This is the subject of the next lesson.

The verb is one word built from pieces in a fixed order: who acts, when, to whom, and the action. One verb can be a whole sentence.

The spelling is regular: each letter or pair of letters stands for one sound, and the stress almost always falls in the same place.

## What you already know

If you know Arabic, you already know many Swahili words in disguise: *kitabu* "book" (*kitāb*), *habari* "news" (*khabar*), *safari* "journey" (*safar*), *saa* "hour" (*sāʿa*). Spanish and French help differently: their vowels are close to Swahili's, and their gender agreement is a small version of the class system. Japanese and Korean will help with the verb: they too stack grammar onto a word, though they stack it at the end and Swahili mostly at the front.
