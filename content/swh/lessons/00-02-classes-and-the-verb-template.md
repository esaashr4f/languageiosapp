## The idea

Swahili grammar rests on two ideas. Almost every lesson in this guide teaches a piece of one or the other.

The first is the **noun class**. Every noun belongs to one of sixteen classes, and the class is usually visible as a prefix at the front of the noun. Every word that refers to that noun, or depends on it, carries a matching prefix: the adjective that describes it, the verb it is the subject of, the word for "my" or "this" that goes with it. This matching is called **agreement**. A Swahili sentence therefore repeats the same signal several times, like a rhyme.

The second is the **verb template**. A Swahili verb is one word built from short pieces in a fixed order: who does it, when, sometimes to whom, the action itself, and a final vowel. Each position in that order is a **slot**. Building a verb means choosing what goes in each slot.

The two ideas meet in the verb's first slot, which carries the class of the subject.

## One sentence, four ways

Here is a short sentence. Do not try to learn the forms yet: they are taught in parts 2 to 4. Watch the prefixes.

:::example
orig: Kitabu kikubwa kilianguka.
morphemes: ki-tabu ki-kubwa ki-li-anguk-a
gloss: C7-book C7-big C7.SM-PST-fall-FV
translation: The big book fell.
note: Preview. Kitabu is a class 7 noun. The adjective and the verb both start with the class 7 prefix ki-.
source: constructed
confidence: constructed
preview: C7, SM, PST, FV
:::

Under the Swahili you see each word cut into pieces, and under that a label for each piece. This is a **gloss**, explained in full in the next lesson. C7 means "class 7". SM marks the subject prefix, PST the past, and FV the final vowel.

Now make the book plural.

:::example
orig: Vitabu vikubwa vilianguka.
morphemes: vi-tabu vi-kubwa vi-li-anguk-a
gloss: C8-book C8-big C8.SM-PST-fall-FV
translation: The big books fell.
note: Preview. The plural of a class 7 noun is in class 8, marked vi-. All three prefixes change together.
source: constructed
confidence: constructed
preview: C8, SM, PST, FV
:::

Swahili has no separate plural ending. A noun shows its number by its class: class 7 is singular, class 8 is its plural. Most classes come in such pairs.

Now change the noun to one that names a person.

:::example
orig: Mtoto mkubwa alianguka.
morphemes: m-toto m-kubwa a-li-anguk-a
gloss: C1-child C1-big C1.SM-PST-fall-FV
translation: The big child fell.
note: Preview. Mtoto is in class 1, the class of people. The adjective takes m-, but the verb takes a-.
source: constructed
confidence: constructed
preview: C1, SM, PST, FV
:::

:::example
orig: Watoto wakubwa walianguka.
morphemes: wa-toto wa-kubwa wa-li-anguk-a
gloss: C2-child C2-big C2.SM-PST-fall-FV
translation: The big children fell.
note: Preview. Class 2 is the plural of class 1. Here all three prefixes are wa-.
source: constructed
confidence: constructed
preview: C2, SM, PST, FV
:::

The echo is not always a copy. Each class has a set of related prefixes, one for adjectives, one for verbs, and others you will meet later. Sometimes they look the same, sometimes not.

:::example
orig: Mti mkubwa ulianguka.
morphemes: m-ti m-kubwa u-li-anguk-a
gloss: C3-tree C3-big C3.SM-PST-fall-FV
translation: The big tree fell.
note: Preview. Class 3 nouns start with m-, like class 1, but the verb prefix is u-. The verb tells you which class the subject is in, even when the noun's own prefix is ambiguous.
source: constructed
confidence: constructed
preview: C3, SM, PST, FV
:::

Notice what stayed the same in all five sentences: *-kubwa* "big", *-li-* "past" and *-anguka* "fall". Only the class prefixes moved.

## What the classes are

The classes are numbered 1 to 18. The numbers are a common Bantu system, used for all the related languages, which is why there are gaps: classes 12 and 13 belong to that system but are absent from standard Swahili. (One reference grammar notes rare traces of class 12.)

The classes are partly about meaning. Classes 1 and 2 hold people. Classes 3 and 4 hold most trees and plants. Class 7 holds many objects, and also the names of languages: *Kiswahili* is a class 7 noun. Classes 16, 17 and 18 are not ordinary nouns at all: they mark places, "at", "around" and "inside". But meaning is only a tendency, and the class of most nouns has to be learnt with the noun. Part 3 takes the classes pair by pair.

If you know Spanish or French, a class is like grammatical gender: *la casa blanca* shows the gender of *casa* again on *la* and *blanca*. The difference is scale and basis. Swahili has sixteen classes, not two, and none of them is based on sex.

## Why the language works this way

Swahili inherited the system. The Bantu ancestor language already had classes marked by prefixes, and its descendants all have them, though many have merged or lost some. Scholars reconstruct the ancestor's word for "person" as *\*mʊ̀ntʊ̀*, with the prefix already in place, and for "years" as *\*mɪ̀jákà*, which became Swahili *miaka*. Polomé's handbook suggests the original classes rested on a classification by meaning, which time has blurred. In Swahili, for example, two ancient classes have fallen together in the prefix *u-*.

The system is still alive. When Swahili borrowed Arabic *kitāb* "book", speakers heard its first syllable as the class 7 prefix *ki-*. So they made its plural the regular class 8 way: *vitabu*. Arabic has no word *vitāb*. The borrowed word was pulled into the class system by its sound alone.

Meaning can also override form. A noun for a person or an animal that happens to sit in another class usually takes the agreement of classes 1 and 2 all the same. Part 3 shows how.

## The verb as a row of slots

The second idea is easiest to see in a verb growing one slot at a time.

:::example
orig: Ninasoma.
morphemes: ni-na-som-a
gloss: 1SG.SM-PRS-read-FV
translation: I am reading.
note: Preview. Subject slot ni- "I", tense slot -na- "now", root -som- "read", final vowel -a. One word, one sentence.
source: constructed
confidence: constructed
preview: 1, SG, SM, PRS, FV
:::

Change the filler of the tense slot, and only that.

:::example
orig: Nilisoma.
morphemes: ni-li-som-a
gloss: 1SG.SM-PST-read-FV
translation: I read. (past)
note: Preview. -li- in the tense slot makes it past. Nothing else moves.
source: constructed
confidence: constructed
preview: 1, SG, SM, PST, FV
:::

Fill an object slot between the tense and the root.

:::example
orig: Nilikisoma.
morphemes: ni-li-ki-som-a
gloss: 1SG.SM-PST-C7.OM-read-FV
translation: I read it. (it = a class 7 thing, such as a book)
note: Preview. The object slot holds -ki-, the class 7 object prefix. Agreement reaches inside the verb.
source: constructed
confidence: constructed
preview: 1, SG, SM, PST, C7, OM, FV
:::

And now a verb with seven pieces.

:::example
orig: nilipokupikia
morphemes: ni-li-po-ku-pik-i-a
gloss: 1SG.SM-PST-C16.REL-2SG.OM-cook-APPL-FV
translation: when I cooked for you
note: Preview. Subject "I", past, -po- "when", object "you", root "cook", -i- "for", final vowel.
source: constructed
confidence: constructed
preview: 1, SG, SM, PST, C16, REL, 2, OM, APPL, FV
:::

Read it left to right and it unfolds like a sentence: I, in the past, at the time when, you, cook, for the benefit of. English needs five words for it.

The slots always come in the same order:

1. subject prefix (who)
2. tense marker (when)
3. relative marker (who, which, when)
4. object prefix (whom)
5. root (the action)
6. extensions (for someone, be done, make do, each other)
7. final vowel (-a, or -e and -i in some forms)

A negative marker joins the row in some forms: *ha-* in front of the subject prefix, or *-si-* immediately after it. Only the subject, the root and the final vowel are present in almost every verb. The others are filled when the meaning needs them. Part 4 fills the slots one at a time, part 6 the relative slot, and part 7 the extensions.

Languages that build words by sticking such pieces together, each with one meaning, are called **agglutinative**. Japanese, Korean and Turkish are agglutinative too. The difference is direction: they add most pieces after the root, while Swahili adds most before it.

## What this means for you

Three habits follow.

Learn every noun with its class and its plural. *Kitabu* is not enough. Learn *kitabu*, class 7, plural *vitabu*. The vocabulary lists in this guide always give both.

Read the front of each word first. The prefix tells you which noun a word belongs to, so it is how you find the subject of a sentence, and how you follow "it" and "they" across a paragraph.

Read a verb from left to right, slot by slot. A long verb is not a single thing to memorise. It is a short sentence with no spaces.
