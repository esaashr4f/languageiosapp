## The route

Read the parts in order. Each lesson uses only what earlier lessons have taught, so skipping ahead will show you forms nobody has explained yet.

Part 1 covers sounds and spelling. Part 2 gets you to short, complete sentences about people: who someone is, what they are doing, where they are, what they have. Part 3 is the class system, pair by pair, with the agreement each class brings. Part 4 is the verb template, filled one slot at a time: tenses, objects, the subjunctive. Part 5 adds adjectives, numbers and linking words. Part 6 builds complex sentences, above all relative clauses. Part 7 covers the verb extensions and special topics, including spoken shortcuts, older texts and the varieties of Swahili. Part 8 reads real texts line by line. Part 9 is reference: tables, vocabulary, a glossary and the book list.

From part 1 on, each lesson has the same shape. First the idea and the reason for it. Then how the form is built, step by step. Then examples, simplest first. From part 2, a section maps every place the construction is used. Then the traps. A table comes only after the pattern has been explained, as a place to look things up. The lesson ends with exercises and worked answers. Do the exercises before reading the answers: building a sentence yourself is the test of whether you understood it.

## How to read an example

Every example has the same layers. Here is one. It is a preview: its forms are taught in part 2.

:::example
orig: Watoto wanasoma.
morphemes: wa-toto wa-na-som-a
gloss: C2-child C2.SM-PRS-read-FV
translation: The children are reading.
note: Preview, to show the layers of an example.
source: constructed
confidence: constructed
preview: C2, SM, PRS, FV
:::

The first line is the Swahili as you would see it printed.

The second line cuts each word at its joins. A hyphen separates the pieces.

The third line is the **gloss**. It labels each piece, word under word and piece under piece. The glosses follow the Leipzig Glossing Rules, the usual standard in linguistics. Meanings are in lower case: *child*, *read*. Grammatical labels are in capitals: SM, PRS. A full stop joins two labels that describe one piece. Tap any label in the app to see what it means. Each label is explained in plain words in the lesson that introduces it.

Then comes a translation, with a literal one where the Swahili order or wording is worth seeing, and a note saying what the example is there to show.

Swahili is written in the Latin alphabet, so the examples need no transliteration line.

## The labels you will see most

**Class numbers** are written C plus the number: C1 to C18. *Wa-toto* is glossed C2-child: the prefix *wa-* marks class 2, and the rest means "child".

**What a prefix does** follows the class after a full stop. SM is a **subject prefix**: the slot at the front of the verb that names the subject. OM is an **object prefix**: the slot before the root that names the object. REL is a relative marker. So C7.SM is "the subject prefix of class 7", C7.OM "the object prefix of class 7".

**Persons** are written 1SG "I", 2SG "you (one person)", 1PL "we", 2PL "you (more than one)". *Ni-* in *ni-na-som-a* is 1SG.SM.

**There is no separate third person on the verb.** "He", "she" and "they" are the prefixes of classes 1 and 2, the classes of people, so they are glossed C1.SM and C2.SM. This keeps one system for people and things. The labels 3SG and 3PL appear only on the two words that mean "he or she" and "they" when they stand alone.

**Tense markers** have short labels: PRS for *-na-*, PST for *-li-*, FUT for *-ta-*, PRF for *-me-*, and a few more in part 4.

**FV** is the final vowel, the last slot of the verb.

A noun with no visible class prefix is glossed whole, with its class after a full stop.

:::example
orig: Nyumba ni kubwa.
morphemes: nyumba ni kubwa
gloss: house.C9 COP big.C9
translation: The house is big.
note: Preview. Nyumba has no separate prefix to cut off, so its class goes after a full stop. COP is the linking word ni, "is".
source: constructed
confidence: constructed
preview: C9, COP
:::

## Notation in the text

In running text a hyphen shows where a piece attaches. *Ki-* is a prefix: something follows it. *-ni* is a suffix: something comes before it. *-na-* goes in the middle of a verb. A verb is quoted as its stem with a hyphen, *-soma* "read", and the vocabulary lists add the infinitive, *kusoma* "to read".

A noun is quoted with its class pair and its plural: *kitabu* (7/8, plural *vitabu*). Learn all three together.

Older textbooks call the pieces inside the verb "infixes". Linguists use that word for something else, so this guide says "marker" or "prefix".

## Spelling and sounds

The guide uses the standard spelling of Tanzania and Kenya, with a straight apostrophe in *ng'*. Stress is not marked, because it is almost always predictable. The few words that break the rule carry an acute accent (*lázima*) in part 1 and in the word lists; the accent is this guide's, not part of Swahili spelling. In part 1, sounds are also written in the International Phonetic Alphabet, in slashes or square brackets.

## Real sentences and made-up ones

In the app, each example and table has a small coloured dot. Tap it to see its label and source.

- **attested**: a real sentence from a real text, with its source. Translations are this guide's own.
- **constructed**: a sentence built for this guide from rules and words that have each been checked. Most early examples are constructed, because real sentences use too much grammar at once.
- **verified**: a form or table checked against a named source.
- **uncertain**: the sources disagree. The lesson says so in words as well.
- **unverified**: not yet checked. You should not see this label in a finished guide.

## Where the facts come from

The sounds and the grammar follow Edgar Polomé's *Swahili Language Handbook* (1967), which is free to read online, checked against two teaching grammars: Thompson and Schleicher's *Swahili Learners' Reference Grammar* (2001) and Almasi, Fallon and Wared's *Swahili Grammar for Introductory and Intermediate Levels* (2014). Word forms, meanings, pronunciations and verb tables were checked against the Swahili entries of Wiktionary. The English Wikipedia article on Swahili grammar served as a map of the topics, never as the authority for a form.

The two classic reference grammars, Ashton's of 1944 and Mohammed's of 2001, could not be consulted for this guide. Where the sources used disagree with each other, the lesson tells you.

The real texts come from Swahili proverbs, folktales recorded in Zanzibar in the 1860s and published by Edward Steere in 1870, the Swahili translation of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the Swahili Wikipedia, and the Tanzanian national anthem. The annotated bibliography in part 9 gives full details.

## How much to memorise

Less than you might fear. The verb is regular: once you know what each slot holds, you can build forms you have never seen. Tables are there to be consulted.

Two things must still become automatic through use: the agreement prefixes of the classes, and the class of each common noun. The exercises and the readings are how that happens.
