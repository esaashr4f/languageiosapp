## The idea

Most Swahili consonants are the ones you already have. *P, t, k, b, d, g, f, v, s, z, m, n, l, w, y* and *h* sound much as they do in English or Spanish. This lesson deals with the few that need care and with the **digraphs**: pairs of letters that spell a single sound.

The Latin alphabet was not made for Swahili. It has no single letters for four common Swahili sounds, so the missionaries who first wrote the language in Latin letters, in the middle of the nineteenth century, used pairs:

- *ch* for [tʃ], the sound in English "church";
- *sh* for [ʃ], as in "ship";
- *ny* for [ɲ], Spanish *ñ*;
- *ng'* for [ŋ], the sound in English "singer".

Each pair is one consonant.

The apostrophe in *ng'* has a history. *Ng* without it spells something else: [ŋ] followed by [ɡ], as in English "finger". The first missionary writers mixed the two up. Polomé records that Bishop Steere's handbook of 1865 introduced *ng'* to keep them apart, and the standard spelling kept it. The difference matters: lesson 3 gives words that differ only in it.

Three letters of the English alphabet are not used: *c* appears only in *ch*, and *q* and *x* do not occur. Four more digraphs, *dh*, *th*, *gh* and *kh*, spell sounds found in Arabic loans. They are the subject of lesson 4.

## How it is built

Read a word from left to right and take these decisions.

1. Is the next letter part of a digraph? Look for *ch*, *sh*, *ny* and *ng'*. Treat each as one consonant. It goes with the vowel after it, like any consonant: *cha-ku-la*, *nyo-ka*, *ng'o-mbe*.
2. Is it *b*, *d*, *j* or *g*? Say a voiced stop as in English, with *g* always hard as in "go" and *j* as in "jam". On the coast you will hear these four as **implosives**: the voice is made while the air is drawn slightly inwards, giving a hollow, popping sound. Wiktionary writes them [ɓ ɗ ʄ ɠ]. Plain [b d dʒ ɡ] are normal for many speakers, and this guide does not ask you to make implosives.
3. Is it *r*? Make a single tap of the tongue, as in Spanish *pero*: [ɾ]. Some speakers roll it.
4. Is it *h*? Say it. *H* is never silent, except as the second letter of a digraph.
5. Is it a consonant followed by *w* or *y*? Say the two together at the start of the syllable: *kwe-li* "truth", *mwa-ka* "year". The *w* rounds the lips while the consonant is made.

*S* is always [s], never [z]. *W* and *y* are as in English "wet" and "yet".

## Watch out

**Do not split a digraph.** *Chakula* has three syllables, *cha-ku-la*, and *nyoka* has two, *nyo-ka*. The *n* of *ny* is not a separate syllable, and the *h* of *ch* or *sh* is not an *h*.

**Ny is one sound.** English speakers tend to say *nyoka* as "nee-oka". Start the word on the Spanish *ñ* sound of *año*.

**Ng' at the start of a word.** English has [ŋ] only in the middle or at the end of words ("singer", "sing"), so starting a word with it feels odd. Say "singing" slowly, hold the *ng* in the middle, then try it again without the "si" in front: *ng'o-*.

**R and l are different letters for a reason.** In standard Swahili *wali* and *wari* are two words; Polomé explains that Arabic loans made the difference meaningful. Some speakers mix them, and one teaching grammar calls this poor standard Swahili. Keep them apart.

**Aspiration is real but unwritten.** A few pairs of words, like the two *paa*, once differed only by a puff of breath after *p*, *t*, *ch* or *k*. Polomé reports that most educated speakers in Dar es Salaam no longer make the difference, though some further north still do. Lesson 3 explains where the puff came from.

## Connections

Spanish speakers have three of the hard sounds already: *ñ* for *ny*, the tapped *r* of *pero*, and a clear *ch*. Arabic speakers will recognise *rafiki* (*rafīq*) and *habari* (*khabar*): the Arabic [q] became Swahili *k*, and the Arabic [x] of *khabar* is written *h* and usually said [h], a change lesson 4 returns to. Japanese has no [ɾ]–[l] contrast; Swahili has one, and Polomé credits Arabic for it.

## Exercises

1. Divide *chakula*, *nyoka* and *ng'ombe* into syllables. How many consonant sounds does each have?
2. Why is *ng'ombe* not written *ngombe*? Answer from the history of the spelling.
3. A learner reads *shule* as "shool". Name the two mistakes.
4. Say which letters in *zawadi* would be implosive in Wiktionary's coastal transcription, and whether you must imitate them.
5. Divide *kweli* and say why the *w* does not make a syllable of its own.
6. *Wali* and *wari* differ only in one letter. What does this pair show, and why did the difference become important?
7. Spell these sounds in Swahili letters: [ˈʃu.lɛ], [ˈɲɔ.kɑ], [hɑˈɓɑ.ɾi].
8. Two words are both spelt *paa*. What can tell them apart in the speech of some coastal speakers, and why does the spelling not help?

## Worked answers

1. *cha-ku-la*: three consonant sounds, [tʃ], [k], [l]; the digraph *ch* is one. *nyo-ka*: two, [ɲ] and [k]. *ng'o-mbe*: [ŋ], then [m] and [b] together (lesson 3).
2. Because *ngombe* would mean [ŋ] followed by [ɡ]. The early missionary spellings confused the two sounds; Steere's handbook of 1865 introduced the apostrophe to mark plain [ŋ], and the standard kept it.
3. The final *e* has been dropped: *shule* has two syllables, *shu-le*, ending in a clear [ɛ]. And the *u* has been made long and English: it is a short, steady [u].
4. The *d*: /zɑˈwɑ.ɗi/. No: plain [d] is normal for many speakers and is understood everywhere.
5. *kwe-li*. The *w* follows a consonant and is said together with it at the start of the syllable, with rounded lips. Only vowels (and syllabic nasals) make syllables.
6. It shows that *r* and *l* are separate sounds in standard Swahili: changing one for the other changes the word. Polomé explains that *r* was once a mere variant of *l* in Bantu words, and the many Arabic loans with *r* made the difference count.
7. *shule*, *nyoka*, *habari*.
8. A puff of breath after the *p* in *paa* "duiker", [pʰ], against plain [p] in *paa* "roof". The standard spelling has never marked aspiration, and most speakers today do not make the difference.
