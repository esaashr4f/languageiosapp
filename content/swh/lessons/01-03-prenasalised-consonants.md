## The idea

Many Swahili words begin with a nasal followed by another consonant: *mbuzi* "goat", *ndege* "bird", *njia* "path", *ngoma* "drum". The same combinations occur inside words: *nyumba* "house", *punda* "donkey".

In these words the nasal and the consonant are said together, as the start of one syllable: the air goes through the nose for a moment, then the consonant is released into the vowel. *Ndege* is *nde-ge*, two syllables, not *n-de-ge*. A consonant started with a brief nasal like this is called **prenasalised**.

There are six such combinations:

- *mb* [ᵐb], as in *mbuzi*;
- *nd* [ⁿd], as in *ndege*;
- *nj* [ⁿdʒ], as in *njia*;
- *ng* [ᵑɡ], as in *ngoma*;
- *mv* [ᶬv], as in *mvua* "rain";
- *nz* [ⁿz], as in *nzige* "locust".

The small raised letter in the IPA shows the short nasal. Linguists disagree on whether these are single sounds or pairs; Wikipedia reports that several studies favour pairs. For you the answer is the same: two letters, said as one onset.

Notice what is missing. There is no *mp*, *nt* or *nk* at the start of a native Swahili syllable, except in a handful of very short words. The reason is history. Wiktionary's etymologies show that *paka* "cat" comes from a Bantu word reconstructed as *\*mpákà*, and *tembo* "elephant" from *\*ntémbó*. Before a voiceless consonant (*p*, *t*, *ch*, *k*), Swahili dropped the nasal. What it left behind, for some speakers, was the puff of breath you met in lesson 2: Bishop Steere already noted in the 1860s that where the *n* is dropped before *k*, *p* and *t*, they are aspirated.

Before a voiced consonant (*b*, *d*, *j*, *g*, *v*, *z*), the nasal stayed. That is why *mbuzi* keeps its *m* and *paka* has none.

## How it is built

1. Find the nasal before a consonant: *m* before *b* or *v*, *n* before *d*, *j*, *g* or *z*.
2. Ask how much of the word follows. If the rest of the word has two or more syllables, the nasal joins the next consonant: *mbu-zi*, *nde-ge*, *ngo-ma*.
3. If the rest of the word is only one syllable, the nasal becomes a syllable of its own: *m-bwa* "dog", like *n-chi* in lesson 1.
4. Inside a word, the nasal and its consonant still go together to the following syllable: *nyu-mba*, *pu-nda*. The syllable before stays open.
5. Read *ng* as [ᵑɡ], with a real *g*. Read *ng'* as [ŋ] alone. The apostrophe is the only difference on the page.

Step 3 is the rule taught in one teaching grammar (Almasi and colleagues). Polomé adds that it is not strict for *n*: some speakers make the *n* of *ndege* a light syllable of its own, and Wiktionary transcribes *nguo* "cloth" with a syllabic nasal. Both are heard. The rule gives the standard you should aim for.

## Watch out

**Do not add a vowel.** English speakers tend to say "em-buzi" or "en-dege". In a word like *mbuzi* the nasal has no vowel and no syllable of its own. Start humming and open straight into the consonant.

**But short words do give the nasal a syllable.** *Mbwa*, *nchi* and *nta* have a syllabic nasal, and in *mbwa* it even carries the stress. The test is how much of the word follows, not which nasal it is.

**Ng is not ng'.** *Ngoa* "jealousy" has [ᵑɡ]; *ng'oa* "uproot" has [ŋ] with no *g*. If you say every *ng* like the *ng* of "singer", you will merge words that Swahili keeps apart.

**Inside a word, the cluster goes forward.** Spanish divides *mundo* as *mun-do* and English hears "pun-der". Swahili divides *punda* as *pu-nda*. This matters for the stress rule of lesson 5, which counts syllables.

**No implosives after a nasal.** Polomé notes that the coastal implosive *b*, *d*, *j*, *g* never occur after a nasal. Plain [ᵐb ⁿd ⁿdʒ ᵑɡ] are always right.

## Connections

Japanese also has a nasal before *b*, *d* and *g* (*shinbun*, *kondo*, *ringo*), but there the nasal is a beat of its own, closing the syllable before it. In Swahili the nasal belongs to the syllable after it: *nyu-mba*. Spanish *tengo* and *mundo* divide like Japanese, not like Swahili. Bantu relatives of Swahili have prenasalised consonants too. Wiktionary's cognates of *nyota* "star" include Kikuyu *njata*, which keeps the *nj* that Swahili has turned into *ny* in this word.

## Exercises

1. Divide *nyumba*, *ngoma* and *mvua* into syllables.
2. Divide *mbwa* and *mbuzi*. Why is the *m* a syllable in one and not in the other?
3. Divide *punda*, and explain why the division *pun-da* is wrong for Swahili.
4. What is the difference in sound between *ngoa* and *ng'oa*? Write both in IPA, using [ᵑɡ] and [ŋ].
5. *Paka* "cat" comes from a word with *mp* at the start. What happened to the nasal, and what trace can it leave?
6. Why does *mbuzi* still have its *m*, when *paka* lost its nasal?
7. *Nta* begins with *n* before a voiceless *t*. Why did that nasal survive?
8. A learner says *ndege* as "en-DAY-gay". Correct the three mistakes.

## Worked answers

1. *nyu-mba*, *ngo-ma*, *mvu-a*. In each, the nasal and its consonant start a syllable together, and two written vowels (*u-a*) are two syllables.
2. *m-bwa* and *mbu-zi*. After the *m* of *mbwa* only one syllable follows, so the nasal is a syllable of its own. After the *m* of *mbuzi* two syllables follow, so the *m* joins the *b*.
3. *pu-nda*. A Swahili syllable ends in a vowel, and a nasal with its consonant goes forward to the next syllable. *Pun-da* would give the first syllable a consonant at the end.
4. *Ngoa* "jealousy" has [ᵑɡ], a nasal and a full *g*; *ng'oa* "uproot" has [ŋ] alone, with no *g*. Roughly [ˈᵑɡɔ.ɑ] and [ˈŋɔ.ɑ].
5. Swahili dropped nasals before voiceless consonants, so *\*mpákà* became *paka*. Some speakers keep a trace as a puff of breath after the *p*: [ˈpʰɑ.kɑ].
6. The *b* is voiced. The loss affected nasals only before voiceless consonants; before *b*, *d*, *j*, *g*, *v* and *z* they stayed.
7. The word is only one syllable apart from the nasal. In such short words the nasal survived as a syllable of its own, which is why *nta*, like *nchi*, begins with a syllabic *n*.
8. The *n* is not a syllable: the word has two syllables, *nde-ge*. The *e*'s are a pure [ɛ], with no glide into "ay". And the stress is on the first syllable, *nde*, as lesson 5 will show: [ˈⁿdɛ.ɡɛ].
