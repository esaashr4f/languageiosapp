## The idea

Swahili borrowed a great many words from Arabic, and with some of them it borrowed sounds that Bantu languages did not have. Standard spelling writes four of them with digraphs:

- *dh* [ð], the *th* of English "this";
- *th* [θ], the *th* of English "thin";
- *gh* [ɣ], a *g* made with friction instead of a full closure, like the Spanish *g* between vowels in *lago*;
- *kh* [x], the *ch* of Scottish "loch" or the Spanish *j* of *jota*.

Polomé's handbook says these sounds occur only in Arabic loanwords. They belong to the speech of educated speakers on the coast and the islands, where Arabic culture has long had prestige. Further inland, speakers who learnt Swahili as a second language tend to replace them with sounds they already had: *z* for *dh*, *s* or *t* for *th*, *g* for *gh*, and *h* for *kh*.

So these four digraphs differ in status from the rest of the alphabet. The standard writes them and careful speech uses them, but you will often hear the substitutes, and you will be understood if you use either.

*Kh* is the weakest of the four. Polomé reports that [x] alternates freely with [h], except in a few words where [x] is usual, such as *sabalkheri* "good morning"; only those are spelt with *kh*. Most words with Arabic [x] are spelt with plain *h*: *habari* "news" comes from Arabic *khabar*.

Other Arabic sounds were not kept at all in ordinary Swahili. The Arabic [q] became *k*: *kalamu* "pen" is *qalam*. The "emphatic" consonants became plain ones: *fedha* "silver" is *fiḍḍa*. The throat consonant *ʿayn* disappeared, often leaving two vowels side by side: *saa* "hour" is *sāʿa*, *maana* "meaning" is *maʿnā*. Polomé describes a formal, Arabised style of recitation and public speaking, especially in the mosque, in which some of these sounds return. It is a style, not the standard.

## How it is built

1. See *dh*, *th*, *gh* or *kh*? Treat it as one consonant, like *ch* or *sh*. It goes with the vowel after it: *dha-ha-bu*, *lu-gha*.
2. For *dh*, put the tongue between the teeth and voice it: [ð]. For *th*, the same without voice: [θ]. Do not confuse them: *th* in Swahili is never the *th* of "this".
3. For *gh*, start a *g* but do not close the back of the tongue completely, so the air rubs through: [ɣ]. It is one sound, not *g* followed by *h*.
4. For *kh*, do the same without voice: [x]. Saying [h] instead is widely accepted.
5. When you meet two vowels side by side in an Arabic loan (*saa*, *maana*), say both. They count as two syllables, as in lesson 1.

## Watch out

**Th is not always "this".** Swahili keeps the Arabic difference: *th* is voiceless [θ] (*thamani*), *dh* is voiced [ð] (*dhahabu*). English uses one spelling for both; Swahili uses two.

**Dh is one sound.** One teaching grammar warns that English speakers read *fedha* as "fed-ha". The *d* and *h* are not separate.

**Gh is one sound.** It is sometimes described in teaching books as a *g* running into an *h*. In phonetic terms it is a single sound, [ɣ], made at the same place as *g* but with friction. If it is hard, plain *g* is the common substitute.

**Do not over-Arabise.** Arabic speakers may be tempted to restore the *ʿayn* in *saa* or the *q* in *kalamu*. In ordinary Swahili that sounds foreign or very formal. Swahili adapted these words long ago.

**Substitutes are not mistakes in other people's speech.** When you hear *zahabu* or *lugha* with a plain *g*, you are hearing a common regional pronunciation, not a different word. Polomé describes them as features of second-language and inland speech.

**H in Arabic loans may stand for [x].** *Habari* and many other words with Arabic [x] are spelt with *h*. Saying [h] is always correct.

## Connections

If you know Arabic, the Swahili digraphs map onto letters you know: *dh* is ذ, *th* is ث, *gh* is غ, *kh* is خ. The changes Swahili made are the same ones many other languages made when borrowing from Arabic: *q* to *k*, emphatics to plain consonants, *ʿayn* dropped.

## Exercises

1. Divide *dhahabu* and *thelathini* into syllables, treating each digraph as one consonant.
2. Which of *dhahabu*, *thamani* and *fedha* have a voiced "th" sound, and which a voiceless one?
3. A speaker from the interior says "zahabu" and "selasini". Which standard words do they mean, and what has happened?
4. *Lugha* is given two pronunciations in Wiktionary. What are they, and which is the standard?
5. Why is *habari* spelt with *h* although it comes from Arabic *khabar*?
6. *Maana* comes from Arabic *maʿnā*. Explain the double *a*, and say how many syllables the word has.
7. *Kalamu* comes from Arabic *qalam*. Name the two changes.
8. A learner reads *fedha* as "fed-ha". Correct them, and write the word in IPA.

## Worked answers

1. *dha-ha-bu*, three syllables. *the-la-thi-ni*, four. Each digraph is a single consonant and starts its syllable.
2. *Dhahabu* and *fedha* have voiced [ð], spelt *dh*. *Thamani* has voiceless [θ], spelt *th*.
3. *Dhahabu* "gold" and *thelathini* "thirty". The speaker has replaced *dh* with *z* and *th* with *s*, the common substitutions Polomé describes for speakers less influenced by Arabic pronunciation.
4. /ˈlu.ɣɑ/, with the Arabic-derived [ɣ], and /ˈlu.ɠɑ/, with an ordinary *g* (here the coastal implosive). The spelling *gh* reflects the first, which is the careful standard; the second is a common substitute.
5. Because Swahili [x] is interchangeable with [h], and the standard spells it *h* except in a few words where [x] is usual. Most speakers say *habari* with [h].
6. Arabic *ʿayn* is not kept in ordinary Swahili. When it dropped out, the vowels on either side of it were left next to each other. Each written vowel is a syllable, so *maana* has three: *ma-a-na*.
7. Arabic [q] became *k*, and a final *u* was added so that the word ends in a vowel, as Swahili syllables do.
8. *Dh* is one sound, the voiced *th* of "this", and it starts the second syllable: *fe-dha*, /ˈfɛ.ðɑ/.
