## The idea

**Stress** is the extra weight given to one syllable of a word: it is said a little louder and longer than the others. In English you have to learn it word by word, and Spanish marks it with an accent wherever it is not where you would expect.

Swahili needs neither. Stress falls on the **penult**, the second-to-last syllable, in almost every word. Polomé's handbook states the rule, the teaching grammars state it, and the World Atlas of Language Structures (WALS) classifies Swahili among the languages with fixed penultimate stress. Swahili spelling never marks stress, because there is almost never anything to mark.

There is one condition. You must count syllables the Swahili way, as in lessons 1 to 3: every written vowel is a syllable, even beside another vowel, and a syllabic *m* or *n* is a syllable too. With that count, the rule is mechanical.

The stressed syllable is also a little longer. Polomé transcribes the stressed vowel of a word like *baba* as lengthened, and the vowels around it as short. That is the "rhythm" you will hear: a long beat near the end of every word.

Because the rule counts from the end of the word, the stress moves when the word grows. Swahili builds words by adding pieces: prefixes in front, and a few suffixes behind. Add a syllable at the end, and the stress moves one syllable to the right. Add prefixes at the front, and the stress stays where it was, counted from the end. *Shule* "school" is *SHU-le*. *Shuleni* "at school", with the place suffix *-ni* of part 3, is *shu-LE-ni*.

Why penultimate? The sources used for this guide do not say how the rule arose. Polomé notes that Swahili lost the tones of its Bantu relatives, and that stress now gives the word its unity, but he offers no history of the stress itself. Take it as a fact.

## How it is built

1. Divide the word into syllables. Each vowel letter is one. A syllabic *m* or *n* is one. Digraphs and nasal clusters belong to the syllable after them.
2. Count from the end: last, second-to-last.
3. Put the stress on the second-to-last.
4. If the word has one syllable apart from a syllabic nasal (*mtu*, *mbwa*), the nasal is the penult and takes the stress.
5. If a suffix or a longer ending is added, divide and count again. Do not keep the old stress.

In this lesson the stressed syllable is written in capitals. The guide does not mark stress anywhere else, except with an acute accent on the few exceptions.

## Watch out

**Count every vowel.** English speakers hear *kiatu* as two syllables and put the stress on *ki*. It has three, *ki-a-tu*, and the stress is on *a*. The same goes for *njia*, *sauti* and every word with two vowels side by side.

**Count the syllabic nasal.** *Mtu* is not stressed on *tu*. The *m* is a syllable and it is the penult.

**Do not carry the stress over.** *Nyumba* is stressed on *nyu*, *nyumbani* on *mba*. Each form of a word is counted afresh. This happens constantly, because Swahili adds suffixes to nouns and verbs.

**A handful of loans break the rule.** Some Arabic loans may keep the stress on the third syllable from the end: *núsura*, *lázima*. Polomé calls this an Arabic stress pattern used as a stylistic feature, and notes that the same words are often stressed regularly; Wiktionary gives both stresses for *lazima*. One teaching grammar says stress "always" falls on the penult. Learn the rule as absolute, and treat these words as exceptions you will hear.

**The pair *barabára* and *barábara*.** Polomé cites it to show that stress can, in rare cases, separate two words. It is the clearest example, not the tip of a large class.

**Small words lean on the next word.** Polomé notes that the linking word *ni* "is" (part 2) and the short "of" words (part 3) have no stress of their own. They are said as if they were part of the following word. Spelling still writes them separately.

## Connections

Spanish has a default stress too: a word ending in a vowel is stressed on the penult unless an accent says otherwise (*casa*, but *café*). Swahili is Spanish without the exceptions, which is why it needs no accent marks.

## Exercises

1. Divide *mwalimu*, *safari* and *kiatu* into syllables and mark the stress with capitals.
2. Where is the stress in *mtu*, and why?
3. *Shule* is stressed *SHU-le*. Where is the stress in *shuleni* "at school", and why did it move?
4. Mark the stress in *chakula* and *ng'ombe* (lessons 2 and 3).
5. Mark the stress in *ninasoma*, and say why adding *ni-* and *-na-* at the front does not move it.
6. A friend says Swahili stress is always penultimate, without exception. Give the strongest counter-example, and say how common such words are.
7. Mark the stress in *sauti* and *njia*. What do the two words have in common?
8. *Lazima* has two pronunciations in Wiktionary. Mark both and say which follows the rule.

## Worked answers

1. *mwa-LI-mu*: the *mw* is one onset, so three syllables. *sa-FA-ri*. *ki-A-tu*: three syllables, because *i* and *a* are two vowels.
2. On the *m*: *M-tu*. The syllabic *m* counts as a syllable, so it is the second-to-last.
3. *shu-LE-ni*. The suffix *-ni* added a syllable at the end, so the second-to-last syllable is now *le*. Stress is counted on each form, not fixed to the root.
4. *cha-KU-la*: *ch* is one consonant. *NG'O-mbe*: *ng'* starts the first syllable, *mb* starts the second.
5. *ni-na-SO-ma*. The rule counts from the end of the word. Prefixes add syllables at the front, which does not change which syllable is second from the end.
6. *Barabara*: *barabára* "road" against *barábara* "exactly", where only the stress separates two words. Such words are rare: a few Arabic loans such as *núsura* and *lázima* (which is also said regularly).
7. *sa-U-ti* and *NJI-a*. In both, two vowels stand side by side and each counts as a syllable. In *sauti* the pair is followed by another syllable, so the second vowel of the pair is the penult. In *njia* the pair ends the word, so the first vowel of the pair is the penult.
8. /ˈlɑ.zi.mɑ/, *LA-zi-ma*, the Arabic-style stress on the third syllable from the end; and /lɑˈzi.mɑ/, *la-ZI-ma*, which follows the penult rule.
