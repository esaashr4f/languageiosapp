## The idea

The first sentence anyone needs says who someone is: "I am a teacher", "they are guests". Swahili builds it from three pieces. A word for the person, the linking word *ni*, and a noun that says what the person is.

A **pronoun** is a short word that stands for a person: "I", "you", "she". Swahili has six of them for people, one for each combination of person (speaker, listener, someone else) and number (one, more than one). They are full words that can stand alone: *mimi* "I", *wewe* "you", *yeye* "he, she", *sisi* "we", *ninyi* "you (more than one)", *wao* "they". There is no separate word for "he" and "she". *Yeye* covers both, and Swahili grammar never marks a person's sex.

A **copula** is a word whose only job is to link a thing to what it is, like English "is". Swahili's copula is *ni*, and it never changes. English needs *am*, *are* and *is*; Spanish needs *soy*, *eres*, *es*, *somos*. Swahili says *ni* for everyone: *mimi ni*, *wewe ni*, *wao ni*. The negative is *si*, also unchanging: *mimi si*, *wao si*.

Why does *ni* not change for the person? Because it did not start as a verb. Wiktionary, after the linguist John McWhorter, traces it to a particle that put a word in focus. It then came to mean "it is (this)", pointing something out, and only later became an ordinary copula. A pointing word has no reason to agree with anyone, and *ni* still does not. That history also explains a use you will meet in a moment: *ni* with no subject at all means "it is".

The nouns that say who someone is come mostly from the first two noun classes, the classes of people. Class 1 is the singular and its prefix is *m-* (*mw-* before a vowel, as lesson 1.6 showed). Class 2 is the plural and its prefix is *wa-*. So the plural of a person noun is made by swapping the prefix: *mtu* "person", *watu* "people"; *mgeni* "guest", *wageni* "guests". There is no plural ending as in English or Spanish. Number lives in the prefix.

## How it is built

Say "we are farmers".

1. Find the subject, the person the sentence is about: "we". The pronoun is *sisi*.
2. Add the copula: *ni*. It does not change for "we".
3. Find the noun for what the subject is: "farmer" is *mkulima*.
4. The subject is plural, so the noun must be plural too. Swap class 1 *m-* for class 2 *wa-*: *wakulima*.
5. Put them in order: subject, *ni*, noun. *Sisi ni wakulima.*

To say "we are not farmers", replace *ni* with *si*. Nothing else moves: *Sisi si wakulima.*

There is no word for "a" or "the". Thompson and Schleicher state it plainly: Swahili has no articles, and the English translation adds whichever one fits. *Mimi ni mwalimu* is "I am a teacher" or, in the right context, "I am the teacher".

A noun can be the subject instead of a pronoun: *Mtoto ni mgeni* "the child is a guest". Names work the same way: *Amina ni mpishi* "Amina is a cook".

## Where it applies

- **Saying who or what someone is.** Name, job, nationality, role: *Mimi ni mwalimu*, *Ninyi ni Watanzania*, *Amina si mpishi*.
- **Any person, any number.** The same *ni* and *si* serve all six pronouns and any noun subject. Thompson and Schleicher list *ni* with every pronoun and *si* likewise.
- **A state expressed as a kind of person.** *Mgonjwa* is "sick person", so *Mzee ni mgonjwa* is how you say someone is ill. Almasi and colleagues give a sentence of this kind with *ni mgonjwa*.
- **Without a subject: "it is".** *Ni kweli* "it's true". Polomé gives *ni mlima* "it is a mountain", and *ni* with no subject for "they are" too (*ni wapagazi* "they are porters"), when the context says who. This is the pointing use from which the copula grew.
- **Two subjects joined by *na*.** *Mimi na wewe ni wanafunzi*. The noun after *ni* is plural.
- **Things as well as people.** *Ni* links any noun to what it is, as the class 9 example shows. Part 3 adds nouns of the other classes, and lesson 3.3 adds adjectives after *ni* ("the house is big").
- **Proverbs and headlines.** Many proverbs are built on *ni*: *Mtu ni watu*. Polomé notes that in speech *ni* is often left out altogether, especially after a pronoun. Use it; recognise the bare form when you hear it.

## Watch out

**Do not drop the pronoun as in Spanish.** *Soy maestro* needs no "yo", because *soy* is first person. *Ni* carries no person, so *\*Ni mwalimu* does not mean "I am a teacher". In today's usage it means "he or she is a teacher" or "it's a teacher", with the subject taken from context. (Polomé records an older written usage in which the "I" prefix of lesson 2.2 did this job, *ni mpishi* "I am a cook"; Thompson and Schleicher teach only the pronoun with *ni*.) Say *Mimi ni mwalimu*.

**Do not put *si* next to *ni*.** *\*Mimi si ni mwalimu* and *\*Mimi ni si mwalimu* both double the copula. *Si* is the whole negative copula: *Mimi si mwalimu*.

**Do not use *hapana* or *la* for "not" here.** They mean "no" as an answer (lesson 2.4). *\*Yeye hapana mwalimu* is wrong; say *Yeye si mwalimu*.

**Make the noun plural with a plural subject.** *\*Wao ni mwalimu* mixes a plural subject with a singular noun. Say *Wao ni walimu*. English agrees here too ("they are teachers"), but learners who think of *ni* as a mere symbol forget it.

**Do not look for an article.** French *un* and Spanish *un* tempt you to add a word before *mwalimu*. Swahili has none. *Mimi ni mwalimu* already means "I am a teacher".

**Do not confuse the copula with the "I" prefix.** The next lesson introduces a prefix *ni-* meaning "I" that sits at the front of a verb. It looks the same and is unrelated. Almasi and colleagues warn about exactly this pair. The copula is a separate word; the prefix is glued to a verb.

**Some plurals are irregular.** *Mwanamke* "woman" is plural *wanawake*, and *mwanamume* "man" is *wanaume*. *\*Wanamke* is wrong. Both nouns are compounds of *mwana* "child" with a word for wife or husband, and the second part changes too. Learn these two plurals as words.

**Do not use *ni* to say where someone is.** "She is at home" is not a matter of identity. Swahili uses a different construction for place, taught in lesson 2.5, much as Spanish uses *estar* and not *ser*.

**Older texts sometimes use a prefix instead of *ni*.** Polomé reports that the written standard of his day used the subject prefix for "I" and "you" in such sentences (*u nani?* "who are you?"), and records *yu mpishi* "he is a cook" in some spoken Swahili. You will meet them in the readings of part 8. Do not imitate them.

## Connections

Arabic also links "I" and "teacher" without a verb in the present: *ʾanā muʿallim*. The difference is that Swahili still has a linking word, *ni*, while Arabic has none at all. Japanese *です* and Korean *이다* are the nearest parallels: like *ni*, they do not change for the person of the subject. *Mwalimu* itself is an Arabic loan, from *muʿallim*.

## Exercises

1. Say "they are farmers" and "they are not farmers".
2. Take apart *Sisi ni wageni* and translate it.
3. Make these plural, keeping the sentence true: *Yeye ni mwanafunzi* ("they are students").
4. Say "Juma is a Kenyan, but Amina is a Tanzanian".
5. A student writes *Ni mvulana* for "I am a boy". What does his sentence mean, and how should it read?
6. Correct *\*Wao ni mwanamke*, meant as "they are women".
7. Say "you (several) are not guests, you are also students".
8. Why does *ni* have the same form after *mimi*, *wewe* and *wao*?

## Worked answers

1. The subject is "they": *wao*. "Farmer" is *mkulima*; the subject is plural, so the noun takes class 2 *wa-*: *wakulima*. *Wao ni wakulima.* For the negative, replace *ni* with *si*: *Wao si wakulima.*
2. *Sisi*: "we". *Ni*: the copula. *Wa-geni*: class 2 prefix plus the stem of "guest". "We are guests."
3. *Yeye* becomes *wao*. *Ni* stays. *Mwanafunzi* becomes *wanafunzi*, with class 2 *wa-*. *Wao ni wanafunzi.*
4. *Juma ni Mkenya, lakini Amina ni Mtanzania.* Each part is subject, *ni*, noun; *lakini* "but" joins them.
5. *Ni* carries no person, so *Ni mvulana* means "he is a boy" or "it's a boy". For "I am a boy", state the subject: *Mimi ni mvulana.*
6. The subject is plural, so the noun must be plural, and the plural of *mwanamke* is irregular: *Wao ni wanawake.*
7. "You (several)" is *ninyi*. *Ninyi si wageni, ninyi ni wanafunzi pia.*
8. *Ni* is not a verb with person endings. Wiktionary traces it to a pointing particle meaning "it is (this)", which became a copula without ever agreeing with a subject.
