## The idea

English says "not" with a separate word. Swahili says it inside the verb, and in the present it says it twice: once at the front and once at the end.

Compare *ninasoma* "I am reading" with *sisomi* "I am not reading". Three things have changed:

1. the subject prefix *ni-* has become the negative prefix *si-*;
2. the tense marker *-na-* has gone;
3. the final vowel *-a* has become *-i*.

The verb is still built on the same slots. Only their fillers change.

**The front.** Negative subject prefixes are made by putting *ha-* in front of the ordinary prefix: *tu-* becomes *hatu-*, *wa-* becomes *hawa-*, *m-* becomes *ham-*. Polomé describes this *ha-* as a separate slot in front of the subject prefix. Three persons fuse with it. "I" is not *\*hani-* but *si-*; Polomé describes *si-* as standing in for *ha-* plus *ni-*. "You" (one person) is *hu-*, which Wiktionary derives from the same source as *ha-*. And class 1 "he, she" is *ha-* alone, with no trace of *a-*.

**The middle.** There is no tense marker at all. Polomé's description of the negative present has an empty tense slot. That is why the negative covers both senses of *-na-*: Thompson and Schleicher give *hasomi* as both "she isn't studying" and "she doesn't study".

**The end.** The final vowel slot, which you met in the last lesson, earns its place here: *-a* becomes *-i*. The sources describe this ending but do not explain where it came from. Verbs borrowed from Arabic, which have no final vowel slot, keep their own vowel: *sijibu* "I don't answer", *sirudi* "I am not going back".

So Swahili marks "not" twice: at the front with *ha-* or a fused prefix, and at the end with *-i*. For a loan verb, the front does all the work.

## How it is built

Say "we don't understand".

1. Find the verb: "understand" is *-elewa*. Its root is *-elew-*.
2. Find the subject: "we", prefix *tu-*.
3. Make the prefix negative: *ha-* in front gives *hatu-*.
4. Leave out *-na-*.
5. Add the root, and change the final vowel to *-i*: *-elewi*.
6. Join: *ha-tu-elew-i*, *hatuelewi*.

Now say "the guests don't eat meat".

1. "Eat" is *-la*, root *-l-*. In the positive it needs *ku-* (*wanakula*). In the negative it does not: lesson 2.2 promised that short verbs appear in the negative, and this is why. Almasi and colleagues state that these verbs drop *ku-* in the negative.
2. Subject *wageni*, class 2: *wa-*, negative *hawa-*.
3. No *-na-*. Root *-l-*, final *-i*: *hawali*.
4. *Wageni hawali nyama.*

One spelling point: before a root beginning with a vowel, *ham-* is written *hamw-*: *hamwoni* "you (all) don't see", *hamwandiki* "you (all) aren't writing". Wiktionary's tables have it for every vowel-initial verb.

## Where it applies

- **"Is not doing" and "does not do".** One form for both: *Sisomi* "I'm not reading" or "I don't read".
- **Every person and every class 1 or 2 noun.** *Mtoto hachezi*, *Watoto hawachezi*. Part 3 gives each other class its negative prefix in the same way: *ha-* plus the class prefix.
- **Loan verbs.** The front changes, the end does not: *hakubali*, *hatusafiri*, *hamfikiri*.
- **Short verbs.** No *ku-*: *sili* "I don't eat", *hunywi* "you don't drink". Almasi and colleagues' examples include *haji* "he isn't coming", from *-ja* "come".
- **Before an infinitive.** Negate the first verb only: *Sipendi kusoma*, *Hataki kulala* "she doesn't want to sleep".
- **With *kabisa* and *tena*.** *Kabisa* becomes "at all", *tena* "any more". Wiktionary records both senses in negative sentences.
- **Fixed answers.** *Sijui* "I don't know" and *Sielewi* "I don't understand" are everyday replies on their own.

## Watch out

**Do not keep *-na-*.** *\*Sinasoma* and *\*Hatunaelewi* combine a negative prefix with the positive tense. The negative present has no tense marker: *sisomi*, *hatuelewi*.

**Do not forget the *-i*.** *\*Sisoma* has a negative front and a positive end. Say *sisomi*.

**Do not give a loan verb *-i*.** *\*Sijibi* changes a vowel that loan verbs keep: *-jibu* keeps its *u*, so *sijibu*. *Harudi* "he isn't coming back" ends in *i* only because *-rudi* does.

**Do not build "I" from *ha-*.** *\*Hanisomi* is not a form. "I" in the negative is always *si-*: *sisomi*.

**Do not put *si* in front of a verb.** The negative copula of lesson 2.1 negates *ni*, not a verb. *\*Mimi si ninasoma* is wrong. *Sisomi*, or *Mimi sisomi*.

**Do not translate English "do not" with a separate word.** *\*Mimi hapana ninasoma* uses the answer word "no" (lesson 2.4) as if it were "not". Swahili negates the verb itself.

**Do not keep *ku-* on short verbs.** *\*Sikuli* and *\*hawakunywi* keep a syllable the negative drops. Say *sili*, *hawanywi*.

**Do not mix up *hu-* and *ha-*.** *Hupiki* is "you don't cook"; *hapiki* is "she doesn't cook". One letter decides the person.

**Two homographs to keep apart.** *Hali* is "he or she isn't eating" (*ha-l-i*) and also a noun meaning "condition" (lesson 2.8). Later, lesson 4.12 introduces a habitual *hu-* that looks like the "you" prefix: *husoma* "one usually reads" against *husomi* "you don't read". The final vowel tells them apart.

**Do not negate the infinitive by mistake.** In *Sipendi kusoma*, "not" belongs to "like". *\*Ninapenda kusomi* is not Swahili; the infinitive has no *-i* form.

## Connections

French also marks "not" twice: *je ne lis pas*. Swahili's two marks are both glued to the verb. Japanese also builds "not" into the verb, at the end: 読む, 読まない. Korean can put it in front: 안 읽어요. A negative verb as one word will feel familiar.

## Exercises

1. Make negative: *Ninapika*, *Unaimba*, *Tunacheka*.
2. Make negative: *Watoto wanakimbia* and *Mwalimu anarudi*.
3. Take apart *Hamwelewi* and translate it.
4. Say "I don't drink water" and "she doesn't eat meat".
5. Correct *\*Sinajibu* and *\*Hasomi* (meant as "you don't read").
6. Say "they don't want to sleep".
7. Translate *Mgonjwa hali, hanywi, hacheki.*
8. Why is the negative of *anajua* "she knows" *hajui*, but the negative of *anafahamu* "she understands" *hafahamu*?

## Worked answers

1. Swap the prefix for its negative, drop *-na-*, change *-a* to *-i*: *sipiki*, *huimbi*, *hatucheki*.
2. Class 2 *wa-* becomes *hawa-*: *Watoto hawakimbii.* The root *-kimbi-* ends in *i*, and the negative *-i* follows it. Class 1 becomes *ha-*; *-rudi* is a loan verb and keeps its vowel: *Mwalimu harudi.*
3. *Ha-*: not. *-mw-*: "you (several)", written *mw* before a vowel. *-elew-*: understand. *-i*: negative final vowel. "You (all) don't understand."
4. *Sinywi maji.* *Hali nyama.* Both are short verbs, so neither keeps *ku-*.
5. *\*Sinajibu* keeps *-na-*: say *sijibu*. *Hasomi* is "she doesn't read"; "you don't read" needs *hu-*: *husomi*.
6. Negate the first verb only, and keep the infinitive: *Hawataki kulala.*
7. *Mgonjwa*: the sick person. *Ha-l-i*: doesn't eat. *Ha-nyw-i*: doesn't drink. *Ha-chek-i*: doesn't laugh. "The patient isn't eating, isn't drinking, isn't laughing."
8. *-jua* is a native verb with a final vowel slot, so *-a* becomes *-i*. *-fahamu* is a loan from Arabic with no final vowel slot; it keeps its *u*, and only the prefix shows "not".
