## The idea

A **yes-no question** is a question whose answer is "yes" or "no": "Are you reading?", "Is she a teacher?". English builds one by moving words about ("you are" becomes "are you") or by adding "do" ("you read" becomes "do you read?"). Swahili does neither.

In Swahili a yes-no question is the statement itself, said with a different melody. *Unasoma* is "you are reading". *Unasoma?* is "are you reading?". The words, their order and their forms stay exactly the same. Thompson and Schleicher say it in so many words: the word order does not change. Polomé makes the stronger point that the difference between a plain statement and a yes-no question lies only in the pitch at the end of the sentence.

This follows from what you already know. The verb is a complete sentence with its subject inside it. There is no separate subject word to move round the verb and no helper verb like "do" to put in front. So Swahili uses the one thing left to change: the voice.

Written down, the melody disappears. Writing therefore has a second tool: the word *je*, placed at the very start of the sentence to announce "a question is coming". Wiktionary's usage note says *je* is more common in writing, where it stands in for the tone of voice that does the work in speech. *Je, unasoma?* and *Unasoma?* ask the same thing.

The answers are short words. *Ndiyo* is "yes". Wiktionary analyses it as *ndi-* plus *-yo*, literally "it is (so)": the same *ndi-* as in focus words like *ndiye* "it is he", taught in part 6. *Hapana* is the everyday "no". *La* is also "no"; it is a loan from Arabic *lā*. *Siyo* is "no, it isn't (so)", the negative partner of *ndiyo*, built on the negative *si* of lesson 2.1.

## How it is built

Ask "do the parents know?"

1. Build the statement first. "Parents" is *wazazi*, class 2, so the subject prefix is *wa-*. Present *-na-*, root *-ju-*, final *-a*: *Wazazi wanajua* "the parents know".
2. Leave every word where it is.
3. In speech, change the melody at the end (described below). In writing, add a question mark, and *je* at the front if you want to make the question unmistakable: *Je, wazazi wanajua?*

Answer it.

1. Choose the answer word: *ndiyo* or *hapana*.
2. Add the verb, built from the questioner's verb with the person changed if needed. The question asked about "they", so the answer stays in "they": *Ndiyo, wanajua.* For "no", use the negative present of lesson 2.3: *Hapana, hawajui.*
3. If the question was about "you", the answer is about "I" or "we": *Unajua?* "do you know?" – *Ndiyo, ninajua* or *Hapana, sijui*.

**The melody.** Polomé describes the normal statement as ending with a high pitch on the stressed second-to-last syllable and a low pitch on the last one: *wa-na-JU-a*, high on *JU*, low on *a*. A yes-no question reverses the final step: the stressed syllable is on a middle pitch and the last syllable is high, falling away at the very end. Almasi and colleagues, following an earlier teaching grammar, describe the same thing as the voice rising over the last two or three syllables and dropping on the final one. Thompson and Schleicher call it a rising intonation like that of English questions. The safe summary: keep the end of the sentence up instead of letting it fall.

**The tag *sivyo*.** To ask for agreement with something you already believe ("..., isn't it?"), add *sivyo* at the end, or *au sivyo* "or not so". Thompson and Schleicher name *sivyo* as the most common tag and say it expects "yes". Polomé glosses *sivyo* "it is not so" and its partner *ndivyo* "it is so". *Au* is "or", from Arabic.

## Where it applies

- **Any statement with a verb.** *Unasoma?*, *Wanafunzi wanaelewa?*, *Mtoto anakimbia?* Present, negative present and every tense to come work the same way.
- **Sentences with *ni* and *si*.** *Wewe ni mwalimu?*, *Yeye si Mkenya?* The copula sentence of lesson 2.1 is turned into a question by melody alone.
- **One-word questions.** Because a verb is a whole sentence, one word can be a whole question: *Unaelewa?* "do you understand?", *Unajua?* "do you know?".
- **Offers and checks.** *Unataka kulala?* "do you want to sleep?" is an offer. *Mnasikiliza?* "are you listening?" is a check.
- ***Je* at the start.** Mostly in writing, and in careful or formal speech. Thompson and Schleicher list it as the usual mark of a yes-no question; Almasi and colleagues use it to alert the listener. It is also the normal opening of the greeting *Je, hujambo?* (lesson 2.8).
- ***Je* after a noun or pronoun: "what about...?".** *Na wewe je?* "and you?", *Na watoto je?* "and the children?".
- **Tag questions.** *Mgeni anafika leo, sivyo?* or *..., au sivyo?* when you expect agreement. Answer with *ndiyo* (or *ndivyo* "that's so") or *hapana* (or *sivyo* "it's not so").
- **Either-or questions with *au*.** *Unasoma au unalala?* The answer names the choice: *Ninasoma.*
- **Short answers.** *Ndiyo*, *hapana*, *la*, *siyo*, alone or followed by the verb. Repeating the verb is common and polite, and in "no" answers it is the clearest way to be understood.
- **"Not yet" answers.** *Bado* "still" also answers a question alone, meaning "not yet": *Unaelewa? Bado.* Wiktionary gives both senses, "still" and "not yet".
- **Questions with question words** ("who?", "where?") are another matter. They do not expect yes or no, and are taught later (*wapi* "where" comes in lesson 2.5; the full set in part 6).

## Watch out

**Do not invert as in English or French.** *\*Anasoma mwalimu?* is not how to ask "is the teacher reading?". Swahili can move a subject to the end for other reasons, but that does not make a question. Keep the statement order: *Mwalimu anasoma?*

**Do not add a "do".** English "do you read?" tempts you to use *-fanya* "do, make". *\*Unafanya kusoma?* means something like "are you doing reading?" and is not a question form. Say *Unasoma?*

**Do not use *je* at the end as a question mark.** After a verb, *-je* is a different word meaning "how": Wiktionary gives *umelala je?* "how did you sleep?". So *\*Unasoma je?* asks "how are you reading?", not "are you reading?". Put *je* first: *Je, unasoma?* After a noun or pronoun, *je* means "what about": *Na wewe je?*

**Do not change *sivyo* to match the subject.** English tags change ("isn't she?", "don't they?", "aren't I?"). *Sivyo* is fixed. *\*Wanafika leo, hawavyo?* is invented. Say *Wanafika leo, sivyo?*

**Do not answer with the copula *si*.** *Si* means "is not" and needs a noun after it (*Mimi si mwalimu*). As a one-word answer, say *hapana*, *la* or *siyo*.

**Do not keep "you" in the answer.** *Unasoma?* – *\*Ndiyo, unasoma* means "yes, you are reading". Switch to the speaker: *Ndiyo, ninasoma.* Questions about "you all" are answered with "we": *Mnaelewa?* – *Ndiyo, tunaelewa.*

**Negative questions: say the verb.** "Don't you understand?" is *Huelewi?*. In English, "no" agrees with the negative ("no, I don't"). *Ndiyo* means "it is so", which suggests that a Swahili *ndiyo* confirms the question as it was put ("yes, that's right, I don't"), as Japanese はい does. The sources used for this guide do not discuss this point directly, so do not rely on a bare *ndiyo* or *hapana*. Add the verb, and the answer is clear either way: *Hapana, ninaelewa* "no, I do understand", or *Ndiyo, sielewi* "right, I don't understand".

**Do not use *hapana* inside a sentence for "not".** Lessons 2.1 and 2.3 already warned: *\*Hapana ninaelewa* as "I don't understand" is wrong. *Hapana* is the answer word, followed by a comma or a pause; "not" lives inside the verb: *Sielewi.*

**Do not read *bado* as "not yet" everywhere.** With a positive verb it means "still": *Bado anafundisha* "she is still teaching". Alone, as an answer, it means "not yet". The full "not yet" verb form is taught in lesson 4.5.

**A melody, not a word, makes the spoken question.** Without *je*, a question said with falling statement melody is heard as a statement. Thompson and Schleicher compare the question melody to English rising intonation; Polomé describes a high, falling last syllable after a mid-pitched stressed one. The sources describe it differently because, as Polomé notes, intonation varies a good deal from speaker to speaker. Listen and copy.

## Connections

Japanese and Korean also ask yes-no questions without moving anything: 読みますか, 읽어요? Japanese adds the particle か at the end where Swahili optionally adds *je* at the start. Spoken French *Tu lis ?* works exactly like *Unasoma?*: statement order, question melody. Arabic *hal* at the start of a question is the closest parallel to *je*.

## Exercises

1. Turn into questions, once with *je* and once without: *Mnasikiliza.* *Yeye ni mzazi.*
2. Answer *Unanunua kahawa?* with "yes" and a full verb, then with "no" and a full verb.
3. Ask "do the workers arrive today?" and answer "no, they arrive tomorrow".
4. Add a tag: "the hosts are waiting, aren't they?"
5. Take apart and translate *Je, mwanafunzi anauliza?*
6. A learner writes *\*Unasaidia je?* for "are you helping?". What does it mean, and how should it read?
7. Ask "are you (all) teaching or studying?" and give an answer.
8. Answer *Huelewi?* so that your meaning is clear whichever logic the listener uses, first saying that you do understand, then that you don't.

## Worked answers

1. The words stay as they are; add the question mark, and *je* if you like. *Je, mnasikiliza? / Mnasikiliza?* *Je, yeye ni mzazi? / Yeye ni mzazi?*
2. The question is about "you", so the answer is about "I". *Ndiyo, ninanunua kahawa.* For "no", use the negative present: *si-* for "I", no *-na-*, final *-i*: *Hapana, sinunui kahawa.*
3. *Wafanyakazi*, class 2: prefix *wa-*. *Wafanyakazi wanafika leo?* The answer stays with "they": *Hapana, wanafika kesho.*
4. *Wenyeji wanangoja, sivyo?* *Sivyo* never changes; *au sivyo* is also possible.
5. *Je*: a question is coming. *Mw-anafunzi*: class 1 prefix and "student". *A-na-uliz-a*: class 1 subject, present, "ask", final vowel. "Is the student asking?"
6. After a verb, *-je* means "how", so the sentence asks "how are you helping?". Put *je* at the front, or leave it out: *Je, unasaidia?* or *Unasaidia?*
7. *Mnafundisha au mnasoma?* The answer is about "we" and names one choice: *Tunafundisha.* (or *Tunasoma.*)
8. Add the verb after the answer word. "I understand": *Hapana, ninaelewa.* "I don't": *Ndiyo, sielewi.* The verb carries the real answer, so the choice of *ndiyo* or *hapana* cannot mislead.
