## The idea

Swahili has no verb meaning "have". It says "be with" instead. "I have a car" is, piece by piece, "I am with a car": *nina gari*, *ni-* "I" plus *na* "with", the same *na* you met as "and, with" in lesson 2.1.

Thompson and Schleicher give the infinitive as *kuwa na*, literally "to be with". Almasi and colleagues translate *kuwa na* as both "to have" and "to be with". In the present tense the verb *kuwa* "be" disappears, and you are left with the subject prefix and *na*. This is the same gap you met in the last lesson: Polomé explains *nipo* and *nina* together, as two results of the fact that the present of "be" does not appear after a subject prefix in modern Swahili. *Ni-po* is "I (am) at a spot"; *ni-na* is "I (am) with". In the past and future, *kuwa* returns: *nilikuwa na* "I had" (part 4).

The same pattern gives "there is". Take the subject prefix of a place class instead of a person, and you get "the place is with": *kuna* "there is (somewhere, in general)", *pana* "there is (at this spot)", *mna* "there is (inside)". The prefixes are *ku-*, *pa-* and *mu-* of classes 17, 16 and 18, the same three that gave *-ko*, *-po* and *-mo*. Wiktionary analyses *kuna*, *pana* and *mna* exactly so, as forms of *kuwa na* with a place-class subject. Swahili treats "there is a lion in the bush" as "the bush area has a lion": Polomé's example is *kuna simba msituni?* "is there a lion in the bush?", answered *Hakuna* "there isn't".

Look back at *-na-*, the present tense marker of lesson 2.2. Wiktionary, after Carl Meinhof, derives that tense marker from this very *na*. Today they are different things: *nina* is a complete word with no verb root, while *ninasoma* has *-na-* between a prefix and a root.

## How it is built

Say "we have a problem".

1. Subject "we": prefix *tu-*.
2. "Have" in the present: add *na*. *Tuna.*
3. Add what is had: *tatizo* "problem". *Tuna tatizo.*

Say "the teacher doesn't have a phone".

1. *Mwalimu*, class 1: prefix *a-*. (Only the place endings take *yu-*; here it is ordinary *a-*.) *Ana.*
2. Negative: as in lesson 2.3, *ha-* replaces class 1 *a-*: *hana*. There is no final *-i*: *na* is not a verb root.
3. *Mwalimu hana simu.*

The whole set follows lesson 2.3: *sina*, *huna*, *hana*, *hatuna*, *hamna*, *hawana*. Thompson and Schleicher give exactly these forms.

Say "there are no guests at home".

1. "There is", general place: *kuna*. Negative: *ha-* in front: *hakuna*.
2. What exists: *wageni*. Where: *nyumbani*.
3. *Hakuna wageni nyumbani*, or *Nyumbani hakuna wageni*. The place can come first or last; Polomé shows both orders with *pana*.

## Where it applies

- **Owning things.** *Nina gari*, *Mwalimu hana simu*, *Una pesa?*
- **Family and people.** *Nina dada* "I have a sister", *Mume na mke wana watoto*, *Ana mume* "she has a husband". Almasi and colleagues' reading passage gives *wana watoto sita* "they have six children".
- **Being with someone.** Almasi and colleagues translate *nilikuwa na watoto wangu* as "I was with my children". In the present, combine the place form with *na*: *Niko na mtoto*, *Yuko na wageni*.
- **Bodily and mental states.** English "be hungry, thirsty, in a hurry, feverish" are "have hunger, thirst, haste, fever": *nina njaa*, *una kiu?* (Polomé), *ana haraka*, *ana homa*. Also *nina furaha* "I'm glad", *nina swali* "I have a question".
- **Having time, room, a chance.** *Una nafasi?* "do you have time / a moment?"; *Sina wakati* "I don't have time".
- **Properties of things.** Polomé gives *kahawa hii ina moto* "this coffee is hot" (literally "has heat"), with the class 9 prefix *i-*. Every class can be the subject of *na* once part 3 has taught its prefix.
- **"There is" in general: *kuna*, *hakuna*.** *Kuna chakula?*, *Hakuna maji*. This is by far the commonest of the three.
- **"There is" at a spot: *pana*, *hapana*.** *Pana mgeni hapa.* Polomé's example: *pana picha juu ya kabati* "there is a picture above the cupboard".
- **"There is" inside: *mna*, *hamna*.** *Mna watu ndani?* Polomé's *mna tope kisimani* "there is mud in the well".
- **Short answers.** *Una pesa? – Hapana, sina.* *Kuna chakula? – Hakuna.* The negative form alone is a complete answer. A short "yes" answer needs a reference form (*ninayo* "I have it"), taught in lesson 3.14; until then, repeat the noun: *Ndiyo, nina pesa.*
- **Set phrases.** *Hakuna shida* "no problem", and the greeting *hujambo*, which Wiktionary explains as a contraction of *huna jambo* "you have no matter (wrong)" (lesson 2.8).

## Watch out

**Do not look for a verb "have".** *\*Ninashika gari* "I am holding a car" or any other verb is not "I have a car". Say *Nina gari.*

**Do not add the tense marker.** *\*Ninana gari* puts present *-na-* in front of *na* "with". *Nina* is already present.

**Do not add *-i* in the negative.** *\*Sini* and *\*Hatuni* treat *na* like a verb root. It keeps its *a*: *sina*, *hatuna*.

**Do not use the copula.** *\*Mimi ni na gari* links "I" and "with a car" with *ni*. The prefix does the linking: *Mimi nina gari.*

**Do not translate "I am hungry" with *ni*.** *\*Mimi ni njaa* says "I am hunger". Swahili has hunger: *Nina njaa.* The same goes for *kiu*, *haraka*, *homa*.

**Do not use a person prefix for "there is".** *\*Wana wageni nyumbani* means "they have guests at home", about some people you were speaking of. For "there are guests at home", the subject is the place: *Kuna wageni nyumbani.*

**Do not use *yuko* for "there is".** *\*Yuko mgeni* is not "there is a guest". *Yuko* says where a known person is (*mgeni yuko nyumbani* "the guest is at home"). "There is a guest" introduces someone new: *Kuna mgeni.*

**Do not make *kuna* agree with what exists.** *Kuna* takes its prefix from the place, never from the thing: *kuna mtoto*, *kuna watoto*, *kuna chakula*, all with *ku-*.

**Two words with two meanings.** *Hapana* is the answer "no" (lesson 2.4) and also "there isn't (at this spot)": Wiktionary lists both. *Hamna* is "you (all) don't have" and "there isn't (inside)". In speech, *hamna* is also used for "there's none": Almasi and colleagues list *hamna tabu* "no problem". Context tells them apart.

**Do not keep *ku-* of "be" in the present.** *\*Ninakuwa na pesa* is not the way to say "I have money now": the present of *kuwa na* is *nina*. The forms with *kuwa* belong to the other tenses (*nilikuwa na*, part 4).

**Do not overuse *hakuna matata*.** It is real Swahili, but Wiktionary notes that it is not usually used in conversation; it is mostly known from abroad. For everyday "no problem", Almasi and colleagues give *hamna tabu*; *hakuna shida* "there's no trouble" is built the same way.

## Connections

"Be with" for "have" is widespread. Arabic says *ʿindī kitāb*, "at me (is) a book". Korean and Japanese use an existence verb: 책이 있어요, 本がある, "a book exists (for me)". Swahili's *kuna* is the Spanish *hay* or French *il y a*, with the twist that the "there" is a real class prefix that changes for a spot (*pana*) or an inside (*mna*).

## Exercises

1. Say "you (one) have a question" and "you (one) don't have a question".
2. Say "the students are thirsty" and "the students are not thirsty".
3. Take apart *Hatuna wakati* and translate it.
4. Ask "is there water?" and answer "no, there isn't".
5. Say "there are people inside" and "there are no people inside".
6. Correct *\*Mimi ni homa*, meant as "I have a fever".
7. Correct *\*Wana watoto shuleni*, meant as "there are children at school".
8. Explain why *nina* and *niko* are built the same way.
9. Say "I'm with my sister" without a possessive: "I am with (my) sister".

## Worked answers

1. "You (one)": *u-*. Add *na*: *Una swali.* Negative: *hu-* replaces *u-*: *Huna swali.*
2. *Wanafunzi*, class 2: *wa-*. Thirst is had: *Wanafunzi wana kiu.* Negative *hawa-*: *Wanafunzi hawana kiu.*
3. *Ha-*: not. *-tu-*: we. *-na*: with. *Wakati*: time. "We don't have time."
4. General "there is": *Kuna maji?* Negative with *ha-*: *Hakuna.* (Or *Hapana, hakuna.*)
5. Inside: class 18 *m-*: *Mna watu ndani.* Negative: *Hamna watu ndani.* With the general class 17, *Kuna watu ndani* is also correct.
6. A fever is had, not been: *Nina homa.*
7. *Wana* is "they have", about some known people. "There are" takes a place subject: *Kuna watoto shuleni.*
8. Both are a subject prefix plus a short element, because the present of "be" does not appear after the prefix (Polomé). *Ni-ko* is "I (am) in an area"; *ni-na* is "I (am) with".
9. "Be with" in its plain sense is the place form plus *na*: *Niko na dada.* (*Nina dada* would mean "I have a sister".)
