## The idea

A **command** (grammarians say **imperative**) tells the listener to do something: "read!", "come here!". Every language makes it short, because the listener is right there and the time is now.

Swahili makes it short by emptying the slots you already know. A verb normally has a subject prefix and a tense marker in front of the root: *u-na-som-a* "you are reading". In a command, both slots are empty. What is left is the root and the final vowel: *soma!* "read!". Thompson and Schleicher put it this way: the singular command of most verbs is the dictionary form. You have been writing these forms all along as verb stems, *-soma*, *-andika*; a command is the stem said on its own.

Swahili distinguishes one listener from several, where English does not. For several people, the final *-a* becomes *-e* and *-ni* is added: *someni!* "read (all of you)!". Thompson and Schleicher describe the plural as the dictionary form minus *-a*, plus *-eni*. The sources do not explain the *e*. The *-ni*, on the other hand, is a mark of "you (several)" that you will meet again and again: Almasi and colleagues point out the same *-ni* in *karibuni* "welcome (all)", *asanteni* "thank you (all)" and *habarini* (lesson 2.8).

Verbs borrowed from Arabic have no final vowel slot to change, so they keep their vowel and add *-ni*: *jibu!*, *jibuni!* "answer!" (Thompson and Schleicher, Almasi and colleagues, Wiktionary all agree).

Two groups break the pattern.

**Short verbs keep *ku-*.** A one-syllable stem like *-la* "eat" cannot stand alone as a word: *la!* would be a single syllable, with no second-to-last syllable to carry the stress. So the command is the infinitive: *kula!* "eat!", *kunywa!* "drink!"; plural *kuleni!*, *kunyweni!* (Almasi and colleagues; Wiktionary). Lesson 4.4 explains why these verbs hold on to *ku-* elsewhere too.

**Three verbs are irregular.** Almasi and colleagues call them the only three exceptions:
- *-ja* "come": *njoo!*, plural *njooni!*
- *-enda* "go": *nenda!*, plural *nendeni!*
- *-leta* "bring": *lete!*, plural *leteni!* (the plural is regular)

The sources used here do not explain their history, so learn them as words.

## How it is built

Tell several children to sit down.

1. The verb: *-keti* "sit down". It ends in *i*, so it has no final *-a* to change.
2. Several listeners: add *-ni*. *Ketini!*
3. Address them if you like, after or before: *Ketini, watoto!*

Tell one person to close the door.

1. The verb: *-funga* "close". One listener: the stem as it is, *funga!*
2. The object follows the verb, as in any sentence: *Funga mlango!*

Tell several people to come here.

1. *-ja* is irregular: *njoo* for one, *njooni* for several.
2. Add the place: *Njooni hapa!*

Soften it with *tafadhali* "please", usually at the start: *Tafadhali, ngoja.* Almasi and colleagues stress that a bare command can sound blunt and that Swahili speakers do not use bare commands to their elders. Politer requests are made with the subjunctive (lesson 4.10).

## Where it applies

- **Telling one person to act.** *Soma!*, *Andika!*, *Kimbia!* Thompson and Schleicher's examples include *Kimbia!* "run!" and *Simama!* "stand up!".
- **Telling several people.** *Someni!*, *Andikeni!*, *Kimbieni!* In a classroom, a meeting or a family.
- **Loan verbs.** *Jibu! Jibuni!*, *Fikiri! Fikirini!*, *Samehe! Sameheni!*, *Jaribu! Jaribuni!*
- **Native verbs ending in a vowel other than *a*.** *Keti! Ketini!* follow the loan-verb pattern because there is no *-a* to replace (Wiktionary gives *ketini*).
- **Short verbs.** *Kula! Kuleni!*, *Kunywa! Kunyweni!*
- **The three irregulars.** *Njoo/njooni*, *nenda/nendeni*, *lete/leteni*.
- **With an object, place or adverb after the verb.** *Funga mlango*, *Njoo hapa*, *Soma polepole* "read slowly", *Ngoja kidogo*.
- **With *tafadhali* "please".** Before or after the command. Wiktionary also gives a plural *tafadhalini*, matching the *-ni* of the verb.
- **Everyday set phrases.** *Karibu!* "welcome" behaves like a command and takes *-ni* for several people: *Karibuni!* Thompson and Schleicher translate it literally "come near!". *Pole!* "sorry", plural *Poleni!*, works the same way (lesson 2.8).
- **Not for "don't...!".** A negative command is built on the subjunctive (*usisome* "don't read"), taught in lesson 4.11. Thompson and Schleicher note that commands with object prefixes also use the subjunctive (lesson 4.10).

## Watch out

**Do not use the infinitive as a command.** *\*Kusoma!* is "reading", not "read!". Only short verbs keep *ku-*: *Soma!* but *Kula!*

**Do not drop *ku-* from short verbs.** *\*La!* and *\*Nywa!* are not commands. Say *Kula!*, *Kunywa!*

**Do not regularise the three irregulars.** *\*Ja!*, *\*Kuja!* for "come!" and *\*Enda!* for "go!" follow the rules but not the standard. Say *Njoo!* and *Nenda!* Wikipedia's grammar article notes that *kuja!* and *enda!* are heard, particularly in Kenya, and may sound less polite; do not copy them. And *\*Leta!* is *Lete!*

**Do not give loan verbs *-eni*.** *\*Jibueni* and *\*Fikireni* change a vowel loan verbs keep. Say *Jibuni!*, *Fikirini!*

**Do not use the singular for a group.** *Soma!* to a whole class tells one person. Use *Someni!* And do not use *Someni!* to one person, however politely.

**Do not add a subject prefix to make it "you".** *\*Usoma!* and *\*Unasoma!* are not commands; the second is a statement, "you are reading". The command has no prefix. (A form with *u-* and final *-e*, *usome*, exists: it is the subjunctive of lesson 4.10.)

**Do not negate a command with the negative present.** *\*Husomi!* means "you don't read", a statement. "Don't read!" needs the subjunctive, *Usisome!*, taught in lesson 4.11.

**Do not confuse the two kinds of *-ni*.** In *nendeni nyumbani*, the first *-ni* means "you (several)" and the second means "at" (the place ending of lesson 3.13). They sound alike and do different jobs.

**Do not command elders.** A bare *Njoo!* to a grandparent or a teacher is rude. Almasi and colleagues say Swahili speakers use requests, not commands, with their elders. Until lesson 4.10, add *tafadhali* and keep commands for equals and children.

**Do not confuse *rudi* and *rudia*.** *Rudi!* (loan verb) is "come back!"; *Rudia!* (native verb with final *-a*) is "repeat!". Their plurals are *rudini!* and *rudieni!*

## Connections

Spanish also separates one listener from several: *lee* / *leed* (Spain) or *lean* (Latin America); Swahili *soma* / *someni*. Japanese and Korean have plain command forms (読め, 읽어라) that are as blunt as a bare *soma*, and soften them in the same spirit Swahili does, with request forms. Arabic *iqraʾ* / *iqraʾū* "read!" also marks the plural.

## Exercises

1. Give the singular and plural commands of *-andika*, *-fikiri* and *-nywa*.
2. Tell several guests to come in. Tell one child to come here.
3. Take apart *Fungueni dirisha* and translate it.
4. Tell one person to bring water, politely.
5. Correct *\*Kusoma polepole!* (to one person) and *\*Jibueni!*
6. Tell a group of students to be quiet and listen.
7. Why is "eat!" *kula* but "read!" *soma*?
8. A learner says *\*Unaingia!* to mean "come in!". What did he say, and what should he say?

## Worked answers

1. *-andika* is native: *andika!*, *andikeni!* *-fikiri* is a loan verb: *fikiri!*, *fikirini!* *-nywa* is short, so it keeps *ku-*: *kunywa!*, *kunyweni!*
2. "Enter" is *-ingia*, native; for several: *Ingieni!* (*Karibuni!* is the usual welcome.) "Come" is irregular: *Njoo hapa!*
3. *Fungu-eni*: the stem *-fungua* "open" with its final *-a* replaced by the plural command ending *-eni*. *Dirisha*: window. "Open the window!" (to several people).
4. *Lete* is the irregular singular of *-leta*: *Tafadhali, lete maji.* or *Lete maji, tafadhali.*
5. Not a short verb, so no *ku-*: *Soma polepole!* *-jibu* is a loan verb: *Jibuni!*
6. Both verbs are native, so both take *-eni*: *Nyamazeni! Sikilizeni!* You can address them too: *Nyamazeni, wanafunzi! Sikilizeni!*
7. *-la* has a one-syllable stem, which cannot stand alone as a command, so it keeps *ku-*: *kula*. *-soma* has two syllables and stands alone.
8. *Unaingia* is a statement: "you are coming in". The command has no prefix and no tense: *Ingia!* (*Karibu!* is the polite way to invite someone in.)
