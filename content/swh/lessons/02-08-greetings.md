## The idea

Almasi and colleagues open their chapter on greetings by saying that Swahili speakers take greetings seriously. A conversation, a visit, a purchase in a shop: each starts with an exchange of greetings, often several, and skipping them is rude. The good news is that you can now build most of them yourself, because they are made of grammar from this part.

The most famous greeting is *hujambo?* "how are you?". Look at it with lesson 2.3 in mind. *Hu-* is the negative prefix "you (one) not", as in *husomi* "you don't read". *Jambo* is a noun, "matter, affair". Wiktionary explains *hujambo* as a contraction of *huna jambo*, "you have no matter", using the "have" of lesson 2.6. So the question is literally "you have nothing (wrong)?", and the answer is the same shape in the first person: *sijambo*, "I have nothing (wrong)", that is, "I'm fine". Both question and answer are negative. A positive answer would announce a problem.

Because the greeting is built on the negative subject prefixes, it changes for person exactly as they do. To several people: *hamjambo?*; they answer *hatujambo*. About someone absent: *hajambo?* "is he or she well?", answered *hajambo*; about several, *hawajambo?*, answered *hawajambo* (Almasi and colleagues). The *jambo* greeting, they note, is the only one that may start with *je*: *Je, watoto hawajambo?*

The second family of greetings asks for news. *Habari* "news" is a noun you know from lesson 1.2. *Habari?* or *Habari gani?* "what news?" asks how things are; *Habari za asubuhi?* "news of the morning?" adds the time of day. The answer, Almasi and colleagues say, always contains *nzuri* "good", even when something is wrong: you say *nzuri* first and then add the bad news with *lakini* "but".

The third greeting is about respect. A younger person greets an older one, or anyone in authority, with *shikamoo*; the only answer is *marahaba*. Wiktionary describes *shikamoo* as a greeting from an inferior to a superior and suggests it is a shortening of a phrase about holding someone's feet, while noting that the etymology is incomplete. *Marahaba* is from Arabic *marḥaban* "welcome". Almasi and colleagues record that *shikamoo* was once the greeting of slaves to their masters on the coast; today it expresses respect to parents, grandparents, teachers and elders.

## How it is built

Greet a group of students and give their answer.

1. "You (several)" in the negative is *ham-*, from lesson 2.3. Add *jambo*: *Hamjambo?*
2. Add who you are greeting, after the greeting: *Hamjambo, wanafunzi?*
3. The answer is about "we": negative *hatu-* plus *jambo*. They add who they are answering: *Hatujambo, mwalimu.*

Ask a friend about her parents.

1. The parents are class 2, absent, so the prefix is *hawa-*: *hawajambo*.
2. You may open with *je*: *Je, wazazi hawajambo?*
3. The answer stays in the third person: *Hawajambo.* Almasi and colleagues point out that the word order can also be reversed: *Hawajambo wazazi?*

Answer "how's the news of the day?" when something is wrong.

1. Say *nzuri* first.
2. Add the problem with *lakini*: *Nzuri, lakini mtoto ana homa.*
3. The other person answers with sympathy: *Pole!* "sorry!" (to several, *poleni*), and you thank them: *Asante.*

## Where it applies

- ***Hujambo* for one person you address.** Anyone of your age or younger, and in neutral situations.
- ***Hamjambo* for a group.** Answer *hatujambo*.
- ***Hajambo*, *hawajambo* for absent people.** Ask after family and friends: *Je, wazazi hawajambo?* Almasi and colleagues note that the answer stays in the third person.
- **With titles and names.** *Hujambo, bwana?* "how are you, sir?"; *Sijambo, bibi* "I'm fine, madam". Almasi and colleagues give exactly these titles. The name or title follows the greeting.
- ***Habari* in all its forms.** *Habari?*, *Habari gani?*, *Habari za asubuhi?*, *Habari za kazi?* "how's work?", *Habari za safari?* "how was the journey?". To several people, Almasi and colleagues give *Habarini?* The answers: *nzuri*, *salama*, *njema*, often with *sana*.
- **Time of day.** *Habari za asubuhi* (morning), *za mchana* (daytime), *za jioni* (evening). Wiktionary also lists *usiku mwema* "good night".
- ***Shikamoo* to elders.** Parents, grandparents, aunts and uncles, older siblings, teachers, people in authority (Almasi and colleagues). To several elders, *shikamooni*; the answer is still *marahaba*.
- ***Salama?*** "peaceful?", answered *Salama* or *Salama sana*. Almasi and colleagues note that it has no plural.
- **Arriving and leaving.** *Hodi!* at the door, *Karibu!* or *Karibuni!* in reply; *Kwa heri* (*kwa herini*) at the end; *Karibu tena* "come again" as an answer to it.
- **Thanks, sympathy, apology.** *Asante (sana)*, *asanteni*; *karibu* "you're welcome" (Wiktionary notes this use copies English); *pole*, *poleni* for sympathy; *samahani* "excuse me, sorry" to apologise or to pass.
- **Casual speech.** *Mambo?* answered *poa* or *safi*. Wiktionary labels *safi* in this sense colloquial and *poa* Sheng, the Nairobi youth language; Almasi and colleagues call *mambo* a recent greeting of young people.
- **A single word *jambo*.** Wiktionary lists *jambo!* as "hello". The full forms *hujambo* and *sijambo* are what this guide teaches.

## Watch out

**Do not answer *hujambo* with *ndiyo*.** The question is not a yes-no question about a fact. Answer with the matching form: *Sijambo.*

**Do not make the answer positive.** *\*Ninajambo* or *\*Nina jambo* would say "I have a matter (wrong)". The answer to a negative greeting is negative: *Sijambo.*

**Do not echo the question.** *Hujambo? – \*Hujambo* repeats "you are fine". Switch the person: *Sijambo.* A group answers *hatujambo*, not *sijambo*.

**Do not use *hujambo* to a group.** *\*Hujambo, wanafunzi?* addresses one student. Say *Hamjambo, wanafunzi?*

**Do not answer *habari* with bad news first.** Almasi and colleagues say the answer always contains *nzuri*: say *Nzuri, lakini...* and then the problem, not a bare negative word.

**Do not put *je* in front of other greetings.** Almasi and colleagues note that the *jambo* greeting is the only one that may be preceded by *je*: *Je, hujambo?* is fine, *\*Je, habari?* is not the usual way.

**Do not greet elders with *hujambo* or *mambo*.** Use *shikamoo*. And do not answer *shikamoo* with *shikamoo*: it is said only upward. The elder answers *marahaba*.

**Do not use *poa* everywhere.** It is youth slang. With strangers, officials and elders, use *sijambo*, *nzuri* or *salama*.

**Do not say *asante* to a group.** Say *asanteni*. *Karibu* likewise becomes *karibuni*, *pole* becomes *poleni*, *kwa heri* becomes *kwa herini*.

**Do not confuse *pole* and *samahani*.** *Pole* is sympathy ("I'm sorry that happened to you") and is said even when it is not your fault. *Samahani* is an apology or "excuse me". Almasi and colleagues give the answer to *samahani* as *bila samahani* "no need to apologise".

**Do not read *hu-* in *hujambo* as the habitual.** Lesson 4.12 introduces a habitual *hu-* that looks the same. In *hujambo* it is the negative "you", as the answers *sijambo* and *hamjambo* show.

## Connections

Many greetings ask a question whose expected answer is "nothing wrong": Arabic *kayf al-ḥāl?* is answered *al-ḥamdu li-llāh* whatever the truth. *Shikamoo*/*marahaba* is a real parallel to the Korean and Japanese habit of choosing greetings by age and rank (안녕하세요 to elders, 안녕 to friends), though Swahili marks it with separate words rather than verb endings. *Karibu*, *asante*, *salama* and *marahaba* are all Arabic loans.

## Exercises

1. Greet one friend with the *jambo* greeting and give her answer.
2. Greet a group of guests and give their answer.
3. Ask a colleague how his wife is, and give his answer.
4. Take apart *Hatujambo* and translate it literally and naturally.
5. You meet your teacher's grandmother. What do you say, and what does she answer?
6. Answer *Habari za kazi?* saying that things are fine but you are in a hurry.
7. Thank a whole family and say goodbye to them.
8. Correct *Hujambo? – \*Ndiyo, hujambo.*
9. Why is the answer to *hujambo* negative?

## Worked answers

1. One person: *Hujambo?* She answers in the first person: *Sijambo.*
2. Several people: *ham-*. *Hamjambo?* They answer about "we": *Hatujambo.* (*Hamjambo, wageni?* names them.)
3. *Mke* "wife" is class 1, so the negative prefix is *ha-*: *Je, mke hajambo?* The answer stays third person: *Hajambo.*
4. *Ha-*: not. *-tu-*: we. *Jambo*: matter. Literally "we (have) no matter"; naturally, "we're fine".
5. Respect for an elder: *Shikamoo, bibi.* She answers *Marahaba.*
6. The answer starts with *nzuri*: *Nzuri, lakini nina haraka.* (Lesson 2.6: haste is "had".)
7. Plural *-ni* on both: *Asanteni sana! Kwa herini!*
8. *Ndiyo* does not answer a greeting, and *hujambo* repeats "you". Switch to the first person: *Sijambo.*
9. *Hujambo* is a contraction of *huna jambo* "you have no matter" (Wiktionary). "I'm fine" is "I have no matter": *sijambo*. A positive form would say that something is wrong.
