## The idea

You already know two noun classes. *Mtu* "person" is class 1 and its plural *watu* is class 2. Every word that depends on them carries a matching prefix: *mtu anasoma*, *watu wanasoma*. You also met the three place classes in lesson 2.5, hidden inside *nipo*, *niko*, *nimo*. Part 3 fills in the rest of the system.

A **noun class** is a set of nouns that share two things: a prefix on the noun itself, and a fixed set of prefixes that other words take when they refer to those nouns. The second part matters more. Two nouns belong to the same class because the rest of the sentence treats them the same way, not because they look alike.

Here is the proof. *Mtu* "person" and *mti* "tree" both start with *m-*. But when they fall, the verb shows they are different: *mtu anaanguka*, *mti unaanguka*. The person takes *a-*; the tree takes *u-*. *Mti* is class 3. And *simu* "telephone" has no visible prefix at all, yet the verb still tells you its class: *simu inalia* "the phone is ringing" (class 9, *i-*), *simu zinalia* "the phones are ringing" (class 10, *zi-*). The noun did not change; the verb did.

Each prefix that a word takes to match a noun is an **agreement prefix**. Grammars of Bantu languages call it a **concord**, and you will meet that word in other books. This guide says "agreement prefix".

Most classes come in pairs: one singular, one plural. Swahili has no plural ending like English *-s*. A noun shows its number by changing class: *kitabu* "book" is class 7, *vitabu* "books" is class 8. In the numbering used here, the odd number is the singular and the even number the plural for classes 1 to 10, as Thompson and Schleicher point out. Class 11 is singular and borrows class 10 for its plural. Class 14 holds abstract nouns and has no plural. Classes 15 to 18 do not count at all: 15 is the class of verbs used as nouns, and 16 to 18 are places.

Why does Swahili sort its nouns like this? It inherited the system. The Bantu ancestor language already had classes marked by prefixes, and Wiktionary traces *mti* back to a reconstructed *\*mʊ̀tɪ́* and *kitu* "thing" back to *\*kɪ̀ntʊ̀*, prefix included. Almasi and colleagues say the division once followed how speakers saw the world: people in one class, animals in another, sharp and elongated objects in another, and so on. Meaning still shows through (classes 1 and 2 are people, classes 3 and 4 hold most trees), but centuries of change and borrowing have blurred it. For most nouns the class has to be learnt with the word.

The system also does a job. Because every verb carries the class of its subject, a listener always knows which noun the verb is about, even after the noun has been dropped. *Inalia* on its own can only be about a class 9 thing: "it (the phone) is ringing".

## How it is built

To use any noun in a sentence you need three facts.

1. **Its class.** Dictionaries and this guide give the pair: *kitabu* (7/8). Wiktionary writes the same thing with Roman numerals ("class VII, plural class VIII").
2. **Its plural.** Swap the singular prefix for the plural one: *ki-tabu* → *vi-tabu*, *m-ti* → *mi-ti*. Where there is no visible prefix, the plural looks the same: *simu* → *simu*.
3. **The agreement prefixes of that class.** Each class has a subject prefix for verbs, an adjective prefix, and others you will meet in this part.

Say "the books are falling".

1. *Kitabu* is class 7; the plural is class 8: *vitabu*.
2. The class 8 subject prefix is *vi-*.
3. Present *-na-*, root *-anguk-*, final *-a*: *vinaanguka*.
4. *Vitabu vinaanguka.*

The same chain works for people, which you already do: *watoto* is class 2, the prefix is *wa-*: *watoto wanaanguka*.

**Three ways of naming the classes.** You will meet all three.

- **Numbers**, 1 to 18, shared by the Bantu languages, so that class 7 in Swahili corresponds to class 7 in its relatives (Wikipedia's grammar article). Class 13 does not exist in Swahili and class 12 is extremely rare (Thompson and Schleicher). This guide uses numbers.
- **Pair names by the noun prefix**: m-wa (1/2), m-mi (3/4), ji-ma (5/6), ki-vi (7/8), n (9/10), u (11 and 14), and the place and infinitive classes pa, ku, mu. Almasi and colleagues organise their book this way, with eight named classes.
- **School names by agreement.** East African schools name each class after the subject prefixes it takes, singular and plural: A-WA, U-I, LI-YA, KI-VI, I-ZI, U-ZI, U-U, KU-KU, PA, KU, MU (University of Kansas course notes). So the m-mi class is "U-I" because *mti* takes *u-* and *miti* takes *i-*.

The Swahili word for a noun class is *ngeli*. Thompson and Schleicher give the class names as *ngeli ya kwanza* "first class", *ngeli ya pili* "second class", and so on.

## Where it applies

- **Every noun has a class**, including loanwords. *Kitabu* came from Arabic and was fitted into class 7 because it began with *ki*; *simu* came from Persian through Omani Arabic and sits in classes 9/10 (Wiktionary).
- **Names of people** take class 1 agreement: *Juma anasoma*. Thompson and Schleicher note that a name such as *Zawadi* is class 1 as a girl's name even though *zawadi* "gift" is class 9.
- **Names of countries** take class 9 agreement, whatever their shape (Almasi and colleagues; lesson 3.6).
- **Every word that refers to a noun agrees with it.** In this part: the subject prefix on the verb (here and in each class lesson), adjectives (lesson 3.3), possessives (3.10), the *-a* of "of" (3.11), demonstratives (3.12). Later: object prefixes (4.8), numbers (5.3), relatives (part 6).
- **When the noun is not said.** The subject prefix alone points back to it: *Vinaanguka* "they (the books) are falling".
- **Places and verbs as nouns.** Classes 16 to 18 (lesson 3.13) and class 15, the *ku-* form used as a noun (lesson 4.12), have agreement prefixes too: you have already used class 17 in *kuna*.
- **Grammar words in Swahili.** *Umoja* "singular" and *wingi* "plural" are themselves class 14 nouns, and a noun class is a *ngeli*.

## Watch out

**Do not use *a-* for things.** *\*Kitabu anaanguka* gives a book the person prefix. A thing takes the prefix of its own class: *Kitabu kinaanguka.*

**Do not read *m-* as "person".** Classes 1, 3 and 18 all show *m-* on the noun. *\*Mti anaanguka* treats a tree as a person. Check the plural (*miti*, not *\*wati*) and use class 3 *u-*: *Mti unaanguka.* The same goes for *mw-*: *mwalimu* "teacher" is class 1, but *mwaka* "year" is class 3 (plural *miaka*).

**Do not look for a plural ending.** *\*Simus* and *\*masimu* are not words. Many nouns, like *simu*, have the same form in both numbers; the agreement shows the difference: *simu inalia*, *simu zinalia*.

**Do not expect the subject prefix to copy the noun prefix.** Sometimes it does (*kitabu kinaanguka*), often it does not (*gari linafika*, *mti unaanguka*). Learn the agreement prefixes as a set for each class.

**Do not think of a class as a gender.** Spanish *la* and French *la* mark feminine nouns. Swahili classes have nothing to do with sex: a man and a woman are both class 1, and both take *a-*: *mwanamume anasoma*, *mwanamke anasoma*.

**Do not mix the naming systems.** The school name "U-I" means classes 3/4 (*mti*, *miti*), not the *u-* nouns of class 11, whose school name is "U-ZI". When a book says "the U class", check which system it uses.

**Do not assume every noun has both numbers.** Abstract class 14 nouns such as *uhuru* "freedom" have no plural, and some class 6 nouns such as *maji* "water" have no singular.

## Connections

French and Spanish gender is the nearest European idea: *la casa blanca* repeats the gender of *casa* on *la* and *blanca*, as *vitabu vinaanguka* repeats class 8. The differences are size and basis: two genders against sixteen classes, and sex against shape and meaning. Japanese counters (一本, 一枚, 一匹) also sort nouns by shape and kind, but only when counting; Swahili sorts them in every sentence.

## Exercises

1. *Mji* "town" has the plural *miji*. Which class pair is it in, and what is its school name?
2. Say "the trees are falling" and "a tree is falling".
3. Take apart *Zinalia* and say what it could be about.
4. Why can *simu* mean "telephone" or "telephones", but *simu inalia* only "the phone is ringing"?
5. Correct *\*Gari anafika*.
6. *Mwaka* and *mwalimu* both start with *mw-*. How would you find out that they are in different classes?
7. Say "the songs are beginning", given that the plural of *wimbo* is *nyimbo*, class 10.
8. A friend says: "Swahili has eight noun classes." Another says: "Swahili has sixteen." Explain how both can be right.

## Worked answers

1. *M-* singular and *mi-* plural: classes 3/4, the m-mi pair. Its school name, from the subject prefixes, is U-I: *mji uko mbali* "the town is far", *miji iko mbali* "the towns are far".
2. *Miti* is class 4: *i-*, then *-na-*, root, *-a*: *Miti inaanguka.* Singular class 3 *u-*: *Mti unaanguka.*
3. *Zi-*: class 10 subject. *-na-*: present. *-li-*: ring. *-a*: final vowel. "They are ringing", about a class 10 noun: *simu* in the plural, for example.
4. The noun is the same in classes 9 and 10. The verb prefix decides: *i-* is class 9, singular; class 10 would be *zinalia*.
5. *Gari* is class 5, and *a-* is the class 1 prefix for people. Class 5 takes *li-*: *Gari linafika.*
6. Look at the plural or at the agreement. *Mwalimu* has the plural *walimu* and takes *a-* (class 1). *Mwaka* has the plural *miaka* and takes *u-* (class 3).
7. *Nyimbo* is class 10, so the subject prefix is *zi-*: *Nyimbo zinaanza.*
8. They count differently. The Bantu numbering lists sixteen classes (1 to 11 and 14 to 18), counting singular and plural separately. Almasi and colleagues group singular and plural together and name eight classes (M-/WA-, M-/MI-, JI-/MA-, KI-/VI-, N-, U-, PA-, KU-). The nouns and their agreement are the same either way.
