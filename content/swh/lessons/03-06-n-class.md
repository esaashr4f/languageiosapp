## The idea

Classes 9 and 10 are the largest pair in Swahili, according to Almasi and colleagues, because most loanwords land here: *meza* "table", *kalamu* "pen", *simu* "telephone", *picha* "picture" (from English), *barua* "letter" (from Arabic). Native words are here too: *nyumba* "house", *nchi* "country", *njia* "path", *nguo* "cloth, clothes", *siku* "day".

The singular and the plural look the same. *Nyumba* is "house" or "houses"; *meza* is "table" or "tables". Only the agreement tells you which: class 9 takes *i-* on the verb, class 10 takes *zi-*. *Nyumba iko wapi?* "where is the house?"; *Nyumba ziko wapi?* "where are the houses?". The school name, I-ZI, comes from these prefixes.

Why are they the same? The prefix of both classes was a nasal, written N by grammarians. Polomé describes what happens when that N meets the first sound of a stem: it becomes *m* before *b* and *v*, *n* before *d*, *g*, *j*, *z*, and it disappears before voiceless sounds (*p*, *t*, *ch*, *k*, *f*, *s*) and before *m* and *n*. Lesson 1.3 told the same story from the sound side: *paka* "cat" lost the nasal it once had. Since the singular and the plural prefix were both N, they produce the same noun.

So a class 9/10 noun may show a nasal (*mbuzi*, *ndizi*, *nguo*, *njia*, *nzige*, *nyumba*) or nothing (*paka*, *kazi*, *meza*, *chai*). Either way, this guide does not cut the nasal off the noun: treat it as part of the word.

The same N is the class 9 and 10 **adjective** prefix, and here it matters, because adjectives start with many different sounds. The adjective takes the shape the rule predicts from its own first letter, not from the noun's:

- before *d*, *g*, *j*, *z*: *n-*. *-dogo* → *ndogo*, *-gumu* → *ngumu*, *-zuri* → *nzuri*, *-zito* → *nzito*.
- before *b*: *m-*. *-baya* → *mbaya*, *-bovu* → *mbovu*.
- before *r*: the N and the *r* become *nd*. *-refu* → *ndefu*. Polomé lists this change (N + *l* or *r* → *nd*); it is also why the plural of *ulimi* "tongue" is *ndimi* (lesson 3.7).
- before everything else (*k*, *t*, *ch*, *f*, *s*, *p*, *n*, *m*): nothing. *Kubwa*, *tamu*, *chafu*, *fupi*, *safi*, *nene*.

Now an old puzzle from lesson 2.8 is solved. *Habari gani?* is answered *Nzuri*. *Habari* "news" is class 9, so "good" agreeing with it is *nzuri*.

## How it is built

Say "the long table is here".

1. *Meza*, class 9. No change to the noun.
2. "Long": *-refu*. N + *r* → *nd*: *ndefu*. *Meza ndefu*.
3. Class 9 subject *i-*, spot *-po*: *ipo hapa*.
4. *Meza ndefu ipo hapa.*

Now "the long tables are here".

1. *Meza*, class 10: still *meza*.
2. The adjective is still *ndefu* (the same N).
3. Class 10 subject *zi-*: *zipo hapa*.
4. *Meza ndefu zipo hapa.* One letter, *z*, carries the plural.

Say "the road is bad".

1. *Njia*, class 9.
2. *-baya*, N before *b* → *mbaya*. After *ni*: *ni mbaya*.
3. *Njia ni mbaya.*

The subject prefixes are *i-* and *zi-*; negatives *hai-* and *hazi-* (Almasi and colleagues, table 10.1; Thompson and Schleicher, chapter 20).

## Where it applies

- **Loanwords**, the biggest group: from Arabic (*kalamu*, *habari*, *barua*, *sahani* "plate", *bei* "price", *sukari*), from English (*picha*, *baiskeli* "bicycle", *wiki* "week"), from Persian through Omani Arabic (*rangi* "colour", *meza*, according to Wiktionary, which notes that *meza* is widely but wrongly claimed to be Portuguese).
- **Native nouns with a nasal**: *nyumba*, *njia*, *nguo*, *ndizi* "banana", *mboga* "vegetable", *ndoo* "bucket", *nchi*.
- **Native nouns that lost the nasal**: *siku* "day", *kazi*, *chupa* "bottle", *taa* "lamp".
- **Countries and places by name**: *Tanzania*, *Kenya*, even those that start with *U-* like *Uingereza* "Britain" (Almasi and colleagues: *Urusi imeendelea* "Russia has progressed", with class 9 *i-*).
- **Animals and kin terms**: *paka*, *mbuzi*, *rafiki*, *baba*. They are class 9/10 nouns but take the agreement of classes 1 and 2 (lesson 3.9).
- **Plural of class 11** *u-* nouns: *ukuta* "wall" → *kuta* (class 10), lesson 3.7.
- **Masses and single things with no plural**: *chumvi* "salt", *sukari*, *mvua*, *chai*. They take class 9 agreement.
- **A noun you cannot see.** Almasi and colleagues note that when an unnamed thing is meant, class 9 is used: *Itanukia* "it will smell nice" (future, part 4).
- **The reply *nzuri***, agreeing with *habari* in *Habari gani?* (lesson 2.8).

## Watch out

**Do not mark the plural on the noun.** *\*Manyumba* or *\*nyumbas* do not exist. "Houses" is *nyumba*, and the verb says *zi-*: *nyumba ziko*.

**Do not use class 9 *i-* for a plural.** *\*Nyumba kubwa iko wapi?* for "where are the big houses?" says "house". Plural: *ziko*.

**Do not leave the adjective bare by analogy with class 5.** *\*Meza zuri* copies *gari zuri*. Class 9 adds N before *z*: *meza nzuri*.

**Do not add a nasal where it cannot go.** *\*Chai ntamu* and *\*nyumba nkubwa* put N before voiceless sounds. The nasal drops: *chai tamu*, *nyumba kubwa*.

**Do not keep *r* after the nasal.** *\*Meza nrefu* is impossible; *\*meza refu* is the class 5 form. Class 9: *meza ndefu*.

**Do not use *n-* before *b*.** *\*Njia nbaya* is wrong: *njia mbaya*. Likewise *mbovu* "rotten".

**Do not take the adjective's shape from the noun.** *Nyumba* starts with *ny*, but "nice house" is *nyumba nzuri*, not *\*nyumba nyzuri*. The adjective prefix adapts to the adjective.

**Do not confuse class 9 *i-* with class 4 *i-*.** *Inaanguka* can be about *miti* (class 4) or *nyumba* (class 9). The adjective often separates them: *miti mirefu*, but *nyumba ndefu*.

**Do not use *u-* agreement for countries in *U-*.** *\*Uingereza ni nchi* is fine, but with a verb or adjective the country agrees in class 9: *Uingereza iko mbali* "Britain is far away", not *\*Uingereza uko mbali*.

**Do not give class 9 animals and relatives *i-* yet.** *\*Paka inakula* treats the cat as a thing. People and animals take class 1 and 2 agreement: *paka anakula*. Lesson 3.9.

## Connections

French has a faint echo of this: *la table*, *les tables* sound alike in speech, and the article does the work of the plural, as *i-* and *zi-* do here. Korean and Japanese go further and usually do not mark the plural at all; Swahili always does, but on the verb and its partners, not on these nouns.

## Exercises

1. Give the class 9 forms of *-dogo*, *-baya*, *-refu* and *-kali*.
2. Say "the bottle is small" and "the bottles are small".
3. Take apart *Hazipo hapa* and say what it could be about.
4. Say "the bananas are not sweet".
5. Correct *\*Meza zuri iko hapa.*
6. Correct *\*Nyumba ni nkubwa.*
7. Translate *Picha nzuri ziko wapi?*
8. *Nguo ndefu iko ndani.* Is the speaker talking about one cloth or several clothes? How do you know?
9. Why does *Habari gani?* get the answer *nzuri* and not *mzuri* or *kizuri*?

## Worked answers

1. *Ndogo* (n before d), *mbaya* (m before b), *ndefu* (N + r → nd), *kali* (no nasal before k).
2. *Chupa ni ndogo* (class 9). The plural noun and adjective are the same; with *ni* nothing else changes: *Chupa ni ndogo* again. To make the number clear, use a verb: *Chupa ndogo zipo hapa* "the small bottles are here" against *Chupa ndogo ipo hapa*.
3. *Ha-*: not. *-zi-*: class 10 subject. *-po*: at this spot. *Hapa*: here. "They aren't here", about class 10 things, such as plates (*sahani*) or buckets (*ndoo*). There is no final *-i*: place forms have no verb root (lesson 2.5).
4. *Ndizi* is class 10 in the plural; *-tamu* has no nasal before *t*: *Ndizi si tamu.* (With *si* the number does not show. *Ndizi tamu hazipo* "there are no sweet bananas here" shows it on the verb.)
5. *Meza* is class 9: N before *z*: *Meza nzuri iko hapa.*
6. N disappears before *k*: *Nyumba ni kubwa.*
7. *Picha*: picture(s). *Nzuri*: class 9/10 "good". *Ziko*: class 10 "are (somewhere)". *Wapi*: where. "Where are the nice pictures?"
8. One cloth. The verb *iko* has the class 9 prefix *i-*; several clothes would be *ziko*.
9. *Habari* "news" is class 9, and the class 9 form of *-zuri* is *nzuri*. *Mzuri* would be class 1 or 3, *kizuri* class 7.
