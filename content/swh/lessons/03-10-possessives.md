## The idea

A Swahili possessive is one word made of two parts. The **stem** says who owns: *-angu* "my", *-ako* "your" (one person), *-ake* "his, her, its", *-etu* "our", *-enu* "your" (several), *-ao* "their". The **prefix** agrees with the thing owned. So "my book" is *kitabu changu*: *ch-* because *kitabu* is class 7, *-angu* because the owner is "I". "My books" is *vitabu vyangu*: the owner is the same, the thing owned is now class 8.

The prefix never agrees with the owner. *Kitabu changu*, *kitabu chako*, *kitabu chake*: the owner changes, the prefix stays *ch-*. This is how French works (*sa mère*, *son père*: the gender of *mère* and *père*, not the owner's), and it is the opposite of English *his* and *her*. Swahili *-ake* does not show the owner's sex at all: *kitabu chake* is "his book" or "her book".

The possessive follows the noun, always: "book my". If there is also an adjective, the possessive comes first, right after the noun: *kitabu changu kikubwa* "my big book". Almasi and colleagues state the order as noun, possessive, adjective.

Where do the prefixes come from? Every possessive stem begins with a vowel, so each class prefix meets a vowel and changes shape by the rules of lesson 1.6. Compare the possessive prefixes with the subject prefixes you know: *ki-* gives *ch-* (*changu*), *vi-* gives *vy-* (*vyangu*), *li-* gives *l-* (*langu*), *zi-* gives *z-* (*zangu*), *i-* gives *y-* (*yangu*), *u-* gives *w-* (*wangu*), *ya-* gives *y-* (*yangu*). Class 1 is the one surprise: its subject prefix is *a-*, but its possessive prefix is *w-*, the same as class 2 (*mtoto wangu*, *watoto wangu*). Almasi and colleagues list the full set (table 12.2), and Wiktionary's table for *-angu* matches it.

## How it is built

Say "my big book is here".

1. The thing owned: *kitabu*, class 7.
2. The owner: "I" → *-angu*. Class 7 possessive prefix *ch-*: *changu*. Straight after the noun: *kitabu changu*.
3. "Big": class 7 *kikubwa*, after the possessive: *kitabu changu kikubwa*.
4. The verb agrees with *kitabu*: *kipo hapa*.
5. *Kitabu changu kikubwa kipo hapa.*

Say "their houses are big".

1. *Nyumba*, plural: class 10. Possessive prefix *z-*.
2. The owners are people, "they": *-ao*. *Nyumba zao*.
3. Predicate: *ni kubwa* (class 10, no nasal before *k*).
4. *Nyumba zao ni kubwa.*

**Kin terms and friends.** Lesson 3.9 said that people take class 1 and 2 agreement. The possessive is the exception. With *rafiki* and with kin terms in class 9/10, the possessive takes the class 9 and 10 prefixes, *y-* and *z-* (Almasi and colleagues; Thompson and Schleicher): *rafiki yangu* "my friend", *rafiki zangu* "my friends", *dada yangu* "my sister", *dada zangu* "my sisters". Everything else still treats them as people: *rafiki yangu anasoma*, *dada zangu wanaishi Mombasa*.

For animals the two grammars differ in wording. Almasi and colleagues limit the exception to kin terms, so animals take *w-*. Thompson and Schleicher state that every noun takes the possessive of its own class, but their own example is *mbwa wetu* "our dogs", with *w-*. Both, then, show *w-* for animals: *paka wangu* "my cat".

**Contractions.** With some very common nouns the possessive fuses with the noun (Thompson and Schleicher; Wiktionary): *mwenzangu* "my companion, my colleague" (from *mwenzi wangu*), *mwanangu* "my child" (from *mwana wangu*), *mamangu* "my mother", *mumewe* "her husband" (from *mume wake*), *mkewe* "his wife". They mean the same as the full forms.

## Where it applies

- **Every class**, with the prefixes of the reference table: *mti wangu*, *miti yangu*, *gari langu*, *magari yangu*, *ukuta wangu*, *kuta zangu*, *uhuru wetu*.
- **"Mine", "yours" standing alone**, after *ni*: *Kiti ni changu.* The prefix still agrees with the thing owned.
- **With adjectives**: noun, possessive, adjective: *nyumba yetu ndogo* "our small house".
- **Kin terms and *rafiki***: class 9/10 prefixes on the possessive (*mama yangu*, *baba zetu*, *rafiki zako*), class 1/2 on everything else. Almasi and colleagues list *mama, baba, dada, kaka, bibi, babu, shangazi, binamu, jamaa, ndugu, shemeji, rafiki*.
- **Contractions** with family words: *mwanangu*, *mwenzangu*, *mamangu*, *mumewe*, *mkewe*.
- **Things with several owners.** Thompson and Schleicher note that a house or a country is usually "ours": *nchi yetu* is the natural "my country", and most speakers would not say *\*nchi yangu*.
- **Places**: *nyumbani kwetu* "at our home", with the place prefix *kw-* (lesson 3.13).
- **"Its" for things**: *-ake*. For several things, Wiktionary gives *-ake* as "their" too; *-ao* is used for people (and animals).

## Watch out

**Do not make the prefix agree with the owner.** *\*Kitabu wangu* takes *w-* from "me, a person". The prefix comes from *kitabu*: *kitabu changu.*

**Do not put the possessive before the noun.** *\*Changu kitabu* follows English and Spanish (*mi libro*). Swahili: *kitabu changu.*

**Do not put the adjective between the noun and the possessive.** *\*Kitabu kikubwa changu* is the wrong order. *Kitabu changu kikubwa.*

**Do not use the subject prefix as it is.** *\*Kitabu kiangu* and *\*nyumba iangu* ignore the vowel meeting. *Changu*, *yangu*.

**Do not use *a-* for class 1.** *\*Mtoto aangu* copies the subject prefix. The class 1 possessive prefix is *w-*: *mtoto wangu.*

**Do not use *w-* with kin terms.** *\*Rafiki wangu* applies the animate rule where it does not reach. *Rafiki yangu*, *rafiki zangu*.

**Do not use *y-* for kin terms in the plural.** *\*Dada yangu wanaishi* mixes a singular possessive with a plural verb. Several sisters: *dada zangu wanaishi*.

**Do not look for "his" versus "her".** *-ake* covers both, as Spanish *su* does. If it matters, say who: *kitabu cha Juma* "Juma's book" (lesson 3.11).

**Do not overuse possessives.** English says "I saw my doctor", "our cleaner was ill". Thompson and Schleicher note that Swahili leaves the possessive out in such cases and uses it mainly for family and friends.

**Do not use *-ao* for "their" with things.** For the roots of trees, the walls of houses, Wiktionary gives *-ake*: *miti na mizizi yake* "trees and their roots" (*mizizi* "roots"). *-ao* is for people.

## Connections

French matches Swahili exactly on the main point: *son livre*, *sa maison* agree with the thing owned, and *son* is "his" or "her". Spanish *su* also hides the owner's sex, and agrees in number with the thing: *sus libros*, like *vyangu* against *changu*. Japanese and Korean say "my" with a separate word before the noun (私の本, 내 책) and agree with nothing.

## Exercises

1. Say "my chair", "your chairs" and "our chair".
2. Say "his name is long".
3. Take apart *Funguo zako ziko wapi?* and translate.
4. Say "my friends are tall".
5. Correct *\*Gari wangu ni zuri.*
6. Correct *\*Changu kikombe kiko wapi?*
7. Correct *\*Kitabu kikubwa changu kipo.*
8. Translate *Mjomba wake na shangazi yake wanaishi Nairobi.*
9. Say "the trees and their roots" in Swahili, and explain why it is not *\*mizizi yao*.

## Worked answers

1. *Kiti changu*; *viti vyako* (class 8 *vy-*); *kiti chetu*.
2. *Jina lake ni refu.* Class 5: possessive *l-*, bare adjective.
3. *Funguo*: keys (class 10, plural of *ufunguo*). *Z-ako*: class 10 "your". *Zi-ko*: class 10 "are". *Wapi*: where. "Where are your keys?"
4. *Rafiki* is a kin-type noun: class 10 *z-* on the possessive; people, so class 2 *wa-* on the adjective: *Rafiki zangu ni warefu.*
5. *Gari* is class 5: the possessive prefix is *l-*. *Gari langu ni zuri.*
6. The possessive follows the noun: *Kikombe changu kiko wapi?*
7. The possessive comes straight after the noun: *Kitabu changu kikubwa kipo.*
8. "His (or her) uncle and his (or her) aunt live in Nairobi." *Mjomba* is class 1, so *wake* with *w-*; *shangazi* is a kin term in class 9, so *yake* with *y-*; the verb is class 2 for both people.
9. *Miti na mizizi yake.* *Mizizi* is class 4: prefix *y-*. The owners are trees, not people, so Wiktionary's form for "their" is *-ake*, not *-ao*.
