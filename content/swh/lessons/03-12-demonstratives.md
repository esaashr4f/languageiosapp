## The idea

English has two pointing words, "this" and "that". Swahili has three, and each agrees with its noun's class.

- **This**, near the speaker: *kitabu hiki* "this book", *mtu huyu* "this person".
- **That**, at a distance from both speaker and listener: *kitabu kile* "that book (over there)", *mtu yule* "that person".
- **That one**, the one just mentioned or already known: *kitabu hicho* "that book (we were talking about)", *mtu huyo*. Wiktionary defines *hiyo* as "that (which we were just speaking of before)". Thompson and Schleicher describe it as "mentioned earlier"; Almasi and colleagues call it the demonstrative of reference (for proximity); they add that the *-le* form placed before the noun does the same job for something at a distance (*lile ua* "that flower we spoke of").

The third form is the new idea. It does not point at a place but back into the conversation. If someone says *Nina gari jipya* "I've got a new car" (*jipya* "new", lesson 5.1), you ask *Gari hilo ni zuri?* "Is it (that car) nice?", not *gari hili* or *gari lile*. This guide glosses it ANA, for "anaphoric": referring back to something already mentioned. Descriptions differ on how to count: the World Atlas of Language Structures codes Swahili with a two-way distance contrast, while Wikipedia's grammar article describes three forms. This guide follows Polomé and the teaching grammars: two distances plus a referring form.

All three are built from the subject prefix you know, and the formulas are regular (Polomé; Thompson and Schleicher; Almasi and colleagues):

- **"This"**: *h-* + the vowel of the subject prefix + the subject prefix. Class 7 *ki-*: *h-i-ki*, *hiki*. Class 6 *ya-*: *h-a-ya*, *haya*. Class 3 *u-*: *h-u-u*, *huu*.
- **"That"**: the subject prefix + *-le*. Class 7: *ki-le*, *kile*. Class 6: *ya-le*, *yale*.
- **"That one"**: the "this" form with its last vowel turned into *-o*. *Hiki* → *hicho*, *haya* → *hayo*, *huu* → *huo*. Where the last syllable ends in *i*, the change to *o* affects the consonant before it, exactly as in lesson 1.6 (Thompson and Schleicher): *hiki* → *hicho*, *hivi* → *hivyo*, *hii* → *hiyo*.

Class 1 is again the exception: instead of *a-* it uses *yu-*, the same *yu-* as in *yuko* (lesson 2.5). *Huyu* "this person", *yule* "that person", *huyo* "that person (mentioned)".

You already know some demonstratives. *Hapa* "here", *pale* "there" and *kule* "over there" from lesson 2.5 are the class 16 and 17 forms: "this place", "that place". And there is a promise to keep from lesson 2.5: *huko*.

## How it is built

Say "this book is mine".

1. *Kitabu*, class 7: subject prefix *ki-*.
2. "This": *h-* + *i* + *ki* = *hiki*. After the noun: *kitabu hiki*.
3. "Mine": class 7 *changu*, after *ni*.
4. *Kitabu hiki ni changu.*

Say "that tree over there is tall".

1. *Mti*, class 3: subject prefix *u-*.
2. "That": *u-* + *-le* = *ule*. *Mti ule*.
3. *ni mrefu*.
4. *Mti ule ni mrefu.*

Someone has mentioned some children. Say "those children are playing".

1. *Watoto*, class 2: *wa-*.
2. "This" would be *hawa*; the referring form turns the last vowel into *-o*: *hao*.
3. *Watoto hao wanacheza.*

**Where it goes.** The usual place is after the noun. If there is a possessive, it comes first, then the demonstrative: Polomé's *matunda yetu haya* "these fruits of ours". A demonstrative can also come before the noun (*huyu mtu*). Thompson and Schleicher say this does not change the meaning; Polomé finds it mainly in lively speech, when the noun is what the conversation is about; Almasi and colleagues link it with the referring use. Put it after the noun yourself, and expect both orders when you read.

**Standing alone.** A demonstrative can replace its noun: *Huyu ni rafiki yangu* "this is my friend"; *Ninataka hiki* "I want this one".

## Where it applies

- **Pointing**: *hiki* "this (here, by me)", *kile* "that (over there)".
- **Referring back**: the *-o* forms for anything already mentioned or known to both speakers: *gari hilo*, *watoto hao*, *huko*. In stories and conversation these are the commonest demonstratives.
- **Near the listener.** Wikipedia's grammar article also describes the *-o* form for something near the person spoken to: *kitabu hicho* "that book (by you)".
- **Standing alone** for a noun: *Huyu ni nani?* "who is this?" (*nani* "who", lesson 5.10); *Ninataka hiki.*
- **Every class**, including people and animals (class 1/2 forms: *paka huyu*, *mbwa wale*), with the reference table below.
- **Places**: *hapa*, *pale*, *hapo* (class 16); *huku*, *kule*, *huko* (17); *humu*, *mle*, *humo* (18). Lesson 3.13 uses them with place nouns.
- **Emphasis by doubling**: Polomé gives *usiku ule ule* "that very night", and identifying pairs such as *papo hapo* "right there, then and there" (Wiktionary).
- **Adverbs from class 8**: *hivi* "like this", *hivyo* "so, thus" (Wiktionary), and *sasa hivi* "right now".

## Watch out

**Do not use the "this" or "that" form for something already mentioned.** After *Nina gari*, asking *\*Gari hili ni zuri?* points at a car in front of you. The referring form is *Gari hilo ni zuri?*

**Do not invent a class 1 form from *a-*.** *\*Haa*, *\*ale* are not words. Class 1 uses *yu-*: *huyu*, *yule*, *huyo*.

**Do not use the plural people forms for things.** *\*Vitabu hawa* gives books the class 2 form. *Vitabu hivi.* But animals do take class 2: *mbwa hawa*.

**Do not take the form from the noun's own class when it is animate.** *\*Paka hii* treats a cat as a class 9 thing. *Paka huyu.*

**Do not forget the consonant change in the *-o* forms.** *\*Hiko* and *\*hivo* keep the *k* and *v*. Before *o*: *hicho*, *hivyo*; *hii* → *hiyo*.

**Do not confuse *huko* "there" with *huko* "you are not there".** Lesson 2.5 promised this one. *Huko* is the negative of *uko* (*hu-* "not you" + *-ko*), and it is also the class 17 referring demonstrative "there". Wiktionary lists both meanings under the same spelling; *humo* likewise is "in there" and "you are not inside". Position tells them apart. The negative stands where a verb stands and has a subject: "you". The demonstrative stands where a place word stands, usually after a verb: in *Hayuko huko* "she isn't there", *hayuko* is the verb and *huko* the place. So *Huko nyumbani* on its own is "you're not at home", while *Yuko huko nyumbani* is "she's there at home".

**Do not use *si* or *ha-* on a demonstrative.** *\*Hakiki* is not "not this". Negate the verb or the link: *Si hiki, ni kile* "it's not this one, it's that one".

**Do not mix up *hapa* and *hapo*.** *Hapa* is "here, where I am"; *hapo* is "there, at that spot we mentioned" (or "then").

**Do not use *mule* where the standard has *mle*.** The class 18 "that inside" is *mle* in Polomé, Thompson and Schleicher, Almasi and colleagues, and Wiktionary's entry; Wiktionary's noun-class appendix writes *mule*, and Polomé notes that the *u* survives in some dialects. Use *mle*.

## Connections

Japanese and Korean are the best anchor: これ / それ / あれ and 이 / 그 / 저 also have three terms, and the middle one (それ, 그) is used both for things near the listener and for things just mentioned, exactly like Swahili *hicho*. Spanish *este / ese / aquel* is a three-way system of distance only, and it does not agree in sixteen ways.

## Exercises

1. Give "this", "that" and "that one" for *jicho* (class 5) and *macho* (class 6).
2. Say "these chairs are ours".
3. Take apart *Mbwa yule ni mkali* and translate.
4. A friend says *Nina rafiki Mkenya.* Ask "Where does that friend live?" using *-ishi* and *wapi*.
5. Correct *\*Vitabu hawa ni vyangu.*
6. Correct *\*Paka hii analala.*
7. Correct *\*Kitabu hiko ni kizuri* (meant: "that book you mentioned").
8. Say "this water is hot" with an *-a* phrase.
9. In *Hayuko huko*, what are the two words? Why could *huko* on its own mean something else?

## Worked answers

1. Class 5 (*li-*): *hili*, *lile*, *hilo*. Class 6 (*ya-*): *haya*, *yale*, *hayo*.
2. *Viti hivi ni vyetu.* Class 8: *h-i-vi*, and the possessive *vy-* + *-etu*.
3. *Mbwa*: dog. *Yule*: that (class 1, because a dog is an animal). *Ni mkali*: is fierce (class 1 adjective). "That dog over there is fierce."
4. *Rafiki huyo anaishi wapi?* The friend has been mentioned: referring form *huyo*, class 1 for a person.
5. Books are class 8: *Vitabu hivi ni vyangu* (or *vile*, *hivyo*, as meant).
6. A cat is an animal: *Paka huyu analala.*
7. The referring form of class 7 is *hicho*, with *ki* → *cho*: *Kitabu hicho ni kizuri.*
8. *Maji haya ni ya moto.* *Maji* is class 6: *haya* "this"; after *ni*, the *-a* phrase *ya moto* "hot".
9. *Hayuko*: *ha-* not, *yu-* class 1, *-ko* there: "she isn't there". *Huko*: the class 17 referring demonstrative "there". On its own, *huko* is also the negative of *uko*, "you aren't there".
