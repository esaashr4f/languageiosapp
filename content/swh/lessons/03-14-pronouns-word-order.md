## The idea

You met the pronouns *mimi*, *wewe*, *yeye*, *sisi*, *ninyi*, *wao* in lesson 2.1, and you have been leaving them out ever since: *Ninasoma* already says "I am reading". This lesson makes three things explicit: when the full pronoun is used, the order of words in a plain sentence, and the short pronoun forms that attach to *na* "and, with".

**Pronouns are for emphasis.** The verb's subject prefix names the subject in every sentence, so a pronoun adds nothing to the meaning except weight. *Mimi ninasoma* is "*I* am reading" or "as for me, I'm reading". Use it to contrast people (*mimi ... lakini wewe ...*), to answer "who?", or after *ni* (*Ni mimi* "it's me"). Spanish works the same way: *leo* is normal, *yo leo* is emphatic. French, which must say *je lis*, does not.

**There is no "it" pronoun.** Polomé notes that the free pronouns are, in practice, used only for people. For a thing, Swahili uses the prefix alone (*Kipo* "it's here"), a demonstrative (*hicho* "that one", lesson 3.12) or a short reference form, below. Wiktionary's gloss of *yeye* includes "it", but in practice *yeye* is "he" or "she".

**Neutral word order is subject, verb, object** (SVO), as in English and Spanish. Polomé gives this as the most common clause pattern, and the World Atlas of Language Structures codes Swahili as SVO. Time words such as *leo* and *sasa* go at the start or at the end; a place usually comes after the object. Nothing in this order changes for a yes-no question (lesson 2.4). Lessons 3.13 and 4.8 show the two common ways of putting something else first: a place as subject (*Chumbani mna kitanda*), and an object with an object prefix.

**The short forms with *na*.** *Na* "and, with" does not combine with the full pronouns in careful style. It takes a short form instead, written as one word: *nami* "and I, with me", *nawe* "and you, with you", *naye* "and he/she, with him/her", *nasi* "with us", *nanyi* "with you (all)", *nao* "with them" (Wiktionary; Polomé). Things have their own: *nacho* "with it (class 7)", *nalo* (class 5), *nayo* (classes 4, 6, 9), *nazo* (class 10), *navyo* (class 8), *nao* (classes 3 and 11).

Where do these come from? Polomé explains them as the class prefix plus a "referential" *-o*, the same *-o* that made *hicho* and *huyo* in lesson 3.12: *ki* + *o* → *-cho*, *zi* + *o* → *-zo*, *wa* + *o* → *-o*. This *-o* means "the one already mentioned". Wiktionary's verb appendix calls it the **o of reference**; you will meet it again in relative clauses (part 6).

**Short answers.** The same reference forms solve a problem left open in lesson 2.6. A "yes, I have it" answer needs to refer back to the thing: *Una kalamu? – Ndiyo, ninayo* "Have you got a pen? – Yes, I have (it)". *Nina* + *-yo* (class 9, for *kalamu*). Polomé gives *tunavyo* "(yes) we have (them)" as the answer to a question about books, and the negative *hatunayo* "we haven't got them" about eggs.

## How it is built

**Contrast.** Say "I'm reading Swahili, but she's reading Arabic".

1. The verbs alone: *ninasoma Kiswahili*, *anasoma Kiarabu*.
2. The point is the contrast between the two people, so add the pronouns: *Mimi ninasoma Kiswahili, lakini yeye anasoma Kiarabu.*

**"X and I".** Say "Juma and I are at the market". Two subjects, one of them "I", make "we": the verb takes *tu-* (Almasi and colleagues' exercises have *mimi na John ... hatujambo* and *mimi na wewe tulionana*).

1. *Juma na mimi*: here *na* joins two subjects, and the full pronoun is normal.
2. "We are (somewhere)": *tuko*.
3. *Juma na mimi tuko sokoni.*

**"With him".** Say "is the child with the dog? – Yes, he's with it".

1. "Be with" is a subject prefix + *na*: *yuko na mbwa* "he is with the dog" (lesson 2.6's *-wa na*).
2. In the answer the dog has been mentioned. A dog is an animal, so the short form is class 1 *-ye*: *naye*.
3. *Mtoto yuko na mbwa? – Ndiyo, yuko naye.*

**"I've got it".** Say "have you got my book? – No, I haven't (got it)".

1. *Una kitabu changu?*
2. Negative "I have": *sina*. Add the class 7 reference form *-cho*: *sinacho*.
3. *Hapana, sinacho.* (*Hapana, sina* is also complete; the reference form makes it clear you mean that book.)

## Where it applies

- **Leaving the pronoun out**: the normal case, in every tense: *Ninasoma*, *Tuko sokoni*.
- **Pronoun for emphasis or contrast**: *Mimi ninasoma, wewe unaandika.*
- **Pronoun after *ni* and *si***: *Ni mimi* "it's me", *Si yeye* "it isn't him".
- **Pronoun alone**, answering "who?": *Nani anapika? – Mimi.* (*nani* "who", lesson 5.10.)
- **Joined subjects**: *Juma na mimi tu-...*, *wewe na Juma m-...* "you and Juma" (plural "you").
- **Short forms after *na***: "with me" (*yuko nami*), "and you" (*mimi nawe*), and *nami pia* "me too". With things: *nacho*, *nalo*, *nayo*, *nazo*, *navyo*, *nao*.
- **Short answers with "have"**: *ninayo*, *ninacho*, *tunavyo*, and the negatives *sinacho*, *hatunayo*. Choose the ending by the class of the thing asked about.
- **"Alone, by myself"**: *peke yangu*, *peke yako*, *peke yake*, *peke yetu*, *peke yenu*, *peke yao* (Polomé, Wiktionary).
- **"Together"**: *pamoja* "together" (Wiktionary: *pa-* + *moja* "one"), and *pamoja na* "together with": *pamoja nami* "together with me" (Polomé).
- **Word order** in every plain statement and yes-no question: subject, verb, object; time first or last; place after the object.

## Watch out

**Do not use a pronoun in every sentence.** *Mimi ninasoma. Mimi ninapenda chai. Mimi niko nyumbani.* sounds insistent, like *yo* in every Spanish sentence. Use the verb alone unless you mean to stress the person.

**Do not drop the subject prefix and keep the pronoun.** *\*Mimi soma* or *\*mimi na-soma* leave the verb without its first slot. The prefix is required; the pronoun is optional: *Mimi ninasoma.*

**Do not use *yeye* for things.** *\*Kitabu? Yeye yuko hapa* treats a book as a person. Say *Kipo hapa*, or *Hicho kipo hapa*.

**Do not write *na* plus a full pronoun where a short form is expected.** *Nami*, *nawe*, *naye* are the standard written forms (Wiktionary marks them as contractions of *na mimi*, *na wewe*, *na yeye*). In the plural you will also see *na sisi*, *na ninyi* written apart: Polomé reported in 1967 that the bound plural forms were then used mainly with *ndi-*.

**Do not use *na* to join clauses.** *Na* joins nouns and pronouns. Lesson 5.9 shows how clauses are joined in careful style; for now, put a comma, as in the examples.

**Do not answer "yes, I have" with *nina* alone.** *Una kalamu? – \*Ndiyo, nina.* leaves *na* "with" hanging. Repeat the noun (*nina kalamu*) or add the reference form: *ninayo*.

**Do not pick the reference form from the person.** In *Una kitabu? – Ninacho*, *-cho* is for *kitabu* (class 7), not for "I". *\*Ninaye* would mean "I have him (or her)".

**Do not use a singular verb for "X and I".** *\*Juma na mimi niko sokoni* agrees with "I" only. Two people including "I" are "we": *tuko*.

**Do not put the object first by itself.** English "This book I like" has no direct Swahili equivalent without an object prefix. Keep subject, verb, object until lesson 4.8.

**Do not confuse *peke yangu* with *changu*.** *Peke yangu* "by myself" always takes class 9 *y-*, whoever is alone; *changu* is "my" for a class 7 thing.

## Connections

Spanish is the closest model for dropping and using pronouns: *estudio* / *yo estudio*. Japanese and Korean drop pronouns even more freely, and like Swahili have no ordinary word for "it". The short forms with *na* have a near parallel in Spanish *conmigo*, *contigo*: a preposition and a pronoun fused into one word.

## Exercises

1. Say "we're at home" without a pronoun, then with one, and explain the difference.
2. Say "you (one) and I are students".
3. Take apart *Tunazo* as an answer to *Mna funguo?* and translate.
4. Answer *Una gari?* with "yes, I have (it)" and with "no, I haven't (got it)".
5. Correct *\*Kisu kiko wapi? Yeye yuko jikoni.*
6. Correct *\*Juma na mimi wako sokoni.*
7. Correct *\*Ndiyo, nina* as an answer to *Una kalamu?*
8. Say "the teacher is with them" and "the teacher is with it" (the "it" being a book).
9. Put these words in a neutral order: *chai / leo / mgeni / anataka*.
10. Someone says *Ninapenda kahawa.* Answer "me too", then say "Juma too".

## Worked answers

1. *Tuko nyumbani.* With the pronoun: *Sisi tuko nyumbani*, "*we* are at home", contrasting us with someone else (lesson 3.14).
2. *Mimi nawe ni wanafunzi.* Two people including "I": a plural noun, *wanafunzi*.
3. *Tu-*: we. *-na*: with. *-zo*: class 10 reference form, pointing back to *funguo*. "(Yes,) we have (them)."
4. *Gari* is class 5: reference form *-lo*. *Ndiyo, ninalo.* *Hapana, sinalo* (or *Hapana, sina*).
5. *Yeye* is for people. *Kisu* is class 7: *Kiko jikoni* "it's in the kitchen".
6. "Juma and I" is "we": *Juma na mimi tuko sokoni.*
7. *Na* needs something after it: *Ndiyo, ninayo* (class 9, for *kalamu*), or *Ndiyo, nina kalamu.*
8. *Mwalimu yuko nao* (people: class 2 *-o*). *Mwalimu yuko nacho* (class 7 *kitabu*: *-cho*).
9. *Leo mgeni anataka chai* or *Mgeni anataka chai leo*: subject, verb, object, with the time word first or last.
10. *Nami pia.* For a named person, *na* takes the noun itself: *Na Juma pia.* The short forms replace pronouns, not names.
