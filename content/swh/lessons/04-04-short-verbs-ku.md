## The idea

A handful of very common verbs have a root of a single consonant: *-l-a* "eat", *-nyw-a* "drink", *-j-a* "come". With the final vowel, the whole stem is one syllable. You have met them behaving oddly since part 2: *ninakula* "I am eating" has a *ku* that *ninasoma* does not; yet the negative is *sili*, without it; and the command is *kula!*, not *\*la!*. Lesson 2.2 promised an explanation, and lesson 2.7 promised to explain the *ku-* of *kula!*. Here it is.

The cause is stress. Lesson 1.5 showed that a Swahili word is stressed on its second-to-last syllable, the penult. Now build "I am eating" without *ku*: *\*ni-na-la*. The penult is *na*. But the tense marker *-na-* is one of the pieces that cannot carry the word's stress. Polomé states it plainly: the markers *-na-*, *-li-*, *-me-*, *-ta-*, *-nge-*, *-ngali-* cannot be stressed. Something stressable has to stand in the penult. Swahili supplies it by keeping the *ku-* of the infinitive: *ni-na-KU-la*.

So the *ku* in *ninakula* is not a new meaning. It is the infinitive prefix (lesson 2.2), kept as a "stress-bearer", in Polomé's words. This guide glosses it KU.

The rule follows at once. **Keep *ku-* whenever the syllable before the root could not carry the stress; drop it when that syllable can.** Wiktionary's verb appendix lists the pieces that can be stressed: subject and object prefixes, the negative past *-ku-*, *-si-*, *-ki-*, *-ka-*, *hu-*, the *-a-* tense, and, for most speakers, *-ja-*. Everything else calls for *ku-*.

Check it on forms you know:

- *Sili* "I'm not eating": the negative present has no tense marker, so the syllable before the root is the subject prefix *si-*. It can carry stress: *SI-li*. No *ku*.
- *Sikula* "I didn't eat": the syllable before the root is the negative past *-ku-*, which can carry stress: *si-KU-la*. No extra *ku* (Almasi and colleagues stress that the *-ku-* here is the past marker).
- *Nilikula*, *nitakula*: *-li-* and *-ta-* cannot be stressed, so *ku-* stays, in the positive and in the negative future (*sitakula*).
- *Kula!* "eat!": a command has no prefix at all (lesson 2.7). Without *ku-*, *\*la!* would be a one-syllable word with no penult. The command therefore takes the infinitive: *kula!*, *kunywa!*, plural *kuleni!*, *kunyweni!* (Almasi and colleagues; Wiktionary).

Two longer verbs behave in the same way: *-enda* "go" and *-isha* "finish". Their infinitives are *kwenda* and *kwisha*, with *kw-* before the vowel, and they keep it where short verbs keep *ku-*: *ninakwenda*, *nilikwenda*, *itakwisha*. Polomé notes that *kw-* appears with these verbs "analogically", under the same conditions as *ku-* with one-syllable stems, and that the root of *kwenda* once began with a consonant, a trace of which survives in another word. Both sources also report that the verb is drifting towards the ordinary pattern: *anaenda*, *ameenda* are also used (wpgrammar), and Thompson and Schleicher print *wameenda*. This guide teaches *kw-* as the standard and accepts the other.

## How it is built

Say "the guests came yesterday".

1. Root: *-ja* "come", root *-j-*, a short verb.
2. Subject *wageni*, class 2: *wa-*. Past: *-li-*.
3. Can *-li-* carry the stress? No. Keep *ku-*: *wa-li-ku-j-a*.
4. *Wageni walikuja jana.*

Now "the guest didn't come".

1. Negative subject, class 1: *ha-*. Negative past: *-ku-*.
2. Can *-ku-* carry the stress? Yes. No extra *ku-*: *ha-ku-j-a*.
3. *Mgeni hakuja.*

And "the child won't drink the medicine".

1. Negative class 1 *ha-*, future *-ta-*.
2. Can *-ta-* carry the stress? No: *ha-ta-ku-nyw-a*.
3. *Mtoto hatakunywa dawa.*

The question is always the same: what stands in front of the root, and can it be stressed?

## Where it applies

- **The short verbs**: *-la* "eat", *-nywa* "drink", *-ja* "come", *-wa* "be, become", *-fa* "die", *-cha* "dawn", *-chwa* "set (of the sun)", and *-pa* "give" (lesson 4.7; it always has an object prefix, so it never shows *ku-*). Almasi and colleagues list these as the common ones.
- **Two longer verbs**: *-enda* (*kwenda*) and *-isha* (*kwisha*), with *kw-*.
- **After *-na-*, *-li-*, *-ta-*, *-me-***: *ninakula*, *nilikula*, *nitakula*, *nimekula* (lesson 4.5). Also after *-nge-*, *-ngali-* (part 6) and after relative markers (part 6).
- **In the negative future**: *sitakula*.
- **Commands without an object**: *kula!*, *kunywa!*, *kuleni!*. The irregular *njoo* "come!" and *nenda* "go!" (lesson 2.7) replace *kuja!* and *kwenda!*.
- **Not after stressable pieces**: negative present *sili*, negative past *sikula*, the "not yet" *-ja-* (*sijala*, lesson 4.5; *sijakula* is also heard, wpgrammar), *-ki-* (lesson 4.14), *-ka-* (lesson 4.15), habitual *hu-* (*hula*, lesson 4.13), and the subjunctive (*nile*, lesson 4.10).
- **Not with an object prefix**: *alikila* "he ate it", *atayanywa* "he'll drink it" (Polomé's example), lesson 4.8.

## Watch out

**Not *\*ninala*.** The *ku* is not optional after *-na-*: *ninakula*. (Spoken *nakula* is the shortened *ninakula*, lesson 2.2, and still has *ku*.)

**Not *\*sikukula*.** The negative past *-ku-* already carries the stress. A second *ku* is wrong: *sikula*. Almasi and colleagues spell this out.

**Not *\*hakuli*.** The negative present drops *ku-*: *hali* "he doesn't eat". Lesson 2.3 noted that *hali* is also a noun, "condition"; context separates them.

**Not *\*la!* or *\*nywa!*.** A one-syllable command cannot be stressed. Say *kula!*, *kunywa!*.

**Do not keep *ku-* in front of an object prefix.** *\*Alikukila* is wrong for "he ate it": *alikila*. (*Alikukula* would mean "he ate you", with object *-ku-*: lesson 4.7.)

**Do not treat *kuja* and *-ja-* as one.** *Kuja* is the verb "come". Lesson 4.5 adds a tense marker *-ja-* "not yet", which Wiktionary derives from this verb. In *hajaja* "he hasn't come yet", the first *ja* is the tense, the second the root.

**Do not spell *kwenda* as *kuenda*.** The standard infinitive is *kwenda* (Polomé; Wiktionary). Thompson and Schleicher print *kuenda* in one exercise; treat it as a variant. Inflected forms with or without *kw-* both occur: *anakwenda*, *anaenda*.

***Kuwa* is "be", not "have".** "I had" needs *na*: *nilikuwa na gari*. *Nilikuwa gari* says "I was a car".

## Connections

Many languages insert material to satisfy stress or syllable shape. Spanish and French do it with vowels (Spanish *escuela* from Latin *schola*). Swahili does it with a whole morpheme borrowed from elsewhere in the grammar, the infinitive prefix. Japanese verbs never face the problem: the shortest stems (見る, 寝る) already have two syllables.

## Exercises

1. Say "we drank juice" and "we didn't drink juice". Explain the *ku* in each.
2. Say "she will come tomorrow" and "she won't come tomorrow".
3. Say "they are going to the restaurant".
4. Tell one child, then several, to drink the porridge.
5. Correct *\*Wageni hawakukula.*
6. Correct *\*Ninala chapati.*
7. Why is it *sili* but *sitakula*?
8. Take apart *kimekwisha* (preview of *-me-*, lesson 4.5) and say whether *kw-* is expected there.

## Worked answers

1. *Tulikunywa juisi*: *-li-* cannot be stressed, so the *ku-* of *kunywa* stays. *Hatukunywa juisi*: the one *ku* is the negative past marker, which carries the stress, so there is no second *ku*.
2. *Atakuja kesho.* *Hatakuja kesho.* *-ta-* cannot carry stress in either, so *ku-* stays in both.
3. *Wanakwenda mkahawani* (or *wanaenda*). *-enda* takes *kw-* after *-na-*.
4. *Kunywa uji!* *Kunyweni uji!* Commands of short verbs keep *ku-*; the plural adds *-eni*.
5. The negative past *-ku-* already does the job: *Wageni hawakula* "the guests didn't eat".
6. *-na-* cannot carry stress: *Ninakula chapati.*
7. In *sili* the syllable before the root is the subject prefix *si-*, which can be stressed. In *sitakula* it would be *-ta-*, which cannot, so *ku-* must be added.
8. *Ki-me-kw-ish-a*: class 7 subject, perfect *-me-*, *kw-*, root "finish", *-a*. "It has run out." *-me-* cannot be stressed (Polomé's list), so *kw-* is expected.
