## The idea

Swahili has two ways of talking about a past action. Lesson 4.2 gave you *-li-*, which places it in the past and stops there. This lesson adds *-me-*, the **perfect**: an action that is complete and whose result is in force now. *Nimepika* "I have cooked" means the food is ready. *Nilipika* "I cooked" says only that cooking happened; wpgrammar points out that the food may long since have been eaten.

Polomé puts it this way: *-me-* shows that the action is completed at the time in question, "but that its result is effectively present". His examples are telling: *msafiri amefika* "the traveller has arrived" (he is here now), and *nimesikia* "I have heard", which in context means "I understand". Thompson and Schleicher add that the perfect is used for something that "has just occurred", or that began in the past and still holds. English "have done" is the nearest equivalent, and works most of the time.

The history fits the meaning. Wiktionary derives *-me-* from an old form meaning "having finished", built on the root of *-maliza* "finish", which you learnt in lesson 4.1. It also notes older Swahili forms of the marker (*-maa-*, *-mele*), and says that this tense marker replaced an older perfect suffix. A perfect that grew out of "finish" is a common development.

The negative of the perfect is a different marker in the tense slot: *-ja-*. *Sijapika* "I haven't cooked (yet)". The front of the verb takes the negative prefix as usual; the final vowel stays *-a*. The meaning is not quite a plain "have not". Polomé describes *-ja-* as stating that the action has still not happened at the moment of speaking, while implying that it will happen in the end. Thompson and Schleicher: it "often implies that the action has not occurred *yet*". Wiktionary derives *-ja-* from *-ja* "come": "not come to doing" is a fair way to picture it.

This guide glosses *-ja-* as PRF.NEG, the negative of the perfect, and translates it "haven't (yet)". The sources disagree on whether it is the negative of *-me-* or a tense of its own (Wiktionary lists it separately as "not yet"; wpgrammar treats it as the negative of *-me-*). For building sentences the difference does not matter.

What if the action will never happen now? Almasi and colleagues give the rule: use *-ja-* if the action could still take place, and the negative past *-ku-* if it can no longer. "Have you written the letter?" can be answered *Sijaandika* "not yet" or *Sikuandika* "I didn't (and that's that)". Polomé gives the second answer exactly.

## How it is built

Say "the guests have arrived".

1. Root: *-fika* "arrive", root *-fik-*.
2. Subject *wageni*, class 2: *wa-*.
3. Completed, with the result now (they are here): *-me-*.
4. *Wa-me-fik-a*: *Wageni wamefika.*

Now "the guests haven't arrived yet".

1. Negative class 2: *hawa-*.
2. Not yet: *-ja-*.
3. Final vowel unchanged: *ha-wa-ja-fik-a*, *hawajafika*.
4. Add *bado* to make "yet" explicit if you like: *Wageni hawajafika bado.*

Short verbs keep *ku-* after *-me-*, which cannot be stressed (lesson 4.4): *nimekula* "I have eaten", *amekuja* "he has come" (Thompson and Schleicher). After *-ja-*, most speakers drop it, since *-ja-* can carry the stress: *sijala* "I haven't eaten yet" (Almasi and colleagues). Wpgrammar reports that *sijakula* is also heard.

## Where it applies

- **A completed action with a present result**: *Nimepika* (the food is ready), *Wamefika* (they are here).
- **Something that has just happened**: *Mvua imeanza* "it has started raining". News and announcements are full of *-me-*: *Rais amefika* "the president has arrived".
- **A change that has led to a state**: *Mtoto amekua* "the child has grown up". Lesson 4.6 builds a whole class of meanings on this: *nimechoka* "I am tired" is literally "I have got tired".
- **Experience, "have ever"**: *Umewahi...?*, and *Sijawahi...* "I have never...".
- **"Not yet"** with *-ja-*, often with *bado*: *Hawajafika bado*. As an answer, *Bado* alone (lesson 2.4).
- **Expected events that have not happened**: *Basi halijafika* "the bus hasn't come (and we are waiting)". Thompson and Schleicher give the same idea with a train.
- **A flat "didn't"** with *-ku-*, when the chance is gone: *Sikuandika*.
- **Every class**: *Chakula kimeiva* "the food is cooked", *Kikombe kimeanguka* "the cup has fallen".
- **Questions**: *Umemaliza?* "Have you finished?" Answer *Ndiyo, nimemaliza* or *Bado* / *Sijamaliza*.

## Watch out

**No negative with *-me-*.** *\*Simepika* or *\*Hawamefika* puts a negative prefix in front of a positive-only marker. The negative of *-me-* is *-ja-*: *Sijapika*, *Hawajafika*.

**No final *-i* after *-ja-*.** *\*Sijapiki* borrows the negative present ending. Say *Sijapika*.

**Do not use *-ja-* for a closed chance.** "I didn't finish the exam (and it's over)" is *Sikumaliza mtihani*, not *\*Sijamaliza mtihani jana*: *-ja-* implies it may still happen (Almasi and colleagues).

**Do not stack *bado* with a positive to mean "not yet".** *Bado nimekula* does not mean "I haven't eaten yet". With a positive verb, *bado* means "still" (lesson 2.4; lesson 4.17). "Not yet" needs *-ja-*.

**The perfect is not English "had".** *Wamefika* is "they have arrived". "They had arrived" (before something else) is a compound with *kuwa*: *walikuwa wamefika* (lesson 4.16).

**Do not use *-me-* for French or Spanish compound pasts everywhere.** French *j'ai mangé* serves as an ordinary past in speech; Swahili *nimekula* does not. "I ate at six yesterday" is a plain past event: *Nilikula jana*, with *-li-*.

***-ja-* is not *kuja*.** In *hajaja*, the tense *-ja-* is followed by the root *-j-*. In *amekuja* there is no tense *-ja-* at all.

## Connections

English "have done" and Swahili *-me-* overlap closely, much more than Spanish or French compound pasts, which drift towards a plain past. Korean has a similar resultative idea in -아/어 있다 (앉아 있다 "be seated"), and lesson 4.6 shows Swahili doing the same with *-me-*.

## Exercises

1. Put into the perfect: *Ninasafisha chumba*, *Wanachagua rais*, *Tunakunywa chai*.
2. Make each answer of exercise 1 "not yet".
3. Translate: *Basi halijafika, lakini tiketi zimekwisha.*
4. Answer *Umeandaa chakula cha jioni?* with "yes", "not yet" and "no, I didn't (and won't)".
5. Say "I have never been to Kenya".
6. Correct *\*Hatumefika.*
7. Correct *\*Sijaenda sokoni jana.*
8. Take apart *hajaja* and *amekuja*, and say where *ja* appears in each.

## Worked answers

1. Replace *-na-* with *-me-*: *Nimesafisha chumba*, *Wamechagua rais*, *Tumekunywa chai* (*ku-* stays after *-me-*).
2. Negative prefix, *-ja-*, final *-a*: *Sijasafisha chumba*, *Hawajachagua rais*, *Hatujanywa chai* (no *ku-* after *-ja-*).
3. "The bus hasn't arrived, but the tickets have run out." *Ha-li-ja-fik-a*: class 5 negative, "not yet". *Zi-me-kw-ish-a*: class 10 perfect of *-isha*, with *kw-*.
4. *Ndiyo, nimeandaa.* *Bado* or *Sijaandaa (bado).* *Hapana, sikuandaa.* The last is the flat negative past (Almasi and colleagues' rule).
5. *Sijawahi kwenda Kenya.* *-ja-* on *-wahi*: "I have not yet had the chance", i.e. "never so far".
6. The perfect has no negative with *-me-*: *Hatujafika* "we haven't arrived yet".
7. *Jana* closes the chance: use the negative past. *Sikwenda sokoni jana* "I didn't go to the market yesterday".
8. *Ha-ja-j-a*: negative class 1, tense *-ja-* "not yet", root *-j-* "come": "he hasn't come yet". *A-me-ku-j-a*: class 1, perfect, KU, root *-j-*: "he has come". *Ja* is a tense marker only in the first.
