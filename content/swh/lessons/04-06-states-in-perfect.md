## The idea

How do you say "I am tired" in Swahili? Not with *ni* and an adjective. Swahili says *nimechoka*, "I have got tired". The verb *-choka* does not mean "be tired". It means "get tired", the change from not tired to tired. Put that change in the perfect, which (lesson 4.5) shows a completed action whose result holds now, and you get the state: having got tired, I am tired.

Verbs like *-choka* describe entering a state. Grammarians call them **inchoative** verbs (from Latin *incohare* "begin"): verbs whose meaning is the beginning of a state. Wpgrammar lists many and states the point directly: Swahili often has no separate word for "be tired", "be drunk", "be late"; these meanings are expressed by an inchoative verb with *-me-*, or with *-ja-* in the negative. Many ideas that English expresses with an adjective are in Swahili verbs of this kind.

Thompson and Schleicher make the same point in a note on the perfect: a few verbs "use the perfect tense with a present tense meaning", and their list includes *nimechelewa* "I am late", *amekaa* "she is sitting", *mmepotea* "you are lost", *wamechoka* "they are tired" and *kimevunjika* "it is broken". Wiktionary gives *-me-* two senses: a perfect with active verbs, and a "stative" marker with verbs like these (*wamelala* "they are asleep", *umechelewa* "you are late").

This changes how you read the other tenses of these verbs. With *-na-*, the verb describes the change in progress: *ninachoka* "I am getting tired", *analala* "she is falling asleep, she goes to sleep". With *-li-*, it reports the change as an event: *nilichoka* "I got tired". Only *-me-* gives the state.

The negative state uses *-ja-*: *sijachoka* "I'm not tired (yet)", *hajalala* "he isn't asleep yet". This is the natural negative: the change has not happened. For "I wasn't tired" or "they were asleep" you need the compound tenses of lesson 4.16 (*walikuwa wamelala*, wpgrammar).

So remember one question for every English adjective or "be + -ing" of posture and condition: is there a Swahili verb for *getting into* this state? If there is, the state is its perfect.

## How it is built

Say "the children are asleep".

1. Find the verb: *-lala* "lie down, go to sleep".
2. Is "asleep" a state reached by a change? Yes: the children went to sleep and are now sleeping.
3. So use the perfect: *-me-*.
4. Subject class 2: *wa-me-lal-a*.
5. *Watoto wamelala.*

Now "the bucket is full of water".

1. Verb: *-jaa* "become full".
2. State: perfect.
3. Subject *ndoo*, class 9: *i-me-ja-a*.
4. What it is full of follows directly, with no word for "of": *Ndoo imejaa maji.* (Polomé gives the same pattern: *mitungi ile imejaa maziwa* "those pots are full of milk".)

And "I'm not hungry, I'm full"? *Sina njaa, nimeshiba.* The first half uses "have" (lesson 2.6): Swahili "has" hunger. The second uses the inchoative *-shiba* "get full (from eating)".

## Where it applies

- **Physical states**: *nimechoka* "tired", *nimeshiba* "full", *amelewa* "drunk", *amenenepa* "fat, has put on weight", *amekonda* "thin", *amezeeka* "old", *amesinzia* "dozing", *ameamka* "awake" (wpgrammar: *nimeamka* "I am awake").
- **Postures**: *ameketi*, *amekaa* "sitting", *amesimama* "standing", *amelala* "lying down, asleep".
- **Clothing**: *amevaa* "is wearing"; the reverse, *-vua* "take off", gives *amevua viatu* "he has his shoes off".
- **Feelings**: *amekasirika* "is angry", *amehuzunika* "is sad", *nimefurahi* "I'm glad", *ametulia* "is calm".
- **Conditions of things**: *imejaa* "full", *zimeiva* "ripe, cooked", *limechafuka* "dirty", *yamekauka* "dry" (of things in class 6), *kimepotea* "lost", *kimeoza* "rotten", *kimevunjika* "broken" (Thompson and Schleicher).
- **Being late, lost, gone**: *umechelewa*, *mmepotea* (Thompson and Schleicher), *ameondoka* "he's gone".
- **Death**: *amekufa*, *amefariki* "has passed away".
- **The negative state** with *-ja-*: *sijachoka*, *hajalala*, *chakula hakijaiva* "the food isn't cooked yet".
- **The process** with *-na-*: *ninachoka*, *mtoto anakua* "the child is growing".
- **The event** with *-li-*: *nilichoka sana jana* "I got very tired yesterday".

## Watch out

**Not *\*Ninachoka* for "I'm tired".** That is "I'm getting tired". The state is *Nimechoka*.

**Not *\*Mimi ni kuchoka* or *\*Mimi ni mchoka*.** English "be + adjective" tempts you to use *ni*. The idea is a verb here: *Nimechoka*.

**Not *\*Sichoki* for "I'm not tired".** *Sichoki* is the negative present: "I don't get tired". The negative of the state is *Sijachoka*.

**Not *\*Nilichoka* for "I was tired".** *Nilichoka* is "I got tired", an event. "I was tired (at the time)" is *nilikuwa nimechoka* (lesson 4.16). The same holds for *\*Walilala* meaning "they were asleep": it means "they went to sleep".

**Spanish *estar sentado* is *ameketi*, not *\*anaketi*.** *Anaketi* is "she is sitting down" (in the act), wpgrammar's gloss. Use the perfect for the posture.

**Do not translate "wearing" with *-na-*.** *Anavaa shati* is "he is putting on a shirt". "He is wearing a shirt" is *Amevaa shati*.

**Some ideas are adjectives after all.** Lesson 3.3 taught *-chafu* "dirty", *-zee* "old", *-kavu* "dry": *shati ni chafu*, *mzee*, *nguo kavu* are fine. Use the adjective for a lasting quality and the perfect for a state that came about: *shati limechafuka* "the shirt has got dirty". Thompson and Schleicher say you must learn which verbs follow the perfect rule; check new verbs in the vocabulary notes.

## Connections

Korean expresses many states the same way: 앉아 있다 "be seated" is "have sat and remain", and 입고 있다 "be wearing" is "put on and remain". Japanese does it with ている on change verbs: 疲れている "is tired", 結婚している "is married". Spanish *estoy cansado* uses a participle, which also comes from a verb of change. Swahili is part of a wide pattern.

## Exercises

1. Say "I'm awake", "I'm waking up" and "I woke up".
2. Say "the bananas are not ripe yet".
3. Translate: *Mgeni amechelewa, na mimi nimechoka kusubiri.*
4. How would you say "the students are sitting" and "the students are sitting down (now, in the act)"?
5. Say "the cup is full of tea" (*kikombe*, class 7).
6. Correct *\*Mimi ni kushiba.*
7. Correct *\*Sishibi* meant as "I'm not full".
8. Explain the difference between *Shati ni chafu* and *Shati limechafuka*.

## Worked answers

1. *Nimeamka* (state, perfect). *Ninaamka* (process, *-na-*). *Niliamka* (event, *-li-*).
2. *Ndizi hazijaiva (bado).* Class 10 negative *hazi-* + *-ja-*.
3. *A-me-chelew-a*: "the guest is late"; *ni-me-chok-a*: "I'm tired"; *kusubiri*: "of waiting" (an infinitive after the verb). "The guest is late, and I'm tired of waiting."
4. *Wanafunzi wameketi* (or *wamekaa*). *Wanafunzi wanaketi.*
5. *Kikombe kimejaa chai.* Class 7 subject, perfect of *-jaa*, the content after the verb.
6. "Full" is the perfect of *-shiba*: *Nimeshiba.*
7. *Sishibi* means "I don't get full". "I'm not full" is the negative state: *Sijashiba.*
8. *Shati ni chafu* describes the shirt with the adjective *-chafu*: it is a dirty shirt. *Shati limechafuka* says it has got dirty: a state that came about. Both are correct; the second points to the change.
