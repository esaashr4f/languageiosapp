## The idea

Lesson 4.10 used the subjunctive on its own: *usome*, *twende*, *nisaidie!* This lesson puts it after other words, where it does its most frequent work, and adds its negative.

**After wanting, asking, telling.** "I want you to come." English uses an infinitive, "to come", and puts "you" in front of it as an object. Swahili cannot do that: an infinitive has no subject slot. Instead the second verb is a full subjunctive with its own subject prefix: *Ninataka uje*, literally "I want (that) you come". Thompson and Schleicher give the rule: when the subject of the second verb differs from the subject of the first, the second verb is in the subjunctive. Their examples include *Nilitaka aje nyumbani kwetu* "I wanted her to come to our house" and *Aliomba nimsaidie* "she asked me to help her".

When the subject is the same, the infinitive is normal: *Ninataka kuja* "I want to come" (lesson 2.2). Wpgrammar adds that *-taka* is sometimes followed by a same-subject subjunctive (*Nataka niende sokoni* "I want to go to the market"), when the speaker can do something about it. Use the infinitive and recognise the other.

The verbs that take a subjunctive this way include *-taka* "want", *-omba* "ask for", *-ambia* "tell", *-shauri* "advise", *-agiza* "order", *-acha* "let" (Almasi and colleagues list these and more). With *-ambia*, *-omba* and *-shauri* the person is also an object prefix on the first verb: *Nilimwambia aende* "I told him to go" (wpgrammar).

**After "must" and "better".** Swahili expresses obligation with words, not with a verb form. *Lazima* "necessity" (lesson 1.5), with or without *ni*, followed by a subjunctive: *(Ni) lazima uende* "you must go", literally "it is a necessity that you go" (Thompson and Schleicher; wpgrammar). *Afadhali* "it would be better" and *bora* work the same way: *Afadhali tumwalike* "we'd better invite her" (Thompson and Schleicher's example uses *-alika*). *Si lazima* "it isn't necessary" + subjunctive is "don't have to": *Si lazima ulale* "you don't have to sleep" (wpgrammar).

**Purpose.** "So that" is *ili* + subjunctive: *Asome vitabu ili ajifunze* (built on Thompson and Schleicher's example) "he should read books so that he learns".

### The negative: -si-

The negative subjunctive does not use the negative prefixes *ha-*, *si-* of the statements. It keeps the ordinary subject prefix and puts *-si-* after it, in the slot this guide numbered 3 in lesson 4.1. The final *-e* stays. *U-si-som-e*: *usisome* "don't read; you shouldn't read". Thompson and Schleicher: subject marker + *-si-* + verb with *-e*.

This is how Swahili says "don't!". There is no negative command form; lesson 2.7 promised this lesson for it. Wpgrammar puts it plainly: "There is no actual negative imperative form"; the negative subjunctive does the job, "as in Spanish". To one person *usi-*, to several *msi-*: *Laila, usiende!* "Laila, don't go!" and *Wanafunzi, msiende!* (Thompson and Schleicher). With an object prefix, the object follows *-si-*: *Usinisaidie* "don't help me" (wpgrammar).

Short verbs stay without *ku-*: *usile* "don't eat", *usije* "don't come". The *-si-* can carry stress (Polomé; Wiktionary's list).

## How it is built

Say "I told the children not to go out".

1. Main verb: "I told them": *ni-li-wa-ambi-a*, *niliwaambia*.
2. The children are the subject of "go out", different from "I": subjunctive.
3. Negative: their subject prefix *wa-*, then *-si-*.
4. Root *-tok-*, final *-e*: *wa-si-tok-e*.
5. *Niliwaambia watoto wasitoke.*

Now "you must not drink this water".

1. Obligation: *lazima*.
2. The forbidden act: subjunctive with *-si-*: *u-si-nyw-e* (no *ku-*).
3. *Lazima usinywe maji haya.* (*Haya* "these", class 6, follows the pattern of lesson 3.12.)

Notice the difference between *si lazima ulipe* "you don't have to pay" and *lazima usilipe* "you must not pay". The position of the "not" decides the meaning, as in English.

## Where it applies

- **After verbs of wanting, asking, telling, advising, ordering, allowing**, when the second subject differs: *ninataka uje*, *aliomba nimsaidie*, *nilimwambia aende*, *alinishauri ninunue* "she advised me to buy", *mwache acheze* "let him play" (Almasi and colleagues).
- **After *lazima*, *ni lazima*, *si lazima*, *afadhali*, *bora*, *sharti* "it is necessary"**, and the impersonal forms *inabidi* "it is necessary" and *inafaa* "it is fitting" (Almasi and colleagues list these).
- **Purpose with *ili***: *ili ajifunze*, *ili wasinione*. After verbs of going and coming, an infinitive can also express purpose: *alirudi kucheza* or *alirudi ili acheze* (Almasi and colleagues).
- **Prohibitions**: *usiende*, *msiende*, *usile*, *usinisaidie*, *usiyanywe* "don't drink it" (class 6, wpgrammar).
- **Negative "let's" and "let me not"**: *tusisahau*, *tusifanye hivi* "let's not do this" (Thompson and Schleicher), *nisiende*.
- **After verbs of forbidding, preventing, refusing**, with the negative: *alikataza watoto wasicheze nje* "she forbade the children to play outside" (built on Almasi and colleagues' list: *-kataza*, *-zuia*, *-kataa*, *-onya*).
- **"Should I or not?"**: *niende nisiende?*
- **A failed attempt**, in narrative: Almasi and colleagues note that a negative subjunctive after a past verb can mean "but did not manage to": *Tulimtafuta paka tusimwone* "we looked for the cat but didn't find him".
- **Questions asking what to do**: *Nisubiri?* "Should I wait?"

## Watch out

**Not *\*Ninataka wewe kuja*.** English "want you to come" does not carry over. The second verb needs its own subject: *Ninataka uje.*

**Not *\*Ninataka nije* as the normal "I want to come".** With the same subject, use the infinitive: *Ninataka kuja.* (Wpgrammar reports the subjunctive here as a less common option.)

**No *ha-* in the negative subjunctive.** *\*Husome* and *\*Hasome* borrow the statement negatives. Use *-si-* after the positive prefix: *usisome*, *asisome*.

**Not *\*Usisoma*.** The final *-e* stays in the negative: *usisome*. (Loan verbs, as always, keep their vowel: *usijibu*.)

**Do not negate a command with the negative present.** *\*Husomi!* is the statement "you don't read". "Don't read!" is *Usisome!*

**Do not put a tense in the second verb.** *\*Nilimwambia atakuja* means "I told him (that) he will come", a report, not an instruction. "I told him to come" is *Nilimwambia aje.*

***Si lazima* is not "must not".** *Si lazima ulipe* "you don't have to pay"; *lazima usilipe* "you mustn't pay".

**Do not use *kwa* or "for" for purpose.** *\*Nilikuja kwa kusoma* translates "for reading" word by word. Use *ili* + subjunctive or an infinitive after a verb of motion: *Nilikuja ili nisome*, *Nilikuja kusoma*.

## Connections

Spanish draws the same line: *quiero ir* (same subject, infinitive) against *quiero que vengas* (different subject, subjunctive), and *no vayas* for "don't go". French *je veux que tu viennes* is the same. Swahili *usiende* is closer to Spanish than to French, which uses an ordinary negative imperative (*ne pars pas*).

## Exercises

1. Say "I want you (several) to read" and "I want you (several) not to read".
2. Say "we must wait" and "we don't have to wait".
3. Say "tell him not to come" (use the object command of lesson 4.10 for "tell him").
4. Forbid one person, then several, to touch the knife: use *-shika*.
5. Translate *Mama alituomba tusile chakula chote.* (*chote* "all": lesson 5.5)
6. Correct *\*Hasome!*
7. Correct *\*Nilimwomba atanisaidia* (meant: "I asked her to help me").
8. Correct *\*Ninataka yeye kuondoka.*

## Worked answers

1. *Ninataka msome.* *Ninataka msisome.* The second verb has its own subject *m-*; the negative puts *-si-* after it.
2. *Lazima tusubiri.* *Si lazima tusubiri.* A loan verb: no *-e*.
3. *Mwambie asije!* Object command *mw-ambi-e* (lesson 4.10), then the negative subjunctive *a-si-j-e*.
4. *Usishike kisu!* *Msishike kisu!*
5. "Mother asked us not to eat all the food." *Tu-si-l-e*: negative subjunctive of a short verb, without *ku-*.
6. The negative subjunctive keeps the positive prefix: *Asisome!* "he mustn't read, don't let him read". For a direct "don't read!", *Usisome!*
7. The second verb is a subjunctive, not a future: *Nilimwomba anisaidie.*
8. A different subject needs a subjunctive: *Ninataka aondoke* "I want him to leave".
