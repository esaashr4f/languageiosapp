## The idea

Swahili has a verb form just for what happens as a rule: *Ng'ombe hula majani* "cattle eat grass", *Jogoo huwika asubuhi* "the cock crows in the morning". This is the **habitual**: a form for actions that happen regularly, or that hold as general truths, without reference to any particular occasion. Polomé describes it as presenting the action "as habitual or recurrent"; Almasi and colleagues translate it with "usually", "generally" or "always".

The form is unusual in one respect. It has **no subject prefix**. The verb is *hu-* + stem, and that is all: *husoma* "(someone) usually reads". Polomé lists the habitual with the command and the infinitive as the only forms without a subject prefix. Wiktionary's verb appendix adds that the only other piece allowed is an object prefix: *hukisoma* "usually reads it". Polomé gives *vidonda hivi hujipolea* "such sores heal by themselves", with reflexive *-ji-*.

With no subject prefix, *husoma* can be "I usually read", "they usually read", "it usually reads". Almasi and colleagues list all six persons for one form. The subject comes from a noun or a pronoun in front, or from context: *Mimi hupika* "I usually cook", *Watoto hucheza nje* "children play outside". Wpgrammar notes that pronouns are therefore very often used with it.

Where does *hu-* come from? Wiktionary's etymology calls it a worn-down, fused form of *ni ku-* "is ...ing", the copula plus the infinitive prefix. That would explain why it takes no subject prefix: it was once "(it) is reading".

Short verbs take no *ku-*: *hula*, *hunywa* (Almasi and colleagues; wpgrammar). *Hu-* can carry the stress (Wiktionary's list in lesson 4.4).

**There is no negative habitual.** Wiktionary says so directly. For "doesn't usually", use the negative present: *Sipiki* "I don't cook" (Almasi and colleagues give it as the negative of *mimi hupika*).

**Proverbs and conversation.** Both Almasi and colleagues and wpgrammar note that the habitual is very common in proverbs and sayings, which state timeless truths: *Haba na haba hujaza kibaba* "little by little fills the measure". In everyday speech, wpgrammar says, it is often replaced by the *-na-* present, which already covers "generally" (lesson 2.2): *tunakula pamoja* "we eat together". To keep the habitual sense explicit, speakers put *huwa*, the habitual of *kuwa* "be", in front of an ordinary verb: *Huwa tunakula pamoja* "we usually eat together". Wpgrammar treats *huwa* as close to an adverb, "habitually".

### Two kinds of *hu-*

Lesson 2.8 taught *Hujambo?* "how are you?". Its *hu-* is not the habitual. Wiktionary explains *hujambo* as a contraction of *huna jambo* "you have no matter": *hu-* is the 2SG negative prefix of lesson 2.3. Lesson 2.3 warned that the habitual would look like it. Here is how to tell them apart:

- **Final vowel.** *Husoma* "one usually reads" ends in *-a*; *husomi* "you don't read" ends in the negative *-i*.
- **Loan verbs** have no final vowel to help. *Husafiri* is both "one usually travels" and "you don't travel". Only context decides. Look for clues: a word like *mara nyingi* "often" points to the habit; *hapana* "no" or a contrast points to the negative.
- ***Hujambo*** contains a noun, not a verb: there is no habitual reading at all.

## How it is built

Say "the rain usually falls heavily on the coast".

1. Is it a regular event? Yes: habitual.
2. Drop the subject prefix: *hu-* + *-nyesha*: *hunyesha*.
3. Name the subject in front: *Mvua hunyesha sana pwani.*

Now "I don't usually drink coffee in the evening".

1. No negative habitual: use the negative present.
2. "I" + "drink": *si-nyw-i*, *sinywi*.
3. *Sinywi kahawa jioni.*

## Where it applies

- **General truths**: *Ng'ombe hula majani.* *Jua huchwa jioni* "the sun sets in the evening".
- **Habits of a person**: *Mimi huamka mapema.* *Yeye hunywa chai bila sukari* "she drinks tea without sugar" (*bila* "without", lesson 5.8).
- **Customs of a group**: *Wakulima hufuga ng'ombe.* Wpgrammar notes that a singular noun can stand for a whole group: *Mtanzania...* "a Tanzanian (usually)...".
- **Proverbs and sayings**: *Haba na haba hujaza kibaba*; *Mchumia juani hulia kivulini.* Part 8 reads more.
- **Descriptions of how things work**: Polomé's *sabuni hupatikana dukani* "soap is (usually) to be had in the shop".
- **With object prefixes**: *hukisoma*, *huwasaidia*, *hujificha*.
- ***Huwa* + another verb**: *Huwa tunakula pamoja*; *huwa ninachelewa* "I'm usually late". Polomé also shows *huwa* in compound forms.
- **Negative**: the negative present, *sipiki*, *hawali nyama* "they don't eat meat".

## Watch out

**No subject prefix.** *\*Anahusoma*, *\*Ahusoma* and *\*Tuhusoma* put a subject prefix in front of *hu-*. The habitual has none: *Yeye husoma*, *Sisi husoma*.

**No *ku-*.** *\*Hukula* (meant as "usually eats") keeps a stress-bearer that *hu-* does not need: *hula*. (*Hukula* is in fact "you didn't eat", lesson 4.2.)

**No negative habitual.** *\*Hahusoma* does not exist. Use the negative present: *Hasomi.*

**Do not read *hu-* + *-i* as a habit.** *Husomi* is "you don't read". Only *-a* (or a loan verb) goes with the habitual.

**Do not use the habitual for one occasion.** *\*Mimi hupika sasa* contradicts itself. "Now" needs *-na-*: *Ninapika sasa.*

**Not for "used to".** The habitual is timeless. "We used to farm" (and no longer do) is a past form: *Zamani tulilima* (lesson 4.2) or *tulikuwa tukilima* (lesson 4.16; Polomé glosses *alikuwa akicheza* "used to play").

**Do not overuse it in conversation.** "Do you like coffee?" is *Unapenda kahawa?* with *-na-*. Wpgrammar notes that everyday speech usually prefers *-na-*; the habitual sounds bookish or proverbial in many contexts.

## Connections

English "cattle eat grass" uses the plain present for general truths, as Spanish and French do. Swahili has a dedicated form but, like those languages, mostly uses its ordinary present in conversation. Japanese marks habits with an adverb such as よく, not a verb form.

## Exercises

1. Say "I usually cook in the evening" and "children usually play outside".
2. Say "farmers harvest in the dry season".
3. Translate *Mvua hunyesha sana wakati wa masika.*
4. Say "I don't usually eat meat".
5. Explain the difference between *husoma* and *husomi*.
6. Is *husafiri* a habitual or a negative? What would you need to decide?
7. Correct *\*Wao wanahupika.*
8. Correct *\*Mtoto hukunywa maziwa kila siku.*

## Worked answers

1. *Mimi hupika jioni.* *Watoto hucheza nje.* No subject prefix; the noun or pronoun shows the subject.
2. *Wakulima huvuna wakati wa kiangazi.* (*Wakati wa* "the time of", lesson 3.11.)
3. "It usually rains a lot in the rainy season." *Hu-nyesh-a*: habitual of *-nyesha*.
4. There is no negative habitual: *Sili nyama.*
5. *Husoma* (final *-a*) is the habitual, "one usually reads". *Husomi* (final *-i*) is the negative present, "you don't read". The *hu-* of the second is the 2SG negative prefix.
6. Either. *-safiri* is a loan verb with no final vowel slot, so *husafiri* is both "one usually travels" and "you don't travel". Context, or a word like *mara nyingi* "often" or *hapana*, decides.
7. No subject prefix with the habitual: *Wao hupika.*
8. No *ku-* after *hu-*: *Mtoto hunywa maziwa kila siku.*
