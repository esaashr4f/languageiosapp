## The idea

The tense markers so far place an action in time: past, present, future. *-ki-* does something different. It marks an action as the **situation** in which another action happens: the background, the circumstance, the condition. This guide calls it the **situational**, following wpgrammar; Polomé describes it as marking an action "in progress at the time under consideration", used beside another verb "to mark a simultaneous process".

That one idea gives *-ki-* several English translations, all of which depend on the other verb in the sentence.

**"While, ...ing".** After a main verb, *-ki-* describes what someone was doing at the time: *Tuliwaona wakicheza* "we saw them playing". Thompson and Schleicher call this form a "tenseless participle": it works like an English "-ing" form attached to a person or thing, and because it carries a subject prefix, it agrees with what it describes: *nikienda* "I going", *wakija* "they coming". Their example *Nilienda dukani nikibeba mfuko* "I went to the shop carrying a bag" has the *-ki-* form agreeing with "I". Almasi and colleagues: "We saw them cooking" is *Tuliwaona wakipika*.

**"If, when".** Before a main verb in the future, or before a command, *-ki-* sets the condition: *Ukija kesho, utamwona mwalimu* "if you come tomorrow, you'll see the teacher". Almasi and colleagues describe this conditional *-ki-* as "invariably followed by a future tense or an imperative". Polomé's example: *ukimwona mwambie aje kwa haraka* "if you see him, tell him to come quickly". Whether to translate "if" or "when" depends on how likely the event is; Swahili does not distinguish.

**"Whenever".** With a habitual or present main verb, the condition is a recurring one: *Nikichoka, hupumzika* "whenever I get tired, I rest".

Notice that *-ki-* has no time of its own. The main verb supplies it: *tuliwaona wakicheza* is past because of *tuliwaona*; *utawaona wakicheza* would be future. Wpgrammar and Wiktionary call *-ki-* the marker of "if/when", and Polomé names it imperfective, the counterpart of *-me-*: *-me-* sees an action as complete, *-ki-* sees it as under way.

*-ki-* can carry stress, so short verbs take no *ku-*: *akija* "if he comes" (Almasi and colleagues; wpgrammar).

**The negative.** Strictly, *-ki-* has no negative of its own (wpgrammar). For a negative condition, "if not, unless", Swahili uses a form built on *-si-* and a place marker *-po-* that you will meet in part 6: *usipokuja* "if you don't come". It keeps *ku-* with short verbs, because *-po-* cannot carry stress (Almasi and colleagues: *asipokuja*). The dossier and Wiktionary note that some grammarians treat *-sipo-* as the negative of *-ki-* while others keep them apart; this guide uses *-sipo-* only for negative conditions. Another way, Almasi and colleagues show, is *kama* "if" with an ordinary negative: *Kama sisomi, sitafaulu* "if I don't study, I won't pass". For a negative "while" clause, negate the main verb instead: *Hatukuwaona wakipika* "we didn't see them cooking".

## How it is built

Say "if it rains, we won't go".

1. The condition: "it rains". Subject *mvua*, class 9: *i-*.
2. Condition marker *-ki-*; root *-nyesh-*, final *-a*: *ikinyesha*.
3. The result: negative future, *hatutakwenda*.
4. *Mvua ikinyesha, hatutakwenda.*

Now "he came running".

1. Main verb: *alikuja* (past).
2. What he was doing at the time: running, with the same subject: *a-ki-kimbi-a*, *akikimbia*.
3. *Alikuja akikimbia.*

And "walking along the road, she saw a snake".

1. Background: *a-ki-tembe-a njiani*.
2. Main event: *aliona nyoka*.
3. *Akitembea njiani, aliona nyoka.* Thompson and Schleicher give a sentence of exactly this shape.

## Where it applies

- **"If" or "when" + future**: *Ukija kesho, utamwona.* *Mvua ikinyesha, hatutakwenda.*
- **"If" + command or request**: *Ukimwona, mwambie aje.* *Ukichoka, pumzika* "if you get tired, rest".
- **"Whenever" + habitual or present**: *Nikichoka, hupumzika.*
- **Background "while" for the subject**: *Alikuja akikimbia.* *Akitembea njiani, aliona nyoka.*
- **"Doing" after verbs of seeing, hearing, finding**: *Tuliwaona wakicheza*; Polomé's *tulimsikia akisema maneno haya* "we heard him saying these words"; *nilimkuta akilala* "I found her sleeping".
- **Several background actions in a row**: wpgrammar's soldiers "came running, both of them carrying a machete" uses two *-ki-* verbs.
- **In compound forms with *kuwa***: *alikuwa akisoma* "he was reading" (lesson 4.16).
- **With *kama*, *ikiwa* "if"** for emphasis (wpgrammar): *Kama ukija...*, *Ikiwa ukija...*. *Ikiwa* is itself *-ki-* on the verb "be", class 9: "if it is".
- **With object prefixes**: *ukimwona*, *akiendesha gari*, *ukikisoma*.
- **Negative conditions** with *-sipo-* or *kama* + negative.

## Watch out

**No *ku-* after *-ki-*.** *\*Ukikuja* is wrong: *Ukija.* (*-ki-* can carry the stress.)

**Do not use *-ki-* with a past result for an unreal "if".** "If you had come, you would have seen him" is not *\*Ukija, ulimwona*. Unreal conditions need *-nge-* and *-ngali-* (part 6).

**Do not put "if" in the result.** *\*Ukija kesho, ukimwona mwalimu* has two conditions and no result. The second verb needs a tense: *utamwona*.

**Do not use the negative present after *-ki-* for "if not".** *\*Ukihuji* is not a word. Use *usipokuja*, or *kama huji*.

**Do not use *-ki-* as a main verb.** *\*Ninakwenda. Nikisoma.* means nothing alone: the *-ki-* form needs another verb to depend on. "I am reading" is *Ninasoma.*

**English "-ing" is three different things.** "Swimming is easy" is an infinitive (*kuogelea*, lesson 4.12). "I am swimming" is *-na-*. "I saw her swimming" is *-ki-* (*nilimwona akiogelea*). Thompson and Schleicher stress telling these apart.

**Keep the subject straight.** In *Tuliwaona wakicheza*, *wa-* agrees with "them", the people playing. *\*Tuliwaona tukicheza* means "we saw them while we were playing".

## Connections

Spanish has a close match in the gerund: *llegó corriendo* "he came running" is *alikuja akikimbia*. Japanese uses ながら for "while" and たら for "if"; Korean uses -(으)면서 and -(으)면. Swahili *-ki-* covers both. The idea "background against which something happens" is the same one that drives the Spanish imperfect in narratives.

## Exercises

1. Say "if you (several) come, we'll eat together".
2. Say "if the bus arrives, call me" (*-pigia simu*: say *nipigie simu*, preview of part 7).
3. Say "we heard them singing".
4. Say "she went to school carrying her books".
5. Translate *Akiendesha gari, dereva aligonga mti.*
6. Say "if you don't come, I won't wait" in two ways.
7. Correct *\*Akikula, atashiba.* Is anything wrong?
8. Correct *\*Ukikunywa maji haya, utaugua.*

## Worked answers

1. *Mkija, tutakula pamoja.* 2PL *m-* + *-ki-*; result in the future.
2. *Basi likifika, nipigie simu.* Class 5 *li-* + *-ki-*; the result is a command.
3. *Tuliwasikia wakiimba.* The *-ki-* verb agrees with "them".
4. *Alikwenda shuleni akibeba vitabu vyake.*
5. "While driving the car, the driver hit a tree." The *-ki-* clause is background; *aligonga* is the event.
6. *Usipokuja, sitakusubiri.* *Kama huji, sitakusubiri.* The first uses *-sipo-* (preview of part 6), the second *kama* + negative present.
7. Nothing is wrong: "if he eats, he'll be full". *Akila* correctly has no *ku-*.
8. No *ku-* after *-ki-*: *Ukinywa maji haya, utaugua* "if you drink this water, you'll fall ill".
