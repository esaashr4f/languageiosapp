## The idea

Part 4 has given you a dozen ways to fill the tense slot. This lesson teaches nothing new. It shows how to choose among them, because English tenses do not map one to one onto Swahili ones, and the choice is where most mistakes happen.

Polomé sorts the Swahili markers along two lines, and they are the clearest way to think about the choice. Some markers place an action in **time** relative to now: *-li-* before, *-na-* at the moment, *-ta-* after. Others describe the **kind of action**, whatever its time: *-me-* complete with its result in force, *-ki-* in progress alongside something else, *-ka-* following on from the last event, *hu-* recurring as a rule. A single verb can carry only one of these (with the exception of *-sha-*, lesson 4.17). When you need both a time and a kind, you use two verbs, with *kuwa* (lesson 4.16).

So ask the questions in this order.

**1. Is the action stated, or wanted?** If someone wants, asks for, proposes or allows it, use the subjunctive (*-e*, no tense): *uje*, *twende*, *lazima tuondoke*, *usiende* (lessons 4.10, 4.11). Everything below is for statements.

**2. Does it depend on another verb in the sentence?** If it is the background, the circumstance or the condition of another action, use *-ki-*: *akitembea njiani, aliona nyoka*; *ukija, utamwona* (lesson 4.14). If it is the next event in a past sequence, use *-ka-*: *alifika, akala* (lesson 4.15).

**3. Is it a rule, a custom, a proverb?** Use *hu-*, with no subject prefix: *Ng'ombe hula majani* (lesson 4.13). In conversation, *-na-* often does the same job.

**4. Does a completed action matter now, or is it a state?** Use *-me-*: *Nimepika* "I've cooked (food's ready)", *Nimechoka* "I'm tired" (lessons 4.5, 4.6). Add *-sha-* for "already" (lesson 4.17).

**5. Otherwise, place it in time.** Now or in general: *-na-*. A past event: *-li-*. Later: *-ta-*.

**6. Does it need a time *and* a kind?** "Was doing", "had done", "will be doing": *kuwa* in the right time + the main verb with *-ki-*, *-na-* or *-me-* (lesson 4.16).

Each choice has its own negative, and the negatives do not always mirror the positives. The reference table below sets them side by side. The two that catch people out: *-me-* is negated by "not yet" *-ja-* (or by the plain past *-ku-* when the chance is gone), and *hu-*, *-ki-* and *-ka-* have no negatives of their own.

## How it is built

Build "When I arrived home, the children had already eaten, so I cooked and we ate together" (without the "when", which needs part 6: "I arrived home; the children had already eaten...").

1. "I arrived home": a past event, the start of a story: *nilifika nyumbani*.
2. "The children had already eaten": completed before that past moment, with "already". Time (past) and kind (complete) together: two verbs. *Walikuwa* + *-me-sha-* + *ku-* + *-l-* + *-a*: *walikuwa wameshakula*.
3. "So I cooked": the next event: *-ka-*: *nikapika*.
4. "And we ate together": the next event, new subject: *tukala pamoja*.
5. *Nilifika nyumbani; watoto walikuwa wameshakula. Nikapika, tukala pamoja.*

Now build one verb slot by slot: "they hadn't yet repaired it (the bicycle)".

1. A past state of not-yet-done: *kuwa* in the past + *-ja-*: *walikuwa* + negative main verb.
2. Main verb slots: negative prefix *ha-* (1), subject *wa-* (2), tense *-ja-* (4), object *-i-* for *baiskeli*, class 9 (6), root *-rekebish-* (7), final *-a* (9).
3. *Walikuwa hawajairekebisha.*

## Where it applies

The questions above apply to every finite verb. Some common English patterns and their Swahili choices:

- **"I'm reading" (now)**: *-na-*. **"I read (every day)"**: *-na-*, or *hu-* for a rule or custom.
- **"I read it (yesterday)"**: *-li-*. **"I've read it"**: *-me-*. **"I've already read it"**: *-me-sha-*.
- **"I haven't read it yet"**: *-ja-*. **"I didn't read it"**: *-ku-*.
- **"I'll read it"**: *-ta-*. **"I'm going to read it"**: also *-ta-*.
- **"I was reading"**: *nilikuwa nikisoma*. **"I used to read"**: the same, or *zamani nilisoma*. **"I had read"**: *nilikuwa nimesoma*.
- **"I'll be reading"**: *nitakuwa nikisoma*. **"I'll have read"**: *nitakuwa nimesoma*.
- **"I'm tired, asleep, full, late"**: *-me-* of a change-of-state verb. **"I was tired"**: *nilikuwa nimechoka*.
- **"I read, then ate, then slept"** (a story): *-li-*, *-ka-*, *-ka-*.
- **"If / when you come..."**: *-ki-*. **"Reading, he...", "I saw him reading"**: *-ki-*.
- **"Read!", "please read", "let's read", "I want you to read", "you must read", "don't read"**: imperative or subjunctive.
- **"I'm still reading"**: *bado* + *-na-*. **"I'm still a student"**: *-ngali*.
- **"Reading is good"**: infinitive (lesson 4.12).

## Watch out

**Do not copy English tense names.** English "present perfect" is not always *-me-*. "I have lived here for years" describes a situation still going on, not a completed action: *\*Nimeishi hapa* misses that. Use the compound of lesson 4.16, *nimekuwa nikiishi hapa* "I have been living here". Ask whether a completed action matters now.

**Not *\*Nilichoka* for "I was tired".** That is "I got tired". A past state is *nilikuwa nimechoka* (lesson 4.6).

**Not *\*Jana ninakwenda* for "yesterday I went".** *-na-* does not move into the past (lesson 4.2): *Jana nilikwenda.*

**Not *\*Nilifika, nilikula, nililala* as a story chain.** It is grammatical but reads as a list. Tell it with *-ka-*: *Nilifika, nikala, nikalala.*

**Not *\*Ninataka unakuja.*** A wanted action is a subjunctive: *Ninataka uje.*

**Not *\*Sijasoma jana.*** A closed past chance takes *-ku-*: *Sikusoma jana* (lesson 4.5).

**Not *\*Mimi hupika sasa.*** *Hu-* is for rules, not one occasion: *Ninapika sasa.*

**Not *\*Ukija jana...*** *-ki-* sets real conditions, usually about the future. An unreal past condition needs *-ngali-* (part 6).

## Connections

Polomé's split between time markers and aspect markers is the same one that separates Spanish *leí* (time) from *leía* (aspect), and English "read" from "was reading". Swahili keeps the two apart more strictly: one slot, one choice, and *kuwa* when you need both. Japanese makes a similar split between た (past) and ている (ongoing or resultant state), which matches *-me-* with change-of-state verbs closely.

## Exercises

1. Choose and build: "Listen! The baby is crying."
2. Choose and build: "Cows give milk." (*-toa maziwa*)
3. Choose and build: "Have you brushed your teeth? Not yet."
4. Tell as a story: "I got up, boiled water, made tea and went out."
5. Choose and build: "If the bus is late, we'll take a motorbike."
6. Choose and build: "This time tomorrow we'll be travelling."
7. Correct *\*Nilikuwa nilisafiri.*
8. Correct *\*Jana nimekutana na rais.* (meant: "yesterday I met the president")
9. Build slot by slot, naming each slot: "they will not give it (a book) to us".
10. Explain why *Nimechoka* and *Nilichoka* are both correct but mean different things.

## Worked answers

1. *Sikiliza! Mtoto analia.* A command (no object), then an action happening now: *-na-*.
2. *Ng'ombe hutoa maziwa.* A general truth: *hu-*.
3. *Umepiga mswaki? Bado* (or *Sijapiga bado*). Done with present relevance: *-me-*; "not yet": *-ja-*.
4. *Niliamka, nikachemsha maji, nikapika chai, nikatoka.* One *-li-*, then *-ka-*.
5. *Basi likichelewa, tutapanda pikipiki.* Condition *-ki-*, result *-ta-*.
6. *Kesho saa hii tutakuwa tukisafiri.* Future time + ongoing action: *kuwa* + *-ki-*.
7. Only *kuwa* carries the past: *Nilikuwa nikisafiri* "I was travelling" or *Nilikuwa nimesafiri* "I had travelled". Or the plain past *Nilisafiri*.
8. A dated past event: *-li-*. *Jana nilikutana na rais.*
9. *Ha-* negative (1), *-wa-* "they" (2), *-ta-* future (4), *-tu-* "us" as receiver (6), *-p-* "give" (7), *-a* (9): *Hawatatupa kitabu.* With one object slot, the person takes it and *kitabu* stays a noun (lesson 4.8). No *ku-*: the object prefix carries the stress.
10. *-choka* means "get tired". *Nimechoka* uses *-me-*: the change is complete and its result holds now, "I'm tired". *Nilichoka* uses *-li-*: the change happened in the past, "I got tired". Lesson 4.6.
