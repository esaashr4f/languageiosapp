## The idea

A **conjunction** is a word that joins two words, phrases or clauses: "and", "or", "but", "because". A **clause** is a group of words built round one verb: "I came" is a clause, "I came because it rained" is two.

You know most of the Swahili ones: *na* "and", *au* "or" (lesson 2.4), *lakini* "but" (lesson 2.1), *pia* "also", *tena* "again, moreover", *halafu* and *kisha* "then" (lesson 4.15). Thompson and Schleicher (p. 299) note that they are invariable: no class, no person, no tense. Many are Arabic: *lakini* (*lākin*), *bali* (*bal*), *ila* (*ʔillā*), *sababu* (*sabab*), *ama* (*ʔam*), *halafu* (*ḵalf*) (Wiktionary).

The interesting one is the commonest. *Na* is not really "and". It is "with": the same word as in *nina* "I have", literally "I am with" (lesson 2.6), and in *nami* "with me" (lesson 3.14). Polomé (§2.5.3) calls it the **associative** particle: it is used "whenever the association of two concepts is expressed", as in *kahawa na sukari* "coffee and sugar", that is, "coffee with sugar". Two things go together; *na* says so.

That explains its limits. Things can be "with" each other; events, in careful style, are not. Wiktionary's usage note says that older and more conservative writing uses *na* only to connect two nouns, never even two adjectives, and that modern colloquial Swahili does not always keep to this. Almasi and colleagues make the practical point that narrative verbs need no *na* at all: the *-ka-* tense (lesson 4.15) already carries "and then". So careful Swahili joins clauses in other ways:

- with *-ka-* for the next event: *Alifika, akaketi* "he arrived and sat down";
- with *tena* "and moreover", *kisha*, *halafu* "and then";
- with nothing but a comma, as lesson 3.14 told you to do for now.

You will hear and read *na* between clauses (*nilikula na nikalala*), especially in speech. Polomé lists *na* among the conjunctions that can link clauses. It is not wrong in conversation; it is not what a careful writer uses.

## How it is built

### Joining nouns: na

1. "Mother and father": *mama na baba*.
2. With pronouns, the bound forms of lesson 3.14: *mimi na wewe* or *mimi nawe* "you and I".
3. In a list, *na* before the last item: *mkate, maziwa na sukari*.
4. The verb agrees with the whole group: two people are class 2 (*Mama na baba wanafanya kazi*). Mixed persons take the "highest": *mimi na wewe* is "we" (*tu-*).

### Joining clauses

Say "I called him, but he didn't come".

1. Clause 1: *Nilimwita*.
2. Contrast: *lakini*.
3. Clause 2: *hakuja*.
4. *Nilimwita, lakini hakuja.*

Say "it rained a lot, so we stayed at home".

1. *Mvua ilinyesha sana.*
2. "So, therefore": *kwa hiyo* "for that (reason)" (*hiyo* is the class 9 *-o* demonstrative, pointing back to the whole first clause).
3. *Tulikaa nyumbani.*
4. *Mvua ilinyesha sana, kwa hiyo tulikaa nyumbani.*

"Because" turns the order round: *Tulikaa nyumbani kwa sababu mvua ilinyesha sana.* *Kwa sababu* is "for the reason" (Wiktionary). With a noun instead of a clause, add *ya*: *kwa sababu ya mvua* "because of the rain" (Almasi and colleagues list it with the compounds of lesson 5.8). Lesson 6.14 adds *kwa kuwa* and the "although" words.

### But, and but rather

*Lakini* is plain "but". *Bali* is "but rather, on the contrary" after a negative: *Si chai, bali kahawa* "not tea but coffee" (Wiktionary: "but instead"; Polomé: "on the contrary"). *Ila* is "except": *wote ila Juma* "everyone except Juma" (Wiktionary, Almasi and colleagues).

### Or, either ... or

*Au* is "or". "Either ... or" doubles it, *au ... au*, or uses *ama ... ama* (Polomé; Almasi and colleagues). The negative "neither ... nor" is *wala ... wala*, taught with the other negatives in lesson 6.15.

## Where it applies

- **na between nouns and pronouns**, in every style: *kahawa na sukari*, *mimi na wewe*, *watoto na wazee*.
- **na "too, as well"**: Polomé's *lete na maziwa pia* "bring milk too" adds an item to an earlier request.
- **na after words of company and nearness** (lesson 5.8): *pamoja na*, *karibu na*; and as "with": *Nilikwenda na rafiki.*
- **kwa joining two nouns as a pair**: Polomé notes *wazee kwa watoto* "old people and children alike" and *wali kwa samaki* "rice with fish". Recognise it.
- **-ka- for the next event** in a past story (lesson 4.15), with or without *kisha*, *halafu*.
- **tena "moreover, and also"** between two clauses or two descriptions.
- **pia "also"** after the word it adds: *Mimi pia* "me too".
- **au, au ... au, ama ... ama** for choices.
- **lakini** for contrast; **bali** after a negative; **ila** "except" (Wiktionary also gives *si ... ila* "not only ... but also").
- **kwa sababu** + clause; **kwa sababu ya** + noun.
- **kwani** "for, because" (Wiktionary; Almasi and colleagues), which also means "why?" at the start of a question.
- **kwa hiyo** "therefore"; Almasi and colleagues also give *kwa hivyo*. *Basi* (the same word as "bus") also means "well then, so" at the start of a sentence: *Basi, twende* "Right, let's go".
- **yaani** "that is, in other words" (Wiktionary, from Arabic *yaʕnī*), also a spoken filler.
- **aidha** "furthermore", a formal written word (Wiktionary).
- **"In addition" and "however" phrases**: *juu ya hayo*, *zaidi ya hayo*, *pamoja na hayo* "on top of that" (Almasi and colleagues; *hayo* points back to the matters just mentioned); *hata hivyo* "however, even so" (Wiktionary). Swahili grammar calls a conjunction *kiunganishi* "joiner" (Thompson and Schleicher; Wiktionary), from *-unga* "join".
- **Clauses with no conjunction at all**: a comma between two clauses is normal: *Nilifika, sikumkuta* "I arrived; I didn't find him".
- **Joining verbs with an infinitive** instead of *na*: *Alianza kusoma* "she began to read" (lesson 4.12).

## Watch out

**Do not use *na* between clauses in careful writing.** *Nilifika nyumbani na nilikula* copies English "and". In writing use *-ka-*: *Nilifika nyumbani, nikala*; or *kisha*: *Nilifika nyumbani, kisha nikala.*

**Do not use *na* between adjectives in formal style.** *Chumba ni kizuri na kikubwa* is heard in modern speech, but Wiktionary's note says conservative writing avoids it. A safe written form: *Chumba ni kizuri, tena ni kikubwa.*

**Do not translate "and" in "go and see" with *na*.** *\*Nenda na uone* is a word-for-word copy. Lesson 4.15: *Nenda ukaone.*

**Do not use the full pronoun after *na* where the bound form is expected.** *Mimi na yeye* is acceptable, but written Swahili prefers *mimi naye* (lesson 3.14).

**Do not put the verb in the singular for a pair.** *\*Mama na baba anafanya kazi* agrees with the last noun only. Two people: *wanafanya*.

**Do not use *lakini* where *bali* is meant.** After a negative that is corrected, *bali* is the precise word: *Si mimi, bali yeye* "not me, him". *Lakini* there sounds like "not me; however, him".

**Do not use *kwa sababu* + noun without *ya*.** *\*Kwa sababu mvua* is incomplete. *Kwa sababu ya mvua* "because of the rain", or a clause: *kwa sababu mvua ilinyesha*.

**Do not confuse *kwani* "because" with *kwani?* "why?".** At the start of a question it asks: *Kwani hukuja?* "Why didn't you come?". Between two statements it explains.

**Do not confuse "or" with "neither ... nor".** After a negative verb, the standard way to deny both is *wala*: lesson 6.15.

## Connections

The idea that "and" is really "with" is familiar from Japanese, where *to* "and, with" joins nouns (*pan to miruku* "bread and milk") while "and" between clauses is a verb form (*tabete, nemashita* "ate and slept"), much as Swahili uses *-ka-*. Korean is the same: *-wa/-gwa* for nouns, *-go* on the verb for clauses. Spanish and French *y*, *et* join anything, which is why English-speakers and Spanish-speakers overuse *na*. Arabic speakers will recognise *lākin*, *bal*, *ʔillā* and *sabab*.

## Exercises

1. Say "the teacher and the students".
2. Say "you and I are friends".
3. Translate *Ninataka kwenda, lakini sina pesa.*
4. Say "not today, but tomorrow".
5. Say "everyone came except the teacher".
6. Say "I was tired, so I slept" with *kwa hiyo*.
7. Say "I slept because I was tired".
8. Rewrite in careful style: *Alifika na alikula na alilala.*
9. Correct *\*Juma na Amina anapika.*
10. Why does Japanese *to* make *na* easy to understand?

## Worked answers

1. *Mwalimu na wanafunzi.* *Na* between nouns.
2. "You and I" is "we": *Mimi nawe ni marafiki* (*marafiki*, the class 6 plural of *rafiki*, lesson 4.19).
3. "I want to go, but I have no money."
4. *Si leo, bali kesho.* A corrected negative takes *bali*.
5. *Wote walikuja ila mwalimu.*
6. *Nilikuwa nimechoka, kwa hiyo nililala.* (Or *Nilichoka, kwa hiyo nililala.*) Lesson 4.16 for the past state.
7. *Nililala kwa sababu nilikuwa nimechoka.*
8. The first event with *-li-*, the rest with *-ka-*: *Alifika, akala, akalala.* Lesson 4.15.
9. Two people take class 2: *Juma na Amina wanapika.*
10. Japanese *to* "and, with" joins nouns; clauses are linked by verb forms. Swahili *na*, in careful style, is the same: nouns with *na*, clauses with *-ka-* or a conjunction. Lesson 5.9.
