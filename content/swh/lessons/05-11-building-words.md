## The idea

A Swahili noun is two pieces: a class prefix and a stem. Lesson 3.1 showed that the prefix carries meaning (people, things, abstracts). This lesson uses that fact to make new words. There are two tools.

**Derivation** makes a noun from a verb. Take the root, replace the final *-a* with a different vowel, and add a class prefix. Polomé (§2.3.1) says that Swahili builds nouns almost only this way, from verbs, and lists the vowels:

- **-i** "the one who does it": *-andika* "write" → *mwandishi* "writer"; *-pika* "cook" → *mpishi* "a cook" (lesson 2.1);
- **-aji** "the one who does it habitually or by trade": *-soma* → *msomaji* "reader"; *-uza* "sell" → *mwuzaji* "seller";
- **-o** "the thing it is done with, or the result": *-funika* "cover" → *kifuniko* "lid"; *-cheza* "play" → *mchezo* "game"; *-imba* "sing" → *wimbo* "song";
- **-u** "the one in that state": *-tulia* "calm down" → *mtulivu* "a calm person", *utulivu* "calm";
- **-e** "the one it is done to": *-kata* "cut" → *mkate* "bread", in Polomé's words "something to be cut"; *-umba* "create" → *kiumbe* "creature".

The class prefix then adds its own meaning: *m-/wa-* a person, *ki-/vi-* a tool or thing, *u-* the abstract idea. One root gives a family: Polomé's *-ongoza* "lead" → *kiongozi* "leader" (lesson 3.9), *uongozi* "leadership".

Polomé also explains *-aji*: it is the agent *-i* added to an old suffix *-ag-* that once meant a repeated or habitual action. That suffix is no longer alive in Swahili, so speakers now feel *-aji* as one piece.

**Class shift** keeps the stem and swaps the prefix:

- **ki-/vi-** makes a thing **small**: *mtoto* → *kitoto* "little child, baby" (Wiktionary), *ndege* → *kidege* "little bird", *nyumba* → *kijumba* "cottage" (Wiktionary derives it from *jumba*, the augmentative, with *ki-*).
- **ji-/ma-** makes it **big**: *mtu* → *jitu* "giant", *nyumba* → *jumba* "mansion", *nyoka* → *joka* "serpent" (Wiktionary; Almasi and colleagues).
- **u-** makes it **abstract**: *mtoto* → *utoto* "childhood" (lesson 3.7).
- **ki-** makes a **language or manner**, **u-** a **country**, **m-/wa-** its **people**: *Kiingereza*, *Uingereza*, *Mwingereza* (lessons 3.2, 3.7).

Polomé gives the history of the size prefixes: the old Bantu class 5 prefix was used to show size, and pairs like *jitu* "giant" and *kijitu* "little person" beside *mtu*, or *toto* "big child" and *kitoto* beside *mtoto*, are its traces.

## How it is built

### Agent nouns and the sound change before -i

Say "writer".

1. Root *andik-* (from *-andika*).
2. The agent ending *-i*. Before *i*, *k* becomes *sh*: *andish-i*. Polomé (§2.2.2.3) describes this: before the derivation suffix *-i*, some consonants turn into fricatives. *k* → *sh* (*-pika* → *mpishi*), *l* → *z* (*-lea* "bring up" → *mlezi* "guardian"), *b* → *z* (*-iba* → *mwizi* "thief", lesson 1.6).
3. Person prefix *m-*, *mw-* before a vowel: *mwandishi*.
4. The abstract: *uandishi* "writing" (Wiktionary).

The *-aji* form needs no sound change: *-soma* → *msomaji*, *-uza* → *mwuzaji*.

### Diminutives

Almasi and colleagues give the rules:

1. Two-syllable or longer stem: replace the prefix with *ki-*/*vi-*: *mtoto* → *kitoto*, *mkono* → *kikono* "little hand".
2. One-syllable stem: add *-ji-* after *ki-*: *mtu* → *kijitu*, *mto* → *kijito* "stream", *mji* → *kijiji* "village" (Wiktionary: "*kiji-* + *mji*"). This is the same *ji* that keeps a one-syllable stem company in *jipya* (lesson 5.1).
3. A noun already in class 7: insert *-ji-*: *kisu* → *kijisu* "little knife", *kikapu* → *kijikapu*.

### Augmentatives

1. Two syllables or more: drop the prefix, keep the bare stem as class 5: *mtoto* → *toto*, *kikapu* → *kapu* "big basket"; plural with *ma-*: *matoto*.
2. One syllable or a vowel stem: add *ji-* or *j-*: *mtu* → *jitu*, *mwiko* "ladle" → *jiko* "big ladle".
3. Class 9 nouns lose the nasal: *nyumba* → *jumba*, *nyoka* → *joka*, *ndege* → *dege*.

### The agreement

Here is the rule that overrides lesson 3.9. A diminutive is class 7/8 and **takes class 7/8 agreement, even for people and animals**; an augmentative is class 5/6 and takes class 5/6 agreement (Almasi and colleagues). So *Kitoto kimelala* "the baby is asleep", not *\*kitoto amelala*; and Polomé's *kuna majitu mengi mjini* "there are many giants in the town" has class 6 *mengi*. (Wiktionary labels *jitu* class 1/2; Polomé's example and Almasi and colleagues' rule say 5/6. This guide follows them.)

### Feelings

Both shifts often add an attitude. Almasi and colleagues: a diminutive can be tender (*kidege* "birdie", which Wiktionary calls "a term of affection") or belittling (*kijiduka* "a wretched little shop"); an augmentative can be impressive or insulting (*jizi* "a hardened thief").

## Where it applies

- **Agent nouns in -i** from many verbs: *mpishi*, *mwandishi*, *mlezi*, *mwizi*, *kiongozi* (a person in class 7: lesson 3.9). Polomé notes that *-i* nouns can also name the whole process with *ma-* (*malezi* "upbringing") or the abstract with *u-* (*ulezi*).
- **-aji** for the habitual or professional doer: *msomaji*, *mwuzaji*; and for things: *kinywaji* "a drink" (lesson 4.4, from *-nywa*, Polomé).
- **-o** for tools: *kifuniko*, *ufunguo* "key" (lesson 3.7, from *-fungua*), *sikio* "ear" (from *-sikia*, Polomé); for results: *mchezo*, *wimbo*, *mwendo* "pace, journey" (from *-enda*), *mpango* "plan" (from *-panga*), *matokeo* "results" (from *-tokea*).
- **-u** for states: *utulivu*, *mtulivu*.
- **-e** for the one affected: *mkate*, *kiumbe*, *mtume* "messenger" (from *-tuma* "send", Polomé).
- **Verb + object compounds**, which Polomé calls the one common kind of compound: *mfanyakazi* "worker" (lesson 2.4: "doer of work"), *kifungua kinywa* "breakfast" (lesson 4.4: "opener of the mouth").
- **Diminutives** with *ki-*/*vi-*: smallness (*kikono*, *kijito*), affection (*kidege*), contempt (*kijiduka*). Some have become words of their own: *kijiji* "village"; *kijiko* "spoon" looks like the diminutive of *mwiko* "ladle" (Almasi and colleagues), though Wiktionary thinks it may come from Turkish *kaşık*.
- **Almasi and colleagues add *ka-*** as a rarer diminutive prefix: *katoto kazuri* "a sweet little child", with *ka-* on the adjective too. Recognise it; do not build it.
- **Augmentatives** with *ji-*/*ma-*: size (*jumba*, *joka*), force or contempt (*jizi*).
- **Collectives** with *ma-*: Almasi and colleagues describe *marafiki* "friends (as a group)", *mamia* "hundreds", *makuta* "walls in general". You met *marafiki* in lesson 4.19.
- **Abstracts in u-** from nouns, adjectives and agent nouns: *utoto*, *uzuri*, *ukubwa* (lesson 3.7), *uongozi*, *uandishi*.
- **Language, country, people**: *Kijerumani*, *Mjerumani*, *Ujerumani* "Germany" (Wiktionary). Country names in *U-* agree in class 9 (lesson 3.7): *Ujerumani iko mbali*.

## Watch out

**Do not give a diminutive person class 1 agreement.** Lesson 3.9 taught *kiongozi mzuri anakuja*, with class 1 agreement for a person in class 7. A diminutive is different: it is a deliberate shift, and it agrees in class 7: *kitoto kizuri kinalala*, not *\*kitoto mzuri analala* (Almasi and colleagues).

**Do not give an augmentative class 1 agreement either.** *Jitu kubwa lilitokea*, not *\*jitu mkubwa alitokea*, following Almasi and colleagues and Polomé's *majitu mengi*.

**Do not confuse the two kinds of class 7 person.** *Kiongozi* "leader" and *kipofu* "blind person" are ordinary class 7 nouns for people and take animate agreement (lesson 3.9); *kitoto*, *kijitu* are diminutives and take class 7 agreement.

**Do not forget the sound change in -i nouns.** *\*Mwandiki* and *\*mpiki* keep the *k*. Before the agent *-i* it becomes *sh*: *mwandishi*, *mpishi*. The *-aji* form keeps the consonant: *msomaji*.

**Do not make every verb into an -aji noun.** Derivation is not fully predictable. *Mpishi*, not *\*mpikaji*, is the usual word for a cook; check a dictionary before coining. Almasi and colleagues say the same of diminutives: "it is not easy to determine which nouns can or cannot be made" diminutive or augmentative.

**Do not read every ki- noun as small.** *Kitabu*, *kiti*, *kisu* are ordinary words, not diminutives. And *kijiko* "spoon" is not "a small spoon".

**Do not shift a noun for a person you mean to respect.** Almasi and colleagues note that diminutives and augmentatives can be derogatory: their *jana* "ill-mannered youth" is the augmentative of the stem in *kijana* "young person". Use the shifted forms about people with care.

**Do not use an augmentative where an adjective will do.** Almasi and colleagues point out that many nouns have no augmentative: for "a big fruit" say *tunda kubwa*.

## Connections

Spanish diminutives and augmentatives (*casita*, *casona*, *perrito*, *perrazo*) carry the same mix of size and feeling, but they keep the noun's gender; Swahili moves the noun into another class, and the agreement follows. Arabic builds nouns from verbal roots too (*kataba* "write" → *kātib* "writer"), with vowel patterns where Swahili uses suffixes; *mwandishi* and *kātib* are parallel products of two different systems.

## Worked answers

1. *Mpishi*: root *pik-* + agent *-i*; *k* becomes *sh* before *i*; person prefix *m-*.
2. *Msomaji*, plural *wasomaji*: *-aji* for the habitual doer, no sound change.
3. *Kijumba* (diminutive, class 7) and *jumba* (augmentative, class 5).
4. A diminutive takes class 7 agreement: *Kidege kimeruka.*
5. Class 5 agreement for the augmentative; short verb *-la* keeps *ku-*: *Jitu linakula.*
6. "This leader's leadership is good." *Uongozi* and *kiongozi* share the root of *-ongoza*.
7. *Kitoto kinacheza.* Diminutives agree in class 7. Lesson 5.11.
8. *Jitu kubwa lilikuja.* Augmentatives agree in class 5.
9. It is a diminutive: *kiji-* + *mji* "town", a small town (Wiktionary). It has become an ordinary word.
10. In *kifuniko* it names the tool that covers; in *mchezo* the result or activity of playing. Polomé's two meanings of *-o*.
