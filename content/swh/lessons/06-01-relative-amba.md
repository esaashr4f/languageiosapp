## The idea

A **relative clause** is a clause that describes a noun: "the teacher **who teaches Swahili**", "the book **which is on the table**". It does the job of an adjective, but it has its own verb.

Part 5 showed how few true adjectives Swahili has, and how much describing it does with *-enye* and *-a*. The relative clause is the third and most powerful tool. Anything a verb can say, a relative clause can attach to a noun.

English marks the relative clause with "who", "which" or "that". Spanish and French use one word for everything, *que*. Swahili does something different again: its "who, which" word **agrees with the noun**. A person takes one form, a book another, a tree a third. By now that should not surprise you. Every word that points at a noun in Swahili carries that noun's class.

The word is built from two pieces. The first is *amba-*, a stem that never changes. The second is the **o of reference**, which you met in lesson 3.14 in *nacho* "with it" and *ninayo* "I have it". This guide uses the name from Wiktionary's verb appendix: the **o of reference** is the short class form ending in *-o* (*-cho*, *-vyo*, *-lo*, *-zo* ...) that points back to a noun already named. That is exactly what "who, which" does: it points back to the noun in front of it.

So *amba-* + *-cho* gives *ambacho* "which" for a class 7 thing, and *amba-* + *-ye* gives *ambaye* "who" for one person. Almasi and colleagues (pp. 295–296) and Wiktionary's table of *amba-* list the same full set.

There is a second way to make relatives, with the o of reference inside the verb itself (*aliyekuja* "who came"). That is lesson 6.2. The *amba-* way comes first because it is the easier one: the verb after it is an ordinary verb, exactly as you have built it since part 4.

## How it is built

### The o of reference, class by class

You already know the pronominal agreement of lesson 3.15: *cha*, *vya*, *la*, *ya*, *za*, *wa*, *kwa*, *pa*, *mwa*. The o of reference takes the consonant part of that agreement and adds *-o*:

- class 7 *cha* → *-cho*; class 8 *vya* → *-vyo*;
- class 5 *la* → *-lo*; class 6 *ya* → *-yo*;
- class 9 *ya* → *-yo*; class 10 *za* → *-zo*;
- class 4 *ya* → *-yo*;
- classes 2, 3, 11 and 14 *wa* → *-o* (the w disappears before o);
- class 15 and class 17 *kwa* → *-ko*; class 16 *pa* → *-po*; class 18 *mwa* → *-mo*.

Class 1 is the one exception. It does not take *-o* but **-ye**, the same piece as in *yeye* "he, she" and *naye* "with him". Treat it as the person form.

### Chain of thought

Say "the teacher who teaches Swahili is my friend".

1. The noun described: *mwalimu*, class 1.
2. "Who" for class 1: *amba-* + *-ye* = *ambaye*. It goes straight after the noun.
3. The relative clause is an ordinary sentence about the teacher: "he teaches Swahili", *anafundisha Kiswahili*. The subject prefix *a-* agrees with *mwalimu* too.
4. The main sentence: "(that teacher) is my friend", *ni rafiki yangu*.
5. *Mwalimu ambaye anafundisha Kiswahili ni rafiki yangu.*

Notice that the noun's class shows up twice inside the relative clause: once on *ambaye*, once on the subject prefix *a-*. Both look back to *mwalimu*.

Now "the tree which fell was tall".

1. *Mti*, class 3.
2. Class 3 o of reference *-o*: *ambao*.
3. "It fell": *ulianguka* (class 3 *u-*).
4. "Was tall": *ulikuwa mrefu*, with the class 3 adjective prefix.
5. *Mti ambao ulianguka ulikuwa mrefu.*

### Any tense, and the negative

The verb after *amba-* is a full verb with its own tense. So *amba-* works with every tense you know: *-na-*, *-li-*, *-ta-*, *-me-*, the negatives, *-ki-* forms with *kuwa*. Almasi and colleagues stress this: "The amba- Relative can be used with any verb and with any tense." The relative inside the verb (lesson 6.2) cannot do this.

To make the relative clause negative, make its verb negative in the usual way. *Ambaye* itself does not change: *mtu ambaye hasomi* "a person who doesn't read", *watu ambao hawakuja* "people who didn't come" (the rule is Almasi and colleagues', pp. 296–297).

## Where it applies

- **Every class.** People (*ambaye*, *ambao*), things in each class (*ambacho*, *ambavyo*, *ambalo*, *ambayo*, *ambazo*), class 11 *ukuta ambao* and class 14 *uhuru ambao*, and an infinitive used as a noun: Almasi and colleagues' *kusoma ambako kulikuwa kuzuri* "the reading which was good".
- **Animate nouns outside classes 1/2** take *ambaye*, *ambao*: *fundi ambaye*, *rafiki ambaye*, *simba ambaye* (lesson 3.9). Almasi and colleagues: *mnyama ambaye*.
- **Pronouns as the noun**: *sisi ambao tulifika*, *wewe ambaye unajua*, and demonstratives used as nouns: *yule ambaye atanunua kompyuta* "that person who will buy a computer" (Almasi and colleagues).
- **Any tense, positive or negative**: *ambaye anasoma*, *ambaye alisoma*, *ambaye atasoma*, *ambaye amesoma*, *ambaye hajasoma*, *ambaye hasomi*.
- **'Have' and place 'be' in the relative**: *nyumba ambayo haina mlango*; *kitabu ambacho kiko mezani*. Lesson 6.5 shows the shorter forms of "who is" and "which has".
- **The relative clause on a subject or an object of the main verb**: *Wanafunzi ambao walifaulu watapata zawadi* (subject); *Ninamjua mwalimu ambaye anafundisha Kiswahili* "I know the teacher who teaches Swahili" (object of the main verb).
- **When the noun is the object inside the relative clause** ("the book which I read"), *amba-* is used too, with an object prefix on the verb: Almasi and colleagues' *mnyama ambaye tumemwona* "an animal which we have seen". Lesson 6.3 teaches this.
- **Place, time and manner nouns**: *ambapo*, *ambako*, *ambamo* exist for the place classes (Wiktionary table). Lesson 6.6 covers "where" and "when".
- **Register.** Both ways of making relatives are standard. Sources disagree on which is more usual in speech (Wiktionary calls the *amba-* form the colloquial norm; Wikipedia's grammar article calls it a newer, urban and media usage). They agree on one practical fact: with *-me-*, the negative past and future and the other tenses outside *-na-*, *-li-*, *-ta-*, *amba-* is the only option (the present negative also has a *-si-* form, lesson 6.4).

## Watch out

**Do not use one form for every noun.** *\*Kitabu ambaye* copies Spanish *que*, which never changes. Class 7 needs *ambacho*: *kitabu ambacho*.

**Do not give class 1 an -o.** *\*Mtu ambao* is class 2. One person is *mtu ambaye*; *ambao* is for *watu*.

**Do not let amba- agree with the wrong noun.** In "the factory which makes shoes", *\*kiwanda ambavyo kinatengeneza viatu* has taken class 8 from *viatu*. *Amba-* looks back to the noun it describes: *kiwanda ambacho*.

**Do not drop the subject prefix inside the relative clause.** *\*Kitabu ambacho iko mezani* has the class 9 prefix. The verb after *amba-* agrees with the same noun as *amba-*: *kiwanda ambacho kinatengeneza*, *kitabu ambacho kiko mezani*.

**Do not use the agreement of the noun's form for a person.** *\*Fundi ambalo alitengeneza gari* takes class 5 from the shape of *fundi*. People take class 1: *fundi ambaye*.

**Do not use ambaye as "who?".** *\*Ambaye amefika?* is not a question. *Amba-* only points back to a noun already said. The question word is *nani* (lesson 5.10): *Nani amefika?*

**Do not leave out the relative word.** *\*Mtu anasoma ni rafiki yangu* is two clauses run together: "a person is reading; (he) is my friend". English can drop "that" in "the book I read"; Swahili needs a relative, either *amba-* or the marker inside the verb (lesson 6.2): *Mtu ambaye anasoma ni rafiki yangu.*

**Do not translate the English relative "that" with kwamba.** *\*Kitabu kwamba kiko mezani* takes the "that" of "I know that ...". *Kwamba* introduces what is said or known (lesson 6.9). The "that" after a noun is a relative: *kitabu ambacho kiko mezani*.

**Do not put -si- into an amba- clause.** *\*Watu ambao wasiokuja* mixes in the negative of lesson 6.4. With *amba-*, negate the verb normally: *watu ambao hawakuja*.

## Connections

Spanish and French *que* is one invariable word; Swahili's *amba-* is closer to Spanish *el cual, la cual, los cuales*, which agree with the noun they point back to. Arabic *alladhī, allatī, alladhīna* also agree, in gender and number. Japanese and Korean put the describing clause before the noun with no relative word at all; Swahili, like English, puts it after.

## Exercises

1. Give the *amba-* form for *vitabu*, *ndoto*, *meli* (plural), *mkoba*, *dawati*, *mafundi*.
2. Say "the tourist who arrived yesterday".
3. Say "the ships which are in the port".
4. Translate *Mwanamuziki ambaye alitunga wimbo huu anaishi Mombasa.*
5. Translate *Mikate ambayo haikuokwa vizuri iko wapi?* (*-okwa* is the passive "be baked", part 7.)
6. Correct *\*Mfanyabiashara ambacho anauza viatu.*
7. Correct *\*Wachezaji ambao wasiokuja.*
8. Say "the doctor who treated me has gone" (*daktari*, class 5 in form, plural *madaktari*).
9. Explain why *ambaye* has -ye but *ambao* has -o.

## Worked answers

1. *Vitabu* class 8: *ambavyo*. *Ndoto* class 9: *ambayo*. *Meli* plural, class 10: *ambazo*. *Mkoba* class 3: *ambao*. *Dawati* class 5: *ambalo*. *Mafundi* are people: *ambao* (lesson 3.9).
2. *Mtalii* class 1: *mtalii ambaye alifika jana*. The verb after *ambaye* takes *a-*.
3. *Meli* plural is class 10: *ambazo*, and the place verb is class 10 too: *meli ambazo ziko bandarini*. (*Bandarini*: the locative -ni of lesson 3.13.)
4. "The musician who composed this song lives in Mombasa." *Ambaye* points to *mwanamuziki*; *alitunga* is the relative clause's past verb; *anaishi* is the main verb.
5. "Where are the loaves which weren't baked properly?" *Mikate* class 4, so *ambayo* and the negative past *haikuokwa* with class 4 *i-*.
6. A trader is a person: *ambaye*, not class 7 *ambacho*. *Mfanyabiashara ambaye anauza viatu* "the trader who sells shoes".
7. With *amba-*, negate the verb normally: *wachezaji ambao hawakuja* "the players who didn't come". The *-si-* form belongs to lesson 6.4 and is not used after *amba-*.
8. A doctor is a person, so class 1 agreement: *Daktari ambaye alinitibu ameondoka.* The object prefix *-ni-* "me" (lesson 4.7) is on *alinitibu*.
9. The o of reference is the pronominal agreement + *-o*; for classes 2, 3, 11 and 14 *wa* + *o* gives *-o*. Class 1 is the person form *-ye*, the same piece as in *yeye* and *naye* (lessons 3.14 and 6.1).
