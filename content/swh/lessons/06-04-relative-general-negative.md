## The idea

The relatives of lesson 6.2 are tied to a time: *aliyekuja* "who came", *anayekuja* "who is coming", *atakayekuja* "who will come". Sometimes you want no time at all: "a dog **that barks**" in general, "animals **that eat** grass", "the people **who sit** here" as a habit. For this Swahili has a relative with **no tense marker**, the **general relative**.

Its shape is unusual. With no tense marker, the slot where the relative marker normally sits is gone, so the o of reference moves to the **end** of the verb, after the final vowel: *a-sema-**ye*** "who says", *wa-kaa-**o*** "who sit, who live". Almasi and colleagues (p. 299) describe it as a present with the *-na-* removed and the relative "attached as a suffix". Wikipedia's grammar article: it is used "when expressing a general meaning or a habit", and gives *Wakaao hapa* "the ones who (usually) sit here".

The general relative belongs above all to proverbs, sayings, titles and careful writing. A famous proverb uses it:

*Mbwa abwekae haumi* "a dog that barks doesn't bite" (Wiktionary). *Abwekae* is *a-bweka-ye*, written without its y, as often happens after *a*.

The second form in this lesson answers a gap left by lesson 6.2: how to say "who does **not**". The **negative relative** puts *-si-* where the tense marker would be, and the relative marker after it: *a-**si-ye**-jua* "who does not know". Like the general relative, it has no tense of its own. Almasi and colleagues (p. 297) give it as the negative of the *-na-* relative (*watoto wanaocheka* → *watoto wasiocheka* "children who don't laugh") and of the general relative (*apandaye* → *asiyepanda*).

You have met this *-si-* + relative before, as a preview. *Usipokuja* "if you don't come" (lesson 4.14) is the negative relative with the class 16 marker *-po-*. Lesson 6.12 returns to it.

## How it is built

### The general relative

Say "a woman who climbs mountains".

1. *Mwanamke*, class 1: subject prefix *a-*.
2. No tense marker.
3. Root and final vowel: *-panda*.
4. Class 1 relative at the end: *-ye*. *A-panda-ye*: *apandaye*.
5. *Mwanamke apandaye milima.* (Almasi and colleagues' example.)

Short verbs take **no** *ku-*: the stress falls on the root. Almasi and colleagues: *wanyama walao* "animals which eat", from *-la*, not *\*wakulao*.

An object prefix goes in its usual place, before the root: Wikipedia's grammar has *nikitafutacho* "which I am looking for", *ni-ki-tafuta-cho*.

### The negative relative

Say "a person who does not know".

1. *Mtu*, class 1: *a-*.
2. Negative *-si-* in the tense slot.
3. Relative marker after it: *-ye-*. *A-si-ye-*.
4. Root: *-jua*. *Asiyejua.*
5. *Mtu asiyejua.*

Short verbs here **keep** *ku-*, because the relative marker cannot carry stress: *wasiokula* "who don't eat" (Almasi and colleagues). Compare *walao*: same verb, no *ku-* in the positive general relative, *ku-* in the negative.

### What time does it mean?

Neither form has a tense. They describe a lasting fact or habit: *mtu asiyejua kusoma* "a person who cannot read". For a negative tied to a particular past or future event, use *amba-* (lesson 6.1): *watu ambao hawakuja* "the people who didn't come", *ambao hawatakuja* "who won't come". Almasi and colleagues say past and future relatives are negated "in only one way", with *amba-*.

## Where it applies

**The general relative:**

- **Proverbs and sayings**: *Mbwa abwekae haumi.* Many Swahili proverbs open with such a form.
- **Habits and lasting facts**: *wanyama walao majani*, *mwanamke apandaye milima*, *wakaao hapa*.
- **Headless, "the one who, those who"**: *wakaao hapa*, *asemaye* "the one who says".
- **With an object prefix**: *nikitafutacho*.
- **Titles and formal writing**: you will meet it in newspapers and in the readings of part 8. In speech, *anayesema* or *ambaye husema* are more usual (Almasi and colleagues treat it as the "indefinite present").
- **Other classes**: Almasi and colleagues: *miti iotayo porini* "trees which grow in the wild", *mahali panukiapo vizuri* "a place which smells good".

**The negative relative:**

- **Negative of the *-na-* relative**: *wanaocheka* → *wasiocheka*.
- **Negative of the general relative**: *apandaye* → *asiyepanda*.
- **Lasting negative qualities, doing the work of an adjective**: *milango isiyofungwa* "unlocked doors", *mtu asiyejua kusoma* "an illiterate person", *maji yasiyo safi* "unclean water" (with "be", lesson 6.5).
- **With an object prefix**: *asiyemjua* "who does not know him".
- **Negative of "be" and "have"**: *asiye* "who is not", *asiye na* "who has no" (lesson 6.5).
- **With place and time markers**: *-sipo-* "where not, when not, if not" (lessons 6.6, 6.12).

## Watch out

**Do not put the relative at the end of a tensed verb.** *\*Alisemaye* mixes the two systems. Either tense + marker in the middle (*aliyesema*) or no tense + marker at the end (*asemaye*).

**Do not add ku in the positive general relative.** *\*Wakulao*: the root carries the stress. *Walao*.

**Do not drop ku in the negative relative.** *\*Wasiola* lacks *ku*: *wasiokula*. The relative marker cannot carry stress.

**Do not put the relative marker at the end in the negative.** *\*Asijuaye* is wrong. The negative relative has the marker in the middle: *asiyejua*. Almasi and colleagues: in the negative the marker "changes from being a suffix to an infix".

**Do not end the negative relative in -i.** *\*Asiyejui* copies the negative present *hajui*. The negative relative keeps *-a*: *asiyejua*.

**Do not use the negative relative for a single past event.** *Mtu asiyekuja* is "a person who doesn't come", a kind of person. "The man who didn't come (yesterday)" is *mtu ambaye hakuja jana*.

**Do not confuse -si- relative with the negative subjunctive.** Both use *-si-* after the subject prefix. *Asiende* (no relative, final *-e*) is "let him not go"; *asiyekwenda* (relative, final *-a*) is "who doesn't go".

**Do not read every -ye ending as a relative.** *Naye* "and he", *yeye* "he" and *ndiye* "it is he" end the same way. The general relative is a verb with a subject prefix and a root before the ending.

**Do not expect -ye written in full every time.** After *a*, writers often drop the y: *abwekae*, *asemae*. Read them as *-ye*.

## Connections

The general relative sits at the end of the verb, like Japanese and Korean relative endings, though those come before the noun. A closer feel comes from English proverbs ("he who hesitates is lost"): a timeless "who" for a type of person. Spanish proverbs use *quien* the same way (*quien mucho abarca poco aprieta*), and Swahili proverbs reach for *-ye* where Spanish reaches for *quien*.

## Exercises

1. Make the general relative of *-sema* for class 1 and class 2.
2. Make the negative relative of *-ja* for class 1 and class 2.
3. Translate *Mwongo asemaye uongo kila siku hapendwi.* (*hapendwi* "is not loved", a passive.)
4. Say "hunters who hunt in the wild".
5. Say "a person who doesn't obey".
6. Correct *\*Watu wasiojui kusoma.*
7. Correct *\*Wanyama wakulao nyama.*
8. Explain the difference between *asiyekuja* and *ambaye hakuja*.
9. Take apart *kisichodumu*.

## Worked answers

1. Subject prefix + root + final vowel + relative: *asemaye*, *wasemao*. Lesson 6.4.
2. *-si-* + relative + *ku-* (short verb): *asiyekuja*, *wasiokuja*.
3. "A liar who tells lies every day is not loved." *Asemaye* is the general relative: a habit.
4. *Wawindaji wawindao porini.* General relative, class 2: *wa-winda-o*.
5. *Mtu asiyetii.* *-tii* is a loan, but the negative relative keeps it as it is.
6. The negative relative keeps final *-a*: *watu wasiojua kusoma*.
7. No *ku* in the positive general relative: *wanyama walao nyama*.
8. *Asiyekuja* is tenseless: "who doesn't come", a kind of person. *Ambaye hakuja* is a past event: "who didn't come". Lessons 6.1 and 6.4.
9. *ki-si-cho-dumu*: class 7 subject *ki-*, negative *-si-*, class 7 relative *-cho-*, root *-dumu* (a loan, no final *-a*). "Which does not last".
