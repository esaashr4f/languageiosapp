## The idea

Lesson 6.12 dealt with conditions that may come true: "if it rains, we'll stay in". This lesson deals with conditions that are imagined: "if I **were** rich, I **would** travel", "if you **had** come, you **would have** seen him". Grammars call these **hypothetical** or **unreal** conditions, and the verb forms that express them the **irrealis**: forms for what is not (or not yet) real.

Swahili has two tense markers for this, both in the tense slot:

- **-nge-** for the imagined present or future: *ningesoma* "(if) I were to study, I would study". Wikipedia's grammar calls it the "present irrealis".
- **-ngali-** for the imagined past, which cannot now happen: *ningalisoma* "(if) I had studied, I would have studied". The "past irrealis".

Almasi and colleagues (pp. 335–337) put the difference plainly. A sentence with *-nge-* describes something that still "has the possibility to occur"; with *-ngali-*, "the possibility of the action occurring has passed and it cannot occur".

What is unusual for an English or Spanish speaker is that **both clauses take the same form**. English uses "were" in one clause and "would" in the other; Spanish uses the imperfect subjunctive and the conditional (*si estudiara, aprobaría*). Swahili uses *-nge-* twice: *Ningesoma vizuri, ningefaulu* "if I studied well, I would pass" (Almasi and colleagues). The first clause is the condition because it comes first. Wikipedia's grammar notes that an if-word, *kama*, *ikiwa* or *endapo*, may be added before the condition "to disambiguate or emphasise", especially when the clauses come in the other order.

The negatives put *-si-* before the marker: *-singe-*, *-singali-*. Almasi and colleagues call this the most common way; Wikipedia's grammar calls it the standard and mentions a southern spoken variant with a negative subject prefix (*hatungekuja*). This guide teaches *-si-*.

## How it is built

### Present or future: -nge-

Say "if I were rich, I would build a house".

1. Condition: "I be rich": *ni-* + *-nge-* + *ku-* + *-wa*. Short verbs keep *ku* after *-nge-*, which cannot carry stress (Almasi and colleagues): *ningekuwa tajiri*.
2. Result: "I would build": *ni-nge-jenga*.
3. *Ningekuwa tajiri, ningejenga nyumba.*

### Past: -ngali-

Say "if I had known, I would have come".

1. Condition: *ni-ngali-jua*: *ningalijua*.
2. Result: *ni-ngali-ku-ja*: *ningalikuja*.
3. *Ningalijua, ningalikuja.*

### Mixed times

Almasi and colleagues allow the two to mix:

- past condition, present result: *Ningalisoma vizuri, ningefaulu* "if I had studied well, I would pass (now)";
- present condition, past result: *Ningekuwa daktari, ningalikutibu* "if I were a doctor, I would have treated you".

### Negatives

*Ni-si-nge-soma*: *nisingesoma* "if I didn't study, I wouldn't study". Wikipedia's grammar: *(Kama) ningalijua hiyo, nisingalikuja hapa* "if I had known that, I wouldn't have come here".

### "Would" alone

*-nge-* is also used without an if-clause, for polite wishes and hypothetical statements. Wikipedia's grammar: *Ningependa kukuona tena* "I would like to see you again". *Ningependa* is the everyday polite "I'd like".

## Where it applies

- **Imagined present or future**: *ningekuwa tajiri*, *ningesoma*. Almasi and colleagues' exercises: "if I were rich, I would marry you", "if we were to travel, we would go to Canada".
- **Imagined past, now impossible**: *ningalijua*, *ningalikuja*.
- **Mixed times**: *-ngali-* + *-nge-*, *-nge-* + *-ngali-* (Almasi and colleagues).
- **Negatives**: *-singe-*, *-singali-*, in either clause or both.
- **With an if-word for emphasis or reversed order**: *kama*, *ikiwa*, *endapo* before the condition (Wikipedia's grammar): *Ningefurahi kama ungekuja* "I'd be glad if you came".
- **Polite wishes and requests**: *Ningependa...* "I'd like...", *Ungeweza kunisaidia?* "could you help me?".
- **Wishes about the past**: *laiti* + *-ngali-*: *Laiti ningalijua!*
- **Reported speech with backshifting** (lesson 6.9): Almasi and colleagues' table turns a reported *-ta-* into *-nge-* and *-ki-* conditions into *-nge-*: *Mtalii alisema kwamba angeandika barua pepe angepata jibu* "the tourist said that if he wrote an email he would get an answer".
- **With object prefixes**: *ningalikutibu*, *nisingemkopesha*.

## Watch out

**Do not use the future in an unreal result.** *\*Ningekuwa tajiri, nitajenga nyumba* mixes an imagined condition with a real future. Keep the irrealis: *ningejenga*.

**Do not use -ki- for an unreal condition.** *\*Nikiwa tajiri, ningejenga* reads as "when I am rich" (lesson 6.12). Use *ningekuwa*.

**Do not drop ku with short verbs.** *\*Ningeja*, *\*ningalila*: *ningekuja*, *ningalikula* (Almasi and colleagues).

**Do not mistake ku the object for ku the short-verb syllable.** In *ningalikutibu*, *-ku-* is "you"; *-tibu* is not a short verb.

**Do not confuse -ngali- the tense with -ngali "still".** *Ningali mtoto* "I'm still a child" (lesson 4.17) has no root after it. *Ningalikuwa mtoto* "if I had been a child" is the past irrealis of *kuwa*.

**Do not put the negative on the subject prefix in the standard.** *Hatungekuja* is a southern spoken form (Wikipedia's grammar). Standard: *tusingekuja*.

**Expect the distinction to blur.** Wikipedia's grammar reports that for many speakers *-nge-* is used for the past too: *Ningejua, nisingekuja* may mean "if I had known, I wouldn't have come". Wiktionary also notes that a third form, *-ngeli-*, is heard but not used in literary Standard Swahili. Use the strict distinction when you write; expect *-nge-* for both when you listen.

**Do not translate English "would" for a past habit with -nge-.** "When I was a child, I would swim every day" is a habit, not a hypothesis: *Nilipokuwa mtoto, nilikuwa nikiogelea kila siku* (lesson 4.16).

## Connections

Arabic *law* "if (unreal)" with *la-* in the result, and *layta* "if only", the source of *laiti*, cover the same ground as *-nge-*, *-ngali-* and *laiti*. French and Spanish need two forms (*si j'étais..., je serais*); Swahili repeats one, rather like colloquial English "if I'd have known, I'd have come", which teachers correct and Swahili makes standard. Japanese *-tara* covers both real and unreal "if", leaving context to decide, as Swahili speakers increasingly do with *-nge-*.

## Exercises

1. Say "if I were a pilot, I would travel the whole world".
2. Say "if you had come, you would have seen him".
3. Say "if they hadn't helped us, we wouldn't have finished".
4. Translate *Ungesoma kwa bidii, ungefaulu.*
5. Translate *Ningalikuwa na hiari, ningalichagua kazi nyingine.*
6. Say politely "I'd like a cup of tea".
7. Correct *\*Ningekuwa na pesa, nitanunua gari.*
8. Correct *\*Ningeja kesho.* ("I would come tomorrow.")
9. Explain the difference between *ningali mgonjwa* and *ningalikuwa mgonjwa*.

## Worked answers

1. *Ningekuwa rubani, ningesafiri dunia nzima.* *-nge-* twice; *ku* in *ningekuwa*. Lesson 6.13.
2. *Ungalikuja, ungalimwona.* Past irrealis with the object prefix *-mw-*.
3. *Wasingalitusaidia, tusingalimaliza.* Negative past irrealis in both clauses.
4. "If you studied hard, you would pass." (Almasi and colleagues' pattern.)
5. "If I had had a choice, I would have chosen another job."
6. *Ningependa kikombe cha chai.*
7. The result must be irrealis too: *Ningekuwa na pesa, ningenunua gari.*
8. Short verbs keep *ku* after *-nge-*: *Ningekuja kesho.*
9. *Ningali mgonjwa* "I am still ill" (the continuative *-ngali*, lesson 4.17). *Ningalikuwa mgonjwa* "if I had been ill / I would have been ill" (past irrealis of *kuwa*).
