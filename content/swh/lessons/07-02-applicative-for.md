## The idea

English adds a participant to a verb with a preposition: "cook **for** the guests", "write **to** my parents", "sleep **on** a mat". Swahili usually does it inside the verb, with the **applicative** extension: *-pikia*, *-andikia*, *-lalia*. The applicative is the suffix *-i-*/*-e-* (*-li-*/*-le-* after a vowel) that lets the verb take one more participant, the one an English preposition would introduce. Learner books call it the "prepositional" form for that reason (Almasi and colleagues, ch. 26; wpgrammar).

Why inside the verb? Swahili has few true prepositions (lesson 5.8), and in a Bantu verb the object prefix slot is the natural home for "who is affected". The applicative turns "for him", "to him", "at it" into an object of the verb, so it can take the object prefix like any other object. Almasi and colleagues put it plainly: when the applicative form is used, "it eliminates the use of the preposition".

This lesson covers three meanings: **for** (someone benefits), **to** (someone or somewhere receives the action), and **at, on, in** (a place). Lesson 7.3 adds reason, instrument and direction, and verbs whose applicative has a fixed meaning.

## How it is built

### Shape

Lesson 7.1 gave the rule. Look at the last vowel of the root:

- a, i, u: *-i-* (*pik-i-a*, *andik-i-a*, *lal-i-a*);
- e, o: *-e-* (*let-e-a*, *som-e-a*);
- vowel-final root: *-li-* or *-le-* (*nunu-li-a*, *ka-li-a*, *poke-le-a*, *zo-le-a*);
- loan verbs in -i, -u: drop the vowel and add *-ia* (*-rudia*, *-jibia*); in -e, *-ea* (*-samehea*) (Almasi and colleagues, pp. 278–280).

### Where the new participant goes

The new participant is an object, and it is usually a person. So it behaves like the person objects of lesson 4.8:

1. it takes the **object prefix** (*a-li-ni-nunu-li-a* "she bought for me");
2. as a noun, it comes **straight after the verb**, before the thing: *alimnunulia mtoto kitabu* "she bought the child a book";
3. the thing (*kitabu*) has no object prefix: the one slot goes to the person.

Almasi and colleagues' example shows all three: *Dada yangu aliwaandikia barua wazazi wetu* "my sister wrote a letter to our parents", with *-wa-* pointing to *wazazi*.

### Chain of thought

Say "I will bring you tea".

1. Verb "bring": *-leta*. Root *let-*, last vowel e.
2. "To you" is a participant English adds with "to" or by word order. Applicative: e takes *-e-*: *-letea*.
3. The person: "you" singular, object prefix *-ku-*.
4. Future, "I": *ni-ta-ku-let-e-a*.
5. The thing, *chai*, comes after: *Nitakuletea chai.*

Say "the clerk opened the door for the guest".

1. "Open": *-fungua*, a vowel-final root *fungu-*: applicative *-fungulia* (Wiktionary "open for").
2. Beneficiary: *mgeni*, a person, so the class 1 object prefix *-m-*.
3. Past, class 1 subject: *a-li-m-fungu-li-a*.
4. Beneficiary first, then the thing: *Karani alimfungulia mgeni mlango.*

## Where it applies

- **For someone (beneficiary)**: *kupikia*, *kununulia*, *kufungulia*, *kulipia*, *kutafutia* "look for on someone's behalf" (Almasi and colleagues, p. 280: *Mabaharia waliitafutia serikali meli* "the sailors searched for the ship on the government's behalf"), *kujibia* "answer for someone".
- **To someone (recipient or addressee)**: *kuandikia*, *kuletea*, *kuuzia*, *kupelekea*, and the *-pigia simu* "phone someone" of part 6 (*-piga simu* + applicative).
- **To somewhere (goal of movement)**: *-endea* "go to" (Wiktionary), and *-kimbilia* "run to" and *-hamia* "move to", learnt whole in parts 4 and 6.
- **At, on, in a place**: *-kalia* "sit on", *-lalia* "sleep on". Almasi and colleagues also give *-ingilia* with *mlango wa kuingilia* "the door for going in".
- **In every tense, the subjunctive and commands**: *nipikie* "cook for me" (applicative + subjunctive, lesson 4.10), *Usinunulie*.
- **In relatives**: *mtu aliyenipikia* "the person who cooked for me"; *mtu niliyempikia* "the person I cooked for" (object relative of lesson 6.3, with *-m-* pointing to the beneficiary).
- **In infinitives after a noun, for a purpose or place**: *mkeka wa kulalia* "a mat to sleep on" (Almasi and colleagues, p. 277). Lesson 7.3 takes this up.
- **With the reflexive -ji-**: *alijinunulia* "she bought for herself" (*-ji-* in the object slot, lesson 4.8).

## Watch out

**Do not keep the English preposition.** *\*Nilinunua kitabu kwa mtoto* is understood but is not how Swahili says "I bought a book for the child". Use the applicative: *Nilimnunulia mtoto kitabu.* (*Kwa* with a person means "at someone's place", lesson 5.8.)

**The object prefix is the person, not the thing.** *\*Alikinunulia mtoto kitabu* gives the prefix to *kitabu*. In an applicative sentence the prefix goes to the beneficiary: *alimnunulia mtoto kitabu*.

**Person before thing.** *\*Alinunulia kitabu mtoto* puts the thing first. The applied person comes straight after the verb: *alimnunulia mtoto kitabu*.

**Only one object prefix.** *\*Alimkinunulia* tries two. Swahili allows one; it goes to the person (lesson 4.8).

**Not every -ia, -ea verb is an applicative.** Almasi and colleagues warn that *-fagia* "sweep" and *-lea* "bring up a child" only look like one. Do not take them apart.

**Harmony again.** *\*Nitakuletia* puts i after e: *nitakuletea*. *\*Alimpikea* puts e after i: *alimpikia*.

**Do not double the extension for two "for"s.** *\*Alininunulilia* is not a word. One applicative adds one participant.

**Do not forget the object prefix with a person.** *Alinunulia mtoto kitabu* (no *-m-*) is heard, but with a specific person the prefix is usual (lesson 4.8): *alimnunulia*.

**Place nouns ending in -ni.** With *-kalia*, the place is a plain object: *amekalia kiti* "he is sitting on the chair". *\*Amekalia kitini* adds the locative a second time. Plain *-kaa* takes the locative: *amekaa kitini*.

## Connections

Korean and Japanese have a "do for" construction too, but as a separate helper verb: Japanese 買ってあげる *katte ageru* "buy for (someone)", Korean 사 주다 *sa juda*. Swahili packs the same idea into one suffix. Spanish comes closer in one way: its indirect object pronoun often marks the beneficiary, *le compré un libro* "I bought him a book", much as *-m-* does in *alimnunulia*.

3. Build "the clerk sold us stamps".
4. Correct *\*Alikiletea mzee kikombe* (meaning "she brought the old man a cup").
5. Translate *Askari analinda kaburi*, then say why there is no applicative.

8. Translate *Utaniletea bahasha?* and name the participant that the applicative brings in.

## Worked answers

1. *Pelek-* has the vowel e, so *-e-*: *-pelekea* (listed in Wiktionary). "I will send you a letter." *-ku-* is "you", the recipient. Lessons 7.1, 7.2.
2. *Utaniandikia?* *u-* "you", *-ta-* future, *-ni-* "me" (the addressee), *andik-i-a*. Lesson 7.2.
3. *Karani alituuzia stempu.* *-uza* has root *uz-* with u, so *-i-*: *-uzia*. *Karani* takes class 1 agreement (*a-*), *-tu-* is "us". Lessons 3.5, 7.2.
4. The object prefix must point to the person: *Alimletea mzee kikombe.* Lesson 7.2, Watch out.
5. "The soldier is guarding the grave." The grave is the plain object of *-linda*; nobody benefits and nothing is moved anywhere, so the plain verb is enough. Lesson 7.2.
6. *Amekalia mwavuli wangu.* *-kaa* is vowel-final: *-kalia* "sit on", with the place as the object and no *-ni*. Lessons 4.5, 7.2.
7. *Pik-* has i, so the applicative is *-i-*: *nilimpikia* "I cooked for him/her". Lesson 7.1.
8. "Will you bring me an envelope?" The applicative brings in "me", the person the envelope is brought to, marked by *-ni-*. Lesson 7.2.
