## The idea

Lesson 7.2 used the applicative for three roles: the person who benefits, the person or place that receives, and the place where something happens. The suffix is the same in every case. What it says is only "one more participant, linked to the action". Which link is meant comes from the verb and the context.

That openness lets the applicative cover three more roles that English gives to prepositions: **reason** ("cry **over** something", "study **for** an exam"), **instrument** ("eat **with** a fork"), and **direction** ("come **for** someone", "run **to** a place"). Almasi and colleagues (ch. 26) list "to, for, on behalf of", "motion towards or away from", and give instrument examples; wpgrammar lists "to", "for", "in", "with" and "from".

The same openness explains the second half of this lesson. When a verb and its applicative are used together for long enough, the applicative can settle on one meaning of its own and become a separate word. *-ambia* "tell" is the applicative of an old verb *-amba* "say" (Wiktionary etymology); *-tumia* "use" is the applicative of *-tuma* "send". These are **lexicalised** applicatives: the form is still an applicative, but the meaning has to be learnt as a word.

## How it is built

### Reason: "for what, over what"

Put the reason in the applied object slot. A question word works too:

1. "What are you crying for?" Verb *-lia* "cry", vowel-final root *li-*: applicative *-lilia* (Wiktionary).
2. The reason is the object: *nini*.
3. *U-na-li-li-a nini?* *Unalilia nini?*

Almasi and colleagues give *Unasomea nini?* "What are you studying for?", where the applicative of *-soma* asks for the purpose of the study.

### Instrument: "with what"

Lesson 5.8 taught *kwa* for tools: *alikata mkate kwa kisu*. That remains the ordinary way in a full clause. The applicative is the other way, and it is especially common in one pattern: a noun + *-a* + an applicative infinitive, "a thing for doing X with":

1. "A knife for cutting with." Noun *kisu* (class 7), so *cha*.
2. Infinitive of the applicative: *-kata* → *-katia* (Wiktionary), *kukatia*.
3. *Kisu cha kukatia.*

Almasi and colleagues use the same pattern for place and purpose: *mkeka wa kulalia* "a mat to sleep on", *mlango wa kuingilia* "the door for going in". They also give applicative instruments in clauses: *Alikulia uma* "he ate with a fork", with *-lia*, the applicative of *-la* "eat" (keeping *ku-*, as short verbs do, lesson 4.4).

### Direction: "to, for, towards"

Verbs of movement take the applicative for the goal: *-endea* "go to" (Wiktionary), *-jia* "come to, come for" (from *-ja*), *-kimbilia* "run to". The person or place you move towards becomes the object: *Nimekujia* "I have come for you, I have come to see you".

### Two objects

An applicative of a verb that already has an object gives a sentence with two: the applied object and the original object. Lesson 7.2 set the rules, which hold for every role:

1. the applied object comes first, the original object second;
2. if the applied object is a person, it takes the object prefix;
3. there is only one object prefix.

So *Mama alimkatia mtoto mkate* "Mother cut the bread for the child": *mtoto* (applied) before *mkate* (original), *-m-* for *mtoto*.

When the applied object is a reason, instrument or place, it is not a person and usually takes no prefix. Then the order is looser; in the sources' examples the thing comes straight after the verb only when it is the single object (*Alikulia uma*).

### Lexicalised applicatives

Wiktionary lists many applicatives whose meaning has moved:

- *-fikia* "reach; stay at (a hotel)": from *-fika* "arrive";
- *-amkia* "greet": from *-amka* "wake up", because the greeting is the first word of the morning (Almasi and colleagues: *Nilimwamkia mzee kwa kusema "shikamoo"* "I greeted the elder by saying shikamoo"; the explanation of the meaning is this guide's, offered as a plausible link, not a sourced etymology);
- *-zungumzia* "discuss, talk about": from *-zungumza* "talk";
- *-tokea* "happen, turn up": from *-toka* "come out" (learnt in part 4);
- *-furahia* "enjoy": from *-furahi* "be happy" (part 4).

Two cases need care. *-shikilia* "hold tight" (from *-shika* "hold") has a doubled suffix, *-ilia*, which Wiktionary analyses as the applicative repeated; it means more effort, not two participants. *-nukia* "smell good" (from *-nuka* "smell, stink") is called an applicative ("prepositional") by Almasi and colleagues but a "conversive" by Wiktionary. The sources disagree on the label; the meaning is not in doubt.

## Where it applies

- **Reason, cause, purpose**: *kulilia* "cry for, cry over", *kusomea* "study for": *Anasomea udaktari* "he is studying to be a doctor" is the kind of sentence Almasi and colleagues' *Unasomea nini?* invites.
- **Instrument, in noun phrases**: *kisu cha kukatia*, *mwiko wa kupakulia* "a spoon for serving" (Almasi and colleagues' exercises use this phrase), *sindano ya kufumia* "a knitting needle" (same source).
- **Instrument in clauses**: *alikulia uma*, *walinywea kikombe* "they drank from a cup" (Almasi and colleagues). *Kwa* is the alternative (lesson 5.8).
- **Place in noun phrases**: *mkeka wa kulalia*, *chumba cha kulia* "dining room" (*-lia* "eat in"). Lesson 3.6's *chumba cha kulala* uses the plain verb.
- **Direction**: *kuendea*, *kujia*, *kukimbilia*, and *-pelekea*, *-letea* of lesson 7.2.
- **Two objects**: any applicative of a transitive verb: *alimkatia mtoto mkate*, *alimnunulia mtoto kitabu*.
- **Lexicalised applicatives**: *-ambia*, *-tumia*, *-fikia*, *-amkia*, *-zungumzia*, *-tokea*, *-furahia*, *-endelea* "continue" (Wiktionary: *-enda* + *-elea*).
- **Even a word for "right"**: Wiktionary derives *kulia* "right (hand side)" from *mkono wa kulia* "the hand for eating with", the applicative infinitive of *-la*.

## Watch out

**Do not use kwa and the applicative together for one role.** *\*Alikulia kwa uma* says "with" twice. Choose one: *alikula kwa uma* or *alikulia uma*.

**Do not read every applicative as "for".** *Alimkatia mtoto mkate* is "for the child"; *kisu cha kukatia* is "with which to cut". The verb and the noun decide. An English speaker's habit of hearing "for" in every *-ia* will mislead with instruments and places.

**Do not put the instrument first when there is a person.** With two applied-looking roles in mind, *\*Alikatia kisu mtoto mkate* tries to fit "for the child" and "with a knife" into one applicative. One applicative adds one participant. Say *Alimkatia mtoto mkate kwa kisu*: the applicative for the child, *kwa* for the knife.

**Do not take lexicalised forms back to their roots for meaning.** *-tumia* is not "send for", and *-fikia* is not only "arrive for". Use the meaning the dictionary gives.

**Do not build -ambia as if from a living verb.** There is no plain *\*kuamba* "to say" in the standard. Wiktionary marks *-ambia* "no plain infinitive"; the root lives on in *amba-* relatives (lesson 6.1) and in *-ambia*.

**Do not split doubled suffixes into two participants.** *-shikilia* is "hold tight", not "hold for someone for someone".

**Short verbs: ku- or not?** *Alikulia uma* keeps *ku-*, because *-lia* is still one syllable plus the final vowel. *Nimekujia*: here *-ku-* is the object "you", not the *ku* of short verbs. Read the slot, not the letters.

**The applicative of -lia "cry" and of -la "eat" are different words.** *-lilia* is "cry for"; *-lia* "eat with" happens to look like plain *-lia* "cry". Context sorts them: *alikulia uma* cannot mean "he cried a fork".

## Connections

French has the same wish to move "with what" and "for whom" into one word, in pronouns: *j'en mange* "I eat some of it", *j'y vais* "I go there". Swahili does it on the verb itself. Spanish speakers will recognise the purpose phrase *kisu cha kukatia* in *una máquina de coser* "a machine for sewing": noun, "of", infinitive. Swahili adds the applicative to make the link explicit.

## Exercises

1. Translate *Mtoto analilia nini?*
2. Build "a needle for sewing" (*-shona*; give the applicative and the whole phrase).
3. Build "the court where (in which) cases are heard" is beyond this lesson; instead build "the farmer cleared the grass for us" (*-fyeka*).
4. Correct *\*Alikulia kwa uma.*
5. Translate *Tutazungumzia kesi hiyo kesho.*
6. Which of these is lexicalised, and what does it mean: *-pikia*, *-amkia*, *-nunulia*?
7. Translate *Alishikilia kamba*, and say why *-shikilia* does not mean "hold for someone".
8. Build "she greeted us" with *-amkia*.

## Worked answers

1. "What is the child crying for?" *-lilia* is the applicative of *-lia* "cry"; the reason, *nini*, is the applied object. Lesson 7.3.
2. *Shon-* has o, so *-e-*: *-shonea*. *Sindano ya kushonea* "a needle for sewing". *Sindano* is class 9, so *ya*. The form *-shonea* follows the rule but is not listed in Wiktionary; *sindano ya kufumia* "a needle for knitting" is attested in Almasi and colleagues. Lessons 7.1, 7.3.
3. *Mkulima alitufyekea nyasi.* *Fyek-* has e, so *-e-*; *-tu-* is the beneficiary; *nyasi*, the original object, comes second. The form *-fyekea* is built by rule (not checked in a dictionary). Lessons 7.2, 7.3.
4. One marker of "with" is enough: *Alikulia uma* or *Alikula kwa uma.* Lessons 5.8, 7.3.
5. "We will discuss that case tomorrow." *-zungumzia* "discuss", with *kesi* as the object; *hiyo* "that one mentioned". Lessons 3.11, 7.3.
6. *-amkia* "greet" is lexicalised: its meaning is not "wake up for". *-pikia* and *-nunulia* are ordinary "cook for", "buy for". Lesson 7.3.
7. "He held on tight to the rope." Wiktionary analyses *-ilia* in *-shikilia* as a doubled applicative that strengthens the meaning rather than adding a person. Lesson 7.3.
8. *Alituamkia.* *a-* "she", *-li-* past, *-tu-* "us", *amk-i-a*. Lesson 7.3.
