## The idea

English can make either object of "give" the subject of a passive: "a book was given to me" and "I was given a book". Swahili is stricter. Almasi and colleagues (p. 282) state the rule: in a plain passive, only the direct object, the thing acted on, can become the subject. To make the person who receives or benefits the subject, you first make that person an object of the verb with the applicative (lesson 7.2), and then make the applicative passive.

So "the parents were written a letter" is built in two steps: *-andika* "write" → *-andikia* "write to" → *-andikiwa* "be written to". Almasi and colleagues' example: *Wazazi wetu waliandikiwa barua na dada yangu* "our parents were written a letter by my sister".

This is the payoff of the applicative. In English, "to" and "for" phrases stay outside the verb. In Swahili they become objects, and objects can be promoted to subject.

The second half of the lesson is the verb "give". *-pa* "give (to someone)" already behaves like an applicative: its object is the person (lesson 4.7). Its passive, *-pewa*, is therefore "be given", with the receiver as subject: *Nilipewa zawadi* "I was given a present".

## How it is built

### Order of the suffixes

The applicative comes first, then the passive, then the final vowel: root + APPL + PASS + FV. Wpgrammar states that in stacked extensions the passive comes last (lesson 7.11 takes stacking further).

1. *andik-a* "write".
2. Applicative: *andik-i-a*.
3. Passive. The applicative ends in i + a, a verb in *-ia*, and lesson 7.4 said most such verbs add plain *-w-*: *andik-i-w-a*.
4. *-andikiwa* "be written to".

The same steps give *-pikiwa* "be cooked for", *-letewa* "have something brought", *-lipiwa* "have (something) paid for" (Wiktionary lists *-lipiwa*), *-pigiwa simu* "be phoned", *-ambiwa* "be told" (Wiktionary: the passive of *-ambia*, itself an old applicative, lesson 7.3).

### Chain of thought

Say "the guests were cooked rice".

1. Who is the subject? The guests, *wageni*, class 2: *wa-*.
2. They are the beneficiaries, so the verb needs the applicative: *-pikia*.
3. They are the subject, so the applicative goes passive: *-pikiwa*.
4. Past: *wa-li-pik-i-w-a*.
5. The thing cooked stays after the verb as an object: *Wageni walipikiwa wali.*

Say "I was told to wait".

1. Subject "I": *ni-*. "Tell" is *-ambia*, whose object is the person told.
2. Passive: *-ambiwa*. Past: *niliambiwa*.
3. What I was told: a command, so the subjunctive (lesson 4.10): *nisubiri*.
4. *Niliambiwa nisubiri.*

### The thing stays as an object

In *wageni walipikiwa wali*, *wali* is still there, after the verb, with no object prefix. A passive with an object left over looks strange to English speakers, but English does the same in "I was given **a book**".

### Short-verb passives

The short verbs have passives that wpgrammar lists one by one:

- *-la* "eat" → *-liwa* "be eaten" (Wiktionary adds "be edible");
- *-nywa* "drink" → *-nywewa* "be drunk";
- *-pa* "give" → *-pewa* "be given".

Each passive is one syllable longer than the plain verb, so, wpgrammar notes, it behaves like an ordinary verb and never takes the *ku-* of short verbs (lesson 4.4): *kimeliwa*, not *\*kimekuliwa*.

### One shape, two readings

For vowel-final roots the rules can give one shape for two verbs. *-nunulia* "buy for" + *-w-* is *-nunuliwa*; so is the plain passive of *-nunua*. The sentence decides: *kitabu kilinunuliwa* "the book was bought"; *mtoto alinunuliwa kitabu* "the child was bought a book", where the leftover object *kitabu* shows that the applicative is at work.

## Where it applies

- **Receivers of giving, sending and bringing**: *nilipewa*, *tuliletewa chai* "we were brought tea", *alipelekewa barua* "a letter was sent to him" (from *-pelekea*, lesson 7.2).
- **Addressees**: *-andikiwa* "be written to", *-ambiwa* "be told", *-pigiwa simu* "be phoned", *-ulizwa* (plain *-uliza* already takes the person, lesson 7.4).
- **Beneficiaries**: *-pikiwa*, *-nunuliwa* + object, *-lipiwa*, *-fanyiwa* "have something done to or for one".
- **Places and things affected by an applicative**: *kiti kilikaliwa* "the chair was sat on" (lesson 7.4's *-kaliwa* is also the passive of *-kalia*).
- **Things paid for, cooked with, used**: *ada ililipiwa*, *kalamu hii inatumiwa* "this pen is used".
- **Short verbs**: *-liwa*, *-nywewa*, *-pewa*, in every tense with no *ku-*.
- **Relatives and infinitives**: *zawadi niliyopewa*; *kupewa* "to be given", *anapenda kuambiwa ukweli* "he likes to be told the truth".

## Watch out

**Do not make the receiver subject of a plain passive.** *\*Wazazi waliandikwa barua* says "the parents were written", as if they were the letter. The person needs the applicative: *waliandikiwa*. Almasi and colleagues set out exactly this contrast.

**Do not make the thing the subject of -pewa.** *\*Zawadi ilipewa kwangu* copies "the present was given to me". *-pa* takes the receiver as object; its passive takes the receiver as subject: *nilipewa zawadi*. To make the present the subject, use another verb, *-tolewa* "be given out" (*zawadi zilitolewa na mwalimu* "the prizes were given out by the teacher").

**Passive last, not first.** *\*Andikwia* puts the passive before the applicative. The order is APPL then PASS: *andikiwa*.

**No ku- on short-verb passives.** *\*Kimekuliwa* and *\*nilikupewa* are wrong: *kimeliwa*, *nilipewa*.

**Do not add an object prefix for the thing.** *\*Wageni walikipikiwa chakula* gives *chakula* a prefix. After the passive the remaining object takes none: *wageni walipikiwa chakula*.

**na for the doer, not for the thing.** In *Wazazi waliandikiwa barua na dada yangu*, *na* goes with the sister. The letter is bare. *\*Waliandikiwa na barua* would make the letter the writer.

**Do not drop the applicative because English has no "to".** "I was told" is *niliambiwa* (from *-ambia*), not a passive of a plain verb "say": *\*nilisemwa* means "I was talked about".

## Connections

Japanese can make the receiver the subject too, and keeps the thing as an object, just as Swahili does: 私は本を与えられた *watashi wa hon o ataerareta* "I was given a book", with *hon o* "book" still marked as an object, like *zawadi* after *nilipewa*. Spanish and French cannot: *\*yo fui dado un libro* is impossible, which is why their speakers reach for the wrong *\*zawadi ilipewa kwangu*.

## Exercises

1. Make the parents the subject: *Dada yangu aliwaandikia wazazi barua.*
2. Translate *Tulipewa posho na serikali.*
3. Build "the child was given a gift by his uncle" (*mjomba*).
4. Correct *\*Mwanafunzi alikupewa hundi.*
5. Translate *Mtoto alinunuliwa nguo*, and explain why *alinunuliwa* here contains an applicative.
6. Build "the patients were cooked food" (*wagonjwa*, *chakula*).
7. Correct *\*Barua ilipewa mimi.*
8. Translate *Wanafunzi watatu walitunukiwa vyeti*, and name the extensions in *walitunukiwa*.

## Worked answers

1. *Wazazi waliandikiwa barua na dada yangu.* The parents need the applicative passive *-andikiwa*; the letter stays as an object; the sister comes back with *na*. Almasi and colleagues' own example. Lesson 7.5.
2. "We were given rations (an allowance) by the government." *-pewa*, passive of *-pa*, with *tu-* as receiver. Lesson 7.5.
3. *Mtoto alipewa zawadi na mjomba wake.* The receiver is the subject of *-pewa*; *zawadi* stays as object; the giver takes *na*. Lessons 7.4, 7.5.
4. *-pewa* takes no *ku-*: *Mwanafunzi alipewa hundi.* Lesson 7.5.
5. "The child was bought clothes." *Nguo* is left over as an object, so the child cannot be what was bought: this is *-nunulia* "buy for" + passive, which has the same shape as the plain passive of *-nunua*. Lesson 7.5.
6. *Wagonjwa walipikiwa chakula.* *wa-* for the patients, *-pikiwa* (applicative + passive), *chakula* as the remaining object. Lesson 7.5.
7. The receiver is the subject of *-pewa*: *Nilipewa barua.* Lesson 7.5.
8. "Three students were awarded certificates." *wa-li-tunuk-i-w-a*: the applicative *-i-* (from *-tunukia* "award to") makes the students objects; the passive *-w-* makes them the subject; *vyeti* stays as the object. Lesson 7.5.
