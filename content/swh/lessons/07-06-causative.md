## The idea

The applicative adds a participant at the receiving end of an action. The **causative** adds one at the start: a new person or thing who makes the action happen. *-cheka* "laugh" has one participant, the person laughing. *-chekesha* "make laugh, amuse" has two: the one who causes the laughter and the one who laughs. *Mchekeshaji aliwachekesha wasikilizaji* "the comedian made the audience laugh" is Almasi and colleagues' example (ch. 25, p. 267).

The causative is the suffix *-ish-* or *-esh-*, with a less common variant *-iz-* or *-ez-*. It follows the vowel harmony of lesson 7.1: i after a, i, u; e after e, o.

What does it mean? Almasi and colleagues: most causatives "express the meaning of 'to make something happen' or 'to cause an action to take place'", while "some convey a somewhat different meaning from the original verb". English translates them in many ways: "make (someone) do", "let", "help to", or one new verb ("teach", "delay", "lend", "feed"). Many of the extended verbs you learnt whole are causatives: *-fundisha* "teach", *-onyesha* "show", *-rudisha* "give back", *-endesha* "drive", *-safisha* "clean", *-fahamisha* "inform", *-kopesha* "lend", *-lisha* "feed".

Where does the suffix come from? Wiktionary's etymology of *-isha* traces it to a Proto-Bantu sequence *-ɪkia*, the stative *-ik-* (lesson 7.9) followed by an old causative *-ya*. The *-iz-*, *-ez-* variant has a related history: wpgrammar explains that the old *-y-* suffix "softened" a consonant before it, turning l into z. Lesson 7.7 shows that softening at work in verbs like *-jaza* "fill".

## How it is built

### Step 1: the shape

Drop the final *-a*, add the causative, put *-a* back:

1. Consonant-final root: *-ish-* after a, i, u; *-esh-* after e, o. *chelew-a* "be late" → *chelew-esh-a* "delay" (the last root vowel is e); *ham-a* "move away" → *ham-ish-a* "transfer, deport" (Wiktionary); *og-a* "bathe" → *og-esh-a* "bathe (someone)" (Almasi and colleagues).
2. Vowel-final root: the l returns, *-lish-* or *-lesh-*: *ka-a* "sit" → *ka-lish-a* "seat"; *va-a* "put on" → *va-lish-a* "dress (someone)"; *onge-a* "speak" → *onge-lesh-a* "make speak" (wpgrammar).
3. Loan verbs drop their final vowel: *-rudi* → *-rudisha*; *-fahamu* → *-fahamisha*; *-furahi* → *-furahisha* "make happy". Loans in *-au* keep it: *-sahaulisha* (wpgrammar).
4. Short verbs are learnt one by one (wpgrammar): *-la* → *-lisha* "feed"; *-nywa* → *-nywesha* "give to drink"; *-fa* "die" → *-fisha* "kill".

### Step 2: -ish- or -iz-?

Most verbs take *-ish-*/*-esh-*, which wpgrammar calls "the most common and productive". Some take *-iz-*/*-ez-*, and a few take both with different meanings (wpgrammar, Wiktionary):

- *-penda* "like": *-pendeza* "please, be attractive" (learnt in part 6) beside *-pendesha* "cause to like";
- *-lipa* "pay": *-lipiza* "take revenge" beside *-lipisha* "charge, make pay";
- *-iga* "imitate": *-igiza* "act, act out" (Wiktionary; Almasi and colleagues: *Tuliigiza mchezo wa Shakespeare* "we acted a Shakespeare play").

Which verbs take *-iz-* cannot be predicted from the sources consulted. Learn them as words.

### Step 3: the sentence

The causative adds a causer as the new subject. The old subject becomes an object, the **causee** (the one made to act). A person causee takes the object prefix, as person objects do (lesson 4.8).

Say "the delay made the passengers late":

1. Plain verb: *wageni walichelewa* "the guests were late".
2. New subject: the cause.
3. Old subject *wageni* becomes the object: class 2 object prefix *-wa-*.
4. *-chelewesha*: *Mvua iliwachelewesha wageni.* "The rain delayed the guests."

If the plain verb already had an object, the causative sentence has two: the causee first, then the original object. *Mwalimu anatusomesha Kiswahili*: *-tu-* "us" (who read), *Kiswahili* (what is read). *Mama aliwalisha watoto uji* "Mother fed the children porridge".

### Causatives in other forms

A causative is an ordinary verb. It takes every tense and the negative (Almasi and colleagues: "can be used with any tense, and are negated in the same way as regular verbs"). It can be made passive with *-w-* after it: *-somesha* → *-someshwa* "be educated" (Almasi and colleagues: *Mwanafunzi alisomeshwa na mwalimu*), *-chelewesha* → *-cheleweshwa* "be delayed" (Wiktionary).

## Where it applies

- **Making someone do something**: *-chekesha*, *-somesha*, *-lisha*, *-nywesha*, *-onyesha* "make see, show".
- **Making something happen to something**: *-chelewesha* "make late", *-furahisha*, *-kasirisha* "make angry" (Wiktionary, from *-kasiri*).
- **Letting or helping**: *-pisha* "let pass, give way" (Almasi and colleagues: *Mlinzi alitupisha kuingia* "the guard let us enter"); *-wezesha* "enable" (Wiktionary).
- **Doing to someone else what you do to yourself**: *-ogesha* "bathe (someone)", *-valisha* "dress (someone)", *-kalisha* "seat (someone)".
- **Moving something or someone**: *-hamisha* "transfer", *-rudisha* "send back", *-simamisha* "stop (something), bring to a halt" (from *-simama* "stand").
- **Lexicalised meanings**: *-somesha* "teach, educate", *-kopesha* "lend" (from *-kopa* "borrow"), *-endesha* "drive, manage", *-pitisha* "approve", *-igiza* "act", *-lipiza* "take revenge".
- **In every tense, the subjunctive, relatives and infinitives**: *usinichekeshe* "don't make me laugh", *mtu aliyetufundisha* "the person who taught us".
- **Passives of causatives**: *-someshwa*, *-cheleweshwa*, *-furahishwa* "be made happy" (Wiktionary).

## Watch out

**Do not use -fanya "make" with a verb, as in English or French.** *\*Alitufanya kucheka* copies "he made us laugh" or *il nous a fait rire*. Swahili uses the causative: *alituchekesha*.

**The causee is an object, with the object prefix.** *\*Mchekeshaji alichekesha* with *wasikilizaji* as subject is wrong. *\*Wasikilizaji walichekesha* means "the audience made (someone) laugh". The laughers are the object: *aliwachekesha wasikilizaji*.

**Harmony.** *\*Anasomisha* puts i after o: *anasomesha*. *\*Alihamesha* puts e after a: *alihamisha*.

**Vowel-final roots need the l.** *\*Alikaisha* is wrong: *alikalisha*.

**Do not build -ish- on every verb.** Some verbs make their causative by a sound change instead (*-amka* → *-amsha*, not *\*-amkisha*; lesson 7.7). And some causatives look regular but mean something special: *-pisha* is "give way", not "make pass" (that is *-pitisha*).

**Do not mix up -iz- and -ish- pairs.** *Alinilipiza* is "he took revenge on me"; *alinilipisha* is "he made me pay, charged me". Choose by meaning.

**A causative passive is not the plain passive.** *Mwanafunzi alisomwa* is nonsense ("the student was read"). "The student was educated" is *alisomeshwa*, causative + passive.

**Short verbs.** *\*Alikulisha* is wrong for "she fed": the causative *-lisha* is long enough to carry the tense, so no *ku-*: *alilisha*, *alimlisha*. (*Alikulisha* with *-ku-* as object means "she fed you".)

**Not every -isha, -esha verb has a living base.** *-fundisha* "teach" is causative in shape, but Almasi and colleagues list it among verbs "causative in their original form". Use it as a word.

## Connections

Japanese *-saseru* is the close match: 笑わせる *warawaseru* "make laugh", from 笑う *warau*, with the causee marked by に or を just as the Swahili causee takes the object prefix. Korean has a closed set of suffix causatives (웃기다 *utgida* "make laugh", from 웃다 *utda*) and a productive phrase *-게 하다* for the rest; Swahili *-ish-* is more productive than the Korean suffixes but, like them, has its share of fixed meanings.

## Exercises

1. Build the causative of *-cheza* "play" and translate *Mama anamchezesha mtoto.*
2. Translate *Habari hiyo ilitukasirisha.*
3. Build "the teacher made us read the story" with *-somesha*, and say why the meaning may come out as "teach".
4. Correct *\*Mvua ilifanya wageni kuchelewa* into the usual Swahili.
5. Why is the causative of *-vaa* *-valisha* and not *\*-vaisha*?
6. Translate *Wasichana waliwaogesha watoto* and *Watoto waliogeshwa na wasichana.*
7. Choose: "he charged me" — *alinilipiza* or *alinilipisha*?
8. Build "don't make me late!" (negative subjunctive of *-chelewesha* with a 1SG object).

## Worked answers

1. *Chez-* has e, so *-esh-*: *-chezesha* (Wiktionary). "Mother is making the child play (playing with the child)." Lessons 7.1, 7.6.
2. "That news made us angry." *-kasirisha* from *-kasiri*, with *-tu-* as the causee. Lesson 7.6.
3. *Mwalimu alitusomesha hadithi.* *-somesha* is lexicalised as "teach, educate" (Wiktionary, wpgrammar), so the sentence also reads "the teacher taught us the story". Lesson 7.6.
4. *Mvua iliwachelewesha wageni.* The causative replaces "make ... be late"; the guests become the object (*-wa-*). Lesson 7.6.
5. *Va-* ends in a vowel, so the lost l returns before the suffix: *-lish-*. Lessons 7.1, 7.6.
6. "The girls bathed the children." "The children were bathed by the girls." The second is the causative *-ogesha* made passive, *-ogeshwa*, with the agent after *na*. Lessons 7.4, 7.6.
7. *Alinilipisha*. *-lipisha* is "make pay, charge"; *-lipiza* is "take revenge". Lesson 7.6.
8. *Usinicheleweshe!* *u-si-* negative subjunctive "you", *-ni-* object "me", *chelew-esh-e* with the subjunctive *-e*. Lessons 4.11, 7.6.
