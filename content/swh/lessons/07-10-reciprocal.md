## The idea

"They love each other" describes two actions at once: A loves B, and B loves A. English needs a phrase, "each other". Swahili uses one more extension, the **reciprocal** *-an-*, a suffix meaning that the participants do the action to one another: *-penda* "love", *-pendana* "love each other". Almasi and colleagues (ch. 27, p. 287) define reciprocal verbs as "doing something to, with or against each other".

Lesson 7.1 previewed it in *wanapendana*. You have met several reciprocals whole: *-kutana* "meet" (from *-kuta* "come upon", Wiktionary), *-shindana* "compete" (from *-shinda* "win"), *-fanana* "resemble", *-lingana* "be equal", *-ungana* "unite". This lesson explains them.

The reciprocal has no harmony: it is *-an-* after every root. Why is it simpler than the applicative? The sources state the fact without a reason, so this guide does not offer one.

Because "each other" needs at least two people, the reciprocal changes how the sentence counts its participants. The two (or more) who act on each other are usually together in the **subject**, which is plural. The object disappears: the subject is both doer and receiver.

## How it is built

### Shape

1. Native verb: drop *-a*, add *-an-*, put *-a* back: *pend-an-a*, *pig-an-a* "fight", *on-an-a* "meet, see each other", *o-an-a* "marry each other" (from *-oa*).
2. Loan verbs and some Bantu verbs take the reciprocal on the **applicative**: *-salimu* "greet" → *-salimiana* "greet each other" (Wiktionary); *-samehe* → *-sameheana* "forgive each other"; *-rudi* → *-rudiana* "get back together"; *-jibu* → *-jibiana* "answer each other" (Almasi and colleagues, p. 289). Wpgrammar gives *-tii* "obey" → *-tiiana* as a direct form, so the rule is a tendency.
3. Short verbs: *-pa* "give" → *-peana*. Almasi and colleagues gloss it "give to each other"; Wiktionary's entry says "give oneself". The sources disagree on the meaning.

### Two ways to name the participants

**Both in the subject** (the usual way): *Juma na Asha wanapendana* "Juma and Asha love each other". The subject prefix is plural, *wa-*.

**One in the subject, one after na**: *Juma anapendana na Asha*, literally "Juma loves-each-other with Asha". Almasi and colleagues: "When a singular Subject Prefix is used it is implied that the object of the sentence is also reciprocating the action." Wpgrammar: a singular subject is possible, "often followed by na and an additional referent". This is where *na* "with" of lesson 3.9 comes back: *nilikutana na rafiki yangu* "I met my friend" is exactly this pattern.

### Chain of thought

Say "the two brothers fought".

1. "Fight" is *-pigana*: the reciprocal of *-piga* "hit". Two people hitting each other.
2. Subject: *ndugu wawili* "two brothers", class 2 agreement: *wa-*.
3. Past: *wa-li-pig-an-a*.
4. *Ndugu wawili walipigana.*

Say "I fought with him" (one person in the subject):

1. Singular subject *ni-*; the other party after *na*: *naye* "with him" (lesson 3.9).
2. *Nilipigana naye.*

### Reciprocal on top of an applicative

When the action is "to" or "for" someone, the applicative comes first and the reciprocal after it: *-andikia* "write to" → *-andikiana* "write to each other, correspond"; *-somea* "read to" → *-someana* "read to each other". Almasi and colleagues point out the difference this makes: *wanaandikiana* "they write to each other", but *wanaandikana* is "they report on each other"; *tulisomeana* "we read to each other", but *tulisomana* "we spied on each other" (pp. 288–289).

### Fixed meanings

As with the other extensions, some reciprocals have drifted:

- *-onana* "meet" (not only "see each other"), from *-ona*;
- *-patana* "agree, get on, be reconciled", from *-pata* "get" (Wiktionary);
- *-kosana* "fall out, quarrel" (Almasi and colleagues: "quarrelling and not wanting to see each other again"), from *-kosa* "miss";
- *-gombana* "quarrel", from *-gomba* "argue with" (Wiktionary).

## Where it applies

- **Two or more people doing something to each other**: *-pendana*, *-pigana*, *-salimiana*, *-saidiana* "help each other", *-elewana*, *-sikilizana* "listen to each other" (all Wiktionary).
- **With a singular subject and na**: *nilikutana na mwalimu*, *alipigana na jirani*, *Juma anapendana na Asha*.
- **With an applicative underneath**: *-andikiana*, *-someana*, *-sameheana*, *-jibiana*.
- **Fixed meanings**: *-onana* "meet", *-patana* "agree", *-kosana* "fall out", *-gombana* "quarrel", *-kutana* "meet", *-shindana* "compete", *-fanana* "resemble".
- **Things as well as people**: *-lingana* "be equal, match", *-ungana* "join together" (part 6): *Rangi hizi zinalingana* "these colours match".
- **Further extensions on top**: causative *-patanisha* "reconcile", *-kutanisha* "bring together"; applicative *-pigania* "fight for" (Wiktionary).
- **Nouns from reciprocals**: *maelewano* "mutual understanding" (from *-elewana*), *mashindano* "competition" (part 6).

## Watch out

**Do not add "each other" as a separate word.** English and Spanish (*se quieren*, *el uno al otro*) mark it outside the verb. *\*Wanapendana wao kwa wao* is not needed. The suffix alone says it.

**No object prefix on a reciprocal.** *\*Wanawapendana* adds *-wa-* "them". The subject is already the receiver: *wanapendana*.

**Do not use the reflexive -ji- for "each other".** *Wanajipenda* means "they love themselves". "Each other" is *wanapendana*.

**Singular subject needs na.** *\*Nilikutana mwalimu* drops the partner's link. Say *nilikutana na mwalimu*.

**Loans usually need the applicative first.** *\*Walisalimana* is not the form; *walisalimiana* is.

**Do not skip the applicative where the meaning needs it.** *Wanaandikana* is "they report on each other", not "they correspond": that is *wanaandikiana* (Almasi and colleagues). Likewise *somana* versus *someana*.

**-pigana is "fight", not only "hit each other".** *Walipigana* will be read as a fight, even a game of punching. And *-pigania* "fight for (a cause)" is its applicative.

**A plural subject alone does not make a reciprocal.** *Watoto walipiga mpira* is "the children kicked the ball", not "kicked each other". The reciprocal needs *-an-*.

## Connections

Spanish and French use the reflexive for "each other" (*se ayudan*, *ils s'aident*), so their speakers may reach for *-ji-*; Swahili keeps the reflexive and the reciprocal apart. Arabic has a closer match: its sixth verb form *tafāʿala* often means "do to each other" (*takātaba* "correspond"), a reciprocal built into the verb's shape just as *-an-* builds it into the Swahili stem.

## Exercises

1. Translate *Watoto hawa wanasaidiana.*
2. Build "Asha and her friend greeted each other".
3. Rebuild exercise 2 with a singular subject: "Asha greeted her friend (and she greeted Asha)".
4. Correct *\*Tulijipenda* if the meaning is "we loved each other".
5. Explain the difference between *Wanaandikana* and *Wanaandikiana*.
6. Translate *Wazee walijaribu kuwapatanisha.*
7. Build "we will meet next week" with *-onana*.
8. Correct *\*Niliwakutana na wageni.*

## Worked answers

1. "These children help each other." *-saidiana*, reciprocal of *-saidia* (lesson 7.10).
2. *Asha na rafiki yake walisalimiana.* Plural subject (*wa-*); the loan *-salimu* takes the reciprocal on its applicative (lesson 7.10).
3. *Asha alisalimiana na rafiki yake.* Singular subject *a-*, the other party after *na* (lesson 7.10).
4. *Tulipendana.* *-ji-* is "ourselves"; "each other" is *-an-* (lessons 4.8, 7.10).
5. *Wanaandikiana* "they write to each other" (applicative, then reciprocal); *wanaandikana* "they report on each other" (Almasi and colleagues). The applicative carries the "to" (lessons 7.2, 7.10).
6. "The elders tried to reconcile them." *-patanisha* is *-pata* + *-an-* + *-ish-*: "make get on with each other" (lessons 7.6, 7.10).
7. *Tutaonana wiki ijayo.* *-onana* "meet"; *wiki ijayo* "next week" (lesson 7.10).
8. A reciprocal takes no object prefix: *Nilikutana na wageni.* (lesson 7.10).
