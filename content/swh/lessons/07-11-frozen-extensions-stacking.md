## The idea

You have used *-fungua* "open" since part 4 as a separate verb. It is *-funga* "close, tie" with a suffix that undoes the action: *fung-u-a*. This suffix, *-u-* (or *-o-* after o), is the **reversive**: an extension meaning "do the opposite of" or "undo". Wiktionary calls it "conversive"; wpgrammar and learner books say "reversive".

Unlike the extensions of lessons 7.2 to 7.10, the reversive is no longer used to make new verbs. Wpgrammar: it "is no longer productive in modern Swahili, but still exists in a number of verbs that are now lexicalized". So you learn the reversive verbs as words; knowing the suffix lets you see the pairs and guess a meaning.

Two other suffixes are frozen even harder: the **static** suffix *-am-* (Wiktionary: "unproductive", "persistence of a state", citing Polomé), found in *-simama* "stand" and *-gandama* "stick, adhere", and the doubling of a whole verb (**reduplication**, lesson 5.7) in a few verbs such as *-sukasuka* "disturb, worry" from *-suka* "shake" (Wiktionary).

The second half of the lesson is about the living extensions combined. A verb can carry two, three, even four suffixes. Their order is not free, and the order changes the meaning.

## How it is built

### The reversive

Shape: drop *-a*, add *-u-* (or *-o-* if the last root vowel is o), put *-a* back (wpgrammar):

- *-funga* "close" → *-fungua* "open";
- *-fumba* "shut (eyes, mouth)" → *-fumbua* "open; reveal, solve" (Wiktionary);
- *-panga* "arrange" → *-pangua* "disarrange";
- *-jenga* "build" → *-jengua* "pull down" (Wiktionary);
- *-kunja* "fold" → *-kunjua* "unfold";
- *-ziba* "block" → *-zibua* "unblock" (Wiktionary: *kizibuo* "corkscrew");
- *-tega* "set (a trap)" → *-tegua* "dismantle a trap; defuse; solve (a puzzle)";
- *-choma* "stab, pierce" → *-chomoa* "pull out" (o after o);
- *-vaa* "put on" → *-vua* "take off (clothes)" (Wiktionary lists it as the conversive of *-vaa*).

Why u? Wiktionary's etymologies give the source: a Proto-Bantu suffix *-ʊda* (*-jengua* from *-jenga* + *-ua*, *-chomoa* from Proto-Bantu *-còmʊda*). The final *-da* contained the l that Swahili later lost between vowels (lesson 7.1). That is why the reversive verbs end in two vowels, and why the l comes back in their own extensions: *-fungulia* "open for", *-funguliwa* "be opened".

### Pairs whose plain verb is lost

Sometimes only the extended verbs survive. Wiktionary derives *-inama* "bend down" and *-inua* "lift" from one root, with the static *-am-* in the first and the reversive in the second; and *-funika* "cover" and *-funua* "uncover, reveal" from one root, with the stative *-ik-* in the first and the reversive in the second. There is no plain *\*-ina* or *\*-funa* with these meanings.

### Stacking: the order

**Stacking** is putting several extensions on one root. The sources' rule and examples:

1. The **passive comes last**. Wpgrammar: "The passive suffix must always be last in Swahili."
2. Other extensions follow the order of meaning: each suffix applies to the verb built so far.

Wpgrammar's chain for *-andika*:

1. *-andika* "write";
2. *-andikia* "write to" (applicative);
3. *-andikiana* "write to each other" (+ reciprocal);
4. *-andikianisha* "make write to each other" (+ causative);
5. *-andikianishwa* "be made to write to each other" (+ passive, last).

Read a stacked verb from the root outward. Each suffix wraps the meaning built so far:

- *-patanisha* = (*pata* "get" + *-an-* "each other") + *-ish-* "make": "make get on with each other", "reconcile" (lesson 7.10);
- *-kumbushia* = (*kumbuka* → *-kumbusha* "remind") + *-i-* "for": "remind on someone's behalf" (Wiktionary lists *-kumbushia* as the applicative of *-kumbusha*);
- *-hamishia* = (*-hamisha* "transfer") + *-i-*: "transfer to" (Wiktionary);
- *-tumikia* "serve" = (*-tuma* → *-tumia* "use" → *-tumika* "be used") + *-i-* (Wiktionary).

### Glossing stacked forms

Each living extension gets its own gloss, in order: *a-li-wa-andik-i-an-ish-a* `C1.SM-PST-C2.OM-write-APPL-RECP-CAUS-FV`. A frozen or sound-change piece is glossed with the root (lesson 7.7): *amsh-i-a* `wake.CAUS-APPL-FV`. The reversive is glossed REV in this lesson; outside it, *-fungua* and its like are glossed whole as vocabulary (`open`), as the dossier sets out.

## Where it applies

- **Undoing a physical action**: *-fungua*, *-fumbua*, *-kunjua*, *-zibua*, *-chomoa*, *-vua*, *-jengua*, *-pangua*.
- **Figurative undoing**: *-fumbua* "solve (a riddle)", *-tegua* "solve a puzzle", *-funua* "reveal". The noun *ufunuo* "revelation" comes from *-funua* (Wiktionary).
- **Further extensions on reversives**: *-fungulia*, *-funguliwa*, *-funguka* (lesson 7.9), *-kunjuka* (Wiktionary, stative of *-kunjua*).
- **Static -am-**: *-simama*, *-gandama*, *-inama*: positions and states that last. Recognise; do not build.
- **Reduplicated verbs**: *-sukasuka* "disturb, worry". Recognise; do not build.
- **Stacking**: any combination the sources list, in the order applicative → reciprocal → causative → passive (wpgrammar's chain), or causative → applicative (*-kumbushia*, *-hamishia*, *-amshia*), stative → applicative (*-tumikia*), reciprocal → causative (*-patanisha*, *-oanisha*, Wiktionary).

## Watch out

**Do not build new reversives.** *\*-pikua* "uncook" or *\*-somua* "unread" are not words. The reversive is closed (wpgrammar). For "undo", use the dictionary verb or a phrase.

**Do not read every -ua verb as a reversive.** *-nunua* "buy", *-chagua* "choose" and *-ua* "kill" have no plain partner: *\*-nuna* does not mean "sell".

**-vua is not "unwear" in every sense.** It is "take off (clothes)".

**Passive last, always.** *\*Andikwiana* or *\*andikiwana* put the passive inside. The passive goes at the end: *-andikianishwa*.

**Order is meaning.** *-patanisha* (reciprocal, then causative) is "make get on with each other". A causative inside a reciprocal would mean "make each other get": a different idea, and not a verb the sources list. Do not reorder suffixes to suit English.

**Stacks are not always natural.** Wpgrammar gives *-likianishwa* "be caused to be edible to each other" as an "implausible example": grammatical in shape, not something anyone says. Use stacks you find in the dictionary.

**Frozen pieces count as part of the root.** In *-simama* do not look for a plain *\*-sima*; in *-amshia*, the *-sh-* is not the causative *-ish-*. Gloss and analyse such forms as wholes.

**The reversive is not the negative.** *Hakufunga mlango* "he did not shut the door" and *alifungua mlango* "he opened the door" are different statements. The reversive is a new action, not the absence of one.

## Connections

English has a living reversive prefix where Swahili has a dead suffix: "do/undo", "fold/unfold", "lock/unlock". Spanish *des-* and French *dé-* do the same job (*hacer/deshacer*, *faire/défaire*). Swahili's *-u-* shows what those prefixes might become if they stopped spreading: a set of fixed pairs, each learnt as a word.

## Exercises

1. Give the reversive of *-funga*, *-kunja* and *-choma*, with their meanings.
2. Translate *Tafadhali zibua chupa hii.*
3. Which of *-nunua*, *-fumbua*, *-chagua*, *-pangua* are reversives? How can you tell?
4. Put the suffixes in order and build the verb: root *-andik-*, causative, passive.
5. Take apart *waliandikianishwa* slot by slot.
6. Correct *\*Tulifungwalia mlango* (meaning "the door was opened for us").
7. Translate *Mzee alisimama, akainua mkono.*
8. Why is *\*-pikua* not a word?

## Worked answers

1. *-fungua* "open", *-kunjua* "unfold", *-chomoa* "pull out" (o after o). All in Wiktionary (lesson 7.11).
2. "Please unstop this bottle." *-zibua* is the reversive of *-ziba* "block" (lesson 7.11).
3. *-fumbua* and *-pangua*: each has a plain partner, *-fumba* "shut" and *-panga* "arrange", and means its reverse. *-nunua* and *-chagua* have no such partner (lesson 7.11).
4. *-andikishwa* "be made to write": causative *-ish-* first, passive *-w-* last (wpgrammar; lesson 7.11).
5. *wa-li-andik-i-an-ish-w-a*: *wa-* class 2 subject, *-li-* past, *andik-* "write", *-i-* applicative "to", *-an-* reciprocal "each other", *-ish-* causative "make", *-w-* passive, *-a*. "They were made to write to each other" (lesson 7.11).
6. *Tulifunguliwa mlango.* Root *fung-*, reversive *-u-* ("open"), applicative *-li-* (vowel-final now, so with l), passive *-w-* last (lessons 7.5, 7.11).
7. "The old man stood up and raised his hand." *-simama* (frozen *-am-*), *-inua* "lift" (lesson 7.11).
8. The reversive is no longer productive (wpgrammar): only the old pairs exist, and *-pika* has none (lesson 7.11).
