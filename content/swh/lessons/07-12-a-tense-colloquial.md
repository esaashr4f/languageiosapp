## The idea

Lesson 4.1's table of tense markers had one gap: a marker that is a bare *-a-*. It makes a present tense: *twasoma* "we read", *asoma* "she reads". Wiktionary calls it the **gnomic** tense, a name for forms that state what is generally or timelessly true. Wpgrammar calls it the "indefinite present" and says that in modern standard Swahili it is "more or less obsolete and rarely used other than its frequent appearance in media headlines", with "no great difference in meaning" from the *-na-* present (citing a 2011 study by Rieger). The two sources describe it differently; both agree you will meet it in reading rather than need it in speech. This guide teaches it for recognition.

The *-a-* tense matters for a second reason. Spoken Swahili has shortcuts that change how the ordinary tenses look, and one of them makes the commonest *-na-* form look like the *-a-* tense. Part 1 promised this: *ni-* + *-na-* is pronounced, and often written, *na-*. To read real Swahili you need to keep the two apart.

This lesson therefore has two halves: the *-a-* tense, and the **colloquial** forms, the shapes used in everyday speech and informal writing that differ from the written standard. The standard is what the guide teaches; the colloquial forms are what you will hear.

## How it is built

### The -a- tense: the prefixes merge

The *-a-* meets the subject prefix and the two vowels merge, just as with the *-a* "of" of lesson 3.11 (wpgrammar: the subject prefixes "are shortened to just a consonant or consonant cluster in a similar manner to the prefix which occur on the genitive preposition -a"). Wiktionary's table for *-soma*:

1. *ni-* + *-a-* → *na-*: *nasoma* "I read";
2. *u-* + *-a-* → *wa-*: *wasoma* "you read";
3. *a-* + *-a-* → *a-*: *asoma* "she reads";
4. *tu-* + *-a-* → *twa-*: *twasoma* "we read";
5. *m-* + *-a-* → *mwa-*: *mwasoma* "you (pl.) read";
6. *wa-* + *-a-* → *wa-*: *wasoma* "they read";
7. other classes: *chasoma* (7), *vyasoma* (8), *yasoma* (9 and 6), *zasoma* (10), *lasoma* (5), *wasoma* (3, 11).

Notice that *wasoma* can mean "you read" or "they read": wpgrammar points out the same of *waandika*.

### Short verbs drop ku

Lesson 4.4 said that ku- stays when the syllable before the root cannot take stress. The *-a-* tense is on Wiktionary's list of pieces that can be stressed, so short verbs drop *ku-*: *nala* "I eat" (wpgrammar), against *ninakula* in the *-na-* present.

### The negative

There is no separate negative. Wpgrammar: "A distinction between these two forms is not made in the negative." *Sisomi* is the negative of both *ninasoma* and *nasoma*.

### Chain of thought: reading a headline

You see *Rais azindua kiwanda*.

1. *azindua*: *a-* + root *zindu-* + *-a*. No *-na-*, no *-li-*. The subject is *rais*, class 1.
2. Class 1 with *-a-* is a single *a-* (step 3 above). So this is the *-a-* tense of *-zindua* "launch, open".
3. "The President opens a factory." English headlines use the present for news too.

### The dropped ni-

Wpgrammar: "In informal Swahili, it is very common for the first person singular concord ni- to collapse into the -na- of the definite present tense marker and become inaudible (and unwritten)." Wiktionary's verb appendix gives the same ("The 1s nina- can be optionally collapsed to na-"). So *ninasoma* is often *nasoma*.

Now *nasoma* has two possible sources: the *-na-* present with ni- dropped, or the *-a-* tense. In speech, assume the first: the *-a-* tense is rare outside headlines and fixed phrases. Short verbs show the difference: the dropped-ni form keeps *ku-* (*nakula* "I'm eating"), the *-a-* tense does not (*nala*) (wpgrammar).

### The habitual -ga

Wpgrammar describes a "non-standard habitual suffix -ga or -nga", which "has entered Swahili from other Bantu languages spoken in East Africa", used in the informal speech of some regions. It goes after the final vowel and means "habitually" or "once in a while": *tunakunywaga* "we drink (as a habit)". Its negative ends in *-gi*: wpgrammar's *Sichezagi* "No, I don't (dance)".

### Other shortcuts

The dossier lists the commonest; each is described by wpgrammar:

- short pronouns *mi*, *we* for *mimi*, *wewe* (wpgrammar also has *wee*);
- *niko* for every kind of "I am (at a place)", where the standard distinguishes *nipo*, *niko*, *nimo* (lesson 2.5);
- regular commands *kuja!*, *enda!* for *njoo*, *nenda*, "particularly in Kenya" and possibly less polite;
- negative future *-to-* for *-ta-*: *hatokuja* for *hatakuja* (lesson 4.3 noted it);
- *ka-* for *a-me-* for some speakers: *kapotea* "he's got lost".

## Where it applies

- **The -a- tense in headlines**: *Rais azindua*, *Bei yaongezeka*, *Mbunge ajiuzulu* "MP resigns" (from *-jiuzulu*, Wiktionary).
- **The -a- tense in older and formal writing and in fixed expressions**, for general truths. Recognise it; use *-na-* or *hu-* yourself.
- **In every class**: *chasoma*, *vyasoma*, *zasoma*, *lasoma*, *yasoma*, *kwasoma* (15 and 17), *pasoma* (16), *mwasoma* (18) (Wiktionary table).
- **Dropped ni-**: everywhere in speech and informal writing: *nataka*, *naenda*, *nakupenda* "I love you".
- **-ga/-nga**: informal, regional (wpgrammar), with *-na-* (*tunakunywaga*) and in the negative (*sichezagi*).
- **Short pronouns, niko, -to-, ka-**: informal speech; label them so if you use them.
- **The standard habitual hu-** (lesson 4.13) stays the normal form in proverbs (wpgrammar: "very commonly used in proverbs dealing with eternal truisms").

## Watch out

**Do not take every na- verb for the -a- tense.** *Nasoma* in a conversation is almost always "I'm reading" (*ninasoma*). Short verbs decide: *nakula* is *-na-*, *nala* is *-a-*.

**Do not write the -a- tense in ordinary prose.** *\*Twaenda sokoni kesho* sounds like a headline or an old text. Say *Tunakwenda sokoni kesho* (or *tutakwenda*).

**Do not add ku- to the -a- tense.** *\*Nakula* as the *-a-* tense is wrong; the *-a-* tense of *-la* is *nala*.

**The negative is shared.** *\*Hatwasomi* does not exist. Both *tunasoma* and *twasoma* are negated *hatusomi*.

**Do not confuse wasoma "you read" (-a- tense) with wanasoma "they are reading".** And *wasoma* alone can be "you read" or "they read": context decides.

**Do not use -ga in formal writing.** Wpgrammar calls it non-standard. In an essay or letter, use *hu-* or *huwa* + *-na-* (wpgrammar: *Huwa tunakunywa*).

**Do not drop other subject prefixes.** Only *ni-* collapses into *-na-*. *\*Nasoma* cannot mean "he reads"; that is *anasoma* (or *asoma*).

**Colloquial forms are not mistakes, but they are marked.** *Kuja!* for *njoo!* may sound abrupt (wpgrammar: "possibly less polite"). Learn the standard first; recognise the rest.

## Connections

English headlines have their own tense rules ("President opens factory"), and so do Spanish ones (*El presidente inaugura una fábrica*). The Swahili *-a-* tense survives in that niche. The dropped *ni-* is like French *je suis* becoming *chuis* in fast speech: everyone does it, it is written in texts and chats, and it is not the standard.

## Exercises

1. Give the *-a-* tense of *-andika* for "we", "you (pl.)" and class 7.
2. Translate the headline *Mbunge ajiuzulu.*
3. Is *nakunywa* the *-a-* tense or the colloquial *-na-* present? How do you know?
4. Give the *-a-* tense of *-la* for "I" and "we".
5. Translate *Wee unacheza ngoma?* and answer it "yes, I do" with *-ga*.
6. Rewrite *Hatokuja* and *Mi niko hapa* in standard written Swahili.
7. Correct *\*Hatwasomi* and say why it is wrong.
8. Translate *Bei ya sukari yapanda*, and say which tense *yapanda* is.

## Worked answers

1. *twaandika*, *mwaandika*, *chaandika* (tu- + -a- → twa-; m- + -a- → mwa-; ki- + -a- → cha-) (lesson 7.12; Wiktionary table pattern).
2. "MP resigns." *a-jiuzulu*: class 1 *a-* merged with *-a-*; the loan has no final *-a* (lesson 7.12).
3. Colloquial *-na-* (*ninakunywa* with ni- dropped). It keeps *ku-*, which the *-a-* tense drops (wpgrammar; lesson 7.12).
4. *nala*, *twala* (no *ku-*) (lesson 7.12).
5. "Do you dance (to drums)?" *Nachezaga.* "Yes, I do (from time to time)." Both from wpgrammar (lesson 7.12).
6. *Hatakuja* (*-ta-*, not *-to-*); *Mimi nipo hapa* (full pronoun; *-po* with *hapa*, lesson 2.5) (lesson 7.12).
7. The *-a-* tense has no negative of its own; both presents are negated *hatusomi* (wpgrammar; lesson 7.12).
8. "Sugar prices rise." *ya-panda*: class 9 *i-* + *-a-* → *ya-*, the *-a-* tense of a headline (lesson 7.12).
