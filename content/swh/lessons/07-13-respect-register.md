## The idea

Korean and Japanese build respect into the verb: a different ending, sometimes a different verb, for a superior. Swahili has no such forms. The dossier's note is plain: "Swahili has no honorific verb forms." Respect is still everywhere in the language, but it is carried by four other means:

1. **greetings** chosen by age and rank (lesson 2.8's *shikamoo* and *marahaba*);
2. **titles and terms of address** (*mzee*, *mama*, *bwana*, *bibi*, *mheshimiwa*);
3. **the choice between forms you already know**: a subjunctive request instead of a bare command, a conditional instead of a plain question;
4. **vocabulary**: a polite word for an unpleasant fact (*-fariki* "pass away" for *-fa* "die", part 4).

The fourth and the choice of grammar together make up **register**: the variety of language suited to a situation, from casual chat to an official letter. Lesson 7.12 showed the casual end; this lesson shows respect and the formal end.

Almasi and colleagues stress how seriously this is taken: greetings are taken "very seriously, especially in the rural areas", and "it is considered impolite to pass by someone without greeting him or her even though the person may be a complete stranger" (p. 4). On commands they are blunter still: "Swahili people never use commands when addressing one's elders but instead they use requests" (p. 315).

## How it is built

### Titles and address

Wiktionary's definitions, which this lesson follows:

- *mzee* "elder, respected old person; title of respect to anyone older than oneself, including parents". *Mzee* alone, or before a name, *Mzee Juma*.
- *mama* "mother"; also a "respectful term of address for an older woman". A stranger in the market can be *mama*.
- *bwana* "master, lord; mister, Mr.; boss; husband", from Arabic *abūnā* "our father". Written *Bw.* before a name.
- *bibi* "lady; Mrs.; grandmother", from Omani Arabic, from Persian. Written *Bi.* "Ms." before a name.
- *mheshimiwa* "honourable, Excellency (title for certain governmental positions)", built from *-heshimiwa*, the passive of *-heshimu* "respect" (lesson 7.4): literally "one who is respected". Plural *waheshimiwa*.
- *mtukufu* "Excellency"; *ndugu* "relative, sibling", also "comrade" in political usage.
- For the dead: *hayati* "the late", placed before the name; *marehemu* "the late", placed after (Wiktionary).

The title comes after a greeting (lesson 2.8): *Shikamoo, mzee*; *Hujambo, bwana?*

### Softening a request

Start from the bare command and move up:

1. *Nipe chai.* "Give me tea." The subjunctive with an object (lesson 4.10): already the normal form, not rude among equals. The bare singular *lete!* "bring!" is blunter.
2. *Tafadhali nipe chai.* Add *tafadhali* "please".
3. *Naomba chai.* "I ask for tea": *-omba* "ask for, request" + the thing. Very common in shops and offices.
4. *Naomba unipe chai.* "I ask that you give me tea": *-omba* + a subjunctive clause (lesson 6.10).
5. *Ungeweza kunipa chai?* "Could you give me tea?": the conditional *-nge-* of lesson 6.13 makes the request hypothetical and so tentative. This guide offers this as a building-block explanation; the sources consulted show *-nge-* for hypotheses, not this use specifically, so treat the politeness value as **unverified**.

To an elder, Almasi and colleagues' rule applies: never a bare command. Use 2 to 4.

### Apologising and softening refusals

- *Samahani* "excuse me, sorry" (part 2) before a request or a refusal.
- *Pole* "sorry (for your trouble)" (lesson 2.8) for sympathy, not apology.
- A refusal is softer as a statement of inability than of unwillingness: *Siwezi* "I can't" rather than *Sitaki* "I don't want to". (This is ordinary pragmatics, not a rule from the sources.)

### The formal written style

Newspapers, official letters, speeches and textbooks share features you have now met:

- the **passive** for actions by authorities (*imetangazwa* "it has been announced"; lesson 7.4);
- **verb-internal relatives** (*aliyeteuliwa* "who was appointed") beside *amba-*; the dossier notes that the sources disagree on which is more literary;
- the **-a- tense** in headlines (lesson 7.12);
- **titles**: *Mheshimiwa Rais*, *Waziri Mkuu*;
- full pronouns (*mimi*, *wewe*), full prefixes (*ninasoma*, never *nasoma* for "I read"), standard *njoo*, *-ta-*.

## Where it applies

- **mzee**: to any older man (and, per Wiktionary, to one's parents); also in the plural *wazee* for elders as a group: *wazee wa kijiji* "the village elders".
- **mama, baba**: *mama* to older women (Wiktionary); *baba* is "father" in Wiktionary's entry, and its use as an address is not stated there.
- **bwana, bibi**: in address (*Asante, bwana*), before names in writing (*Bw.*, *Bi.*), and for "husband", "lady".
- **mheshimiwa, mtukufu**: officials, MPs, ministers.
- **Requests**: subjunctive with or without *tafadhali*; *naomba* + noun; *naomba* + subjunctive; for elders, always one of these.
- **Polite words for hard facts**: *-fariki*, *hayati*, *marehemu*.
- **Formal writing**: passives, titles, the *-a-* tense in headlines, full standard forms.
- **Thanks and congratulations**: *asante* (part 2), *hongera* "congratulations!" (Wiktionary; *pongezi* is the noun, lesson 7.5).

## Watch out

**Do not greet an elder with hujambo or mambo** (lesson 2.8), and do not command one: *\*Kaa!* to an elder is rude. Use a request: *Karibu ukae*, *Tafadhali ukae*.

**Do not look for honorific verbs.** *\*Anakaliwa* is not "he (honoured) sits". Respect goes in the title and the choice of request, not in the verb.

**Do not use bwana to a woman or bibi to a man.** And note that *bibi* also means "grandmother": context decides.

**Do not put hayati after the name or marehemu before it** in careful writing. Wiktionary: *hayati* precedes the name; *marehemu* is used after.

**Do not use colloquial shortcuts in formal writing.** *\*Nasoma* for "I read", *mi*, *-to-*, *-ga* (lesson 7.12) all mark casual speech.

**Plain -fa about a respected person sounds harsh.** Prefer *-fariki* (part 4).

**Do not over-translate English politeness formulas.** "Would you be so kind as to ..." has no fixed Swahili equivalent; *naomba* + subjunctive does the job.

## Connections

Korean and Japanese speakers will find the Swahili system familiar in spirit and different in machinery: age and rank decide the greeting (*shikamoo* is to elders what 안녕하십니까 is to a superior), but the verb itself never changes. Arabic speakers will recognise *marahaba* (*marḥaban*), *heshima* (*ḥišma*) and *adabu* (*ʔadab*): much of the vocabulary of manners came from Arabic.

## Exercises

1. Greet an old man you do not know, and give his answer.
2. Turn *Lete maji!* into three politer requests, in rising order of politeness.
3. Translate *Naomba unieleze njia ya kwenda sokoni.*
4. Correct the register: a pupil says to a teacher *\*Kaa hapa!*
5. Write "Mr Juma" and "Ms Asha" as they would appear in a newspaper.
6. Translate *Hayati mzee wangu alikuwa mkulima.*
7. Rewrite *Mi nasoma gazeti* in formal written Swahili.
8. Why is *mheshimiwa* a sensible word for "honourable"? Take it apart.

## Worked answers

1. *Shikamoo, mzee.* He answers *Marahaba.* (lessons 2.8, 7.13).
2. *Niletee maji* (subjunctive with the applicative, lessons 4.10, 7.2); *Tafadhali niletee maji*; *Naomba uniletee maji* (*-omba* + subjunctive). Lesson 7.13.
3. "Could you please explain to me the way to the market?" *Naomba* + subjunctive *unieleze* (*-eleza* "explain", subjunctive *-e*) (lesson 7.13).
4. A command is rude to an elder or a teacher (Almasi and colleagues). Use an invitation: *Karibu ukae hapa*, *Tafadhali ukae hapa* (lesson 7.13).
5. *Bw. Juma*, *Bi. Asha* (Wiktionary abbreviations; lesson 7.13).
6. "My late father (my late elder) was a farmer." *hayati* before the noun; *mzee wangu* "my elder", often a parent (Wiktionary) (lesson 7.13).
7. *Mimi ninasoma gazeti.* Full pronoun, full *ni-* (lessons 7.12, 7.13).
8. *m-heshim-iw-a*: class 1 *m-* (a person) + *-heshimu* "respect" + passive *-iw-*: "one who is respected". Lessons 7.4, 7.13.
