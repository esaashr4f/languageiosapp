## The idea

Everything in this guide so far has been **Standard Swahili**, *Kiswahili sanifu* (Wiktionary: *sanifu* "standard, proper"). A standard is one variety of a language chosen and fixed for schools, print and government. It is not the only Swahili, and not the oldest.

Swahili has many **dialects** (Swahili *lahaja*): varieties of one language that differ in sounds, words and some grammar, tied to a place or a community. The standard is built on one of them. Older Swahili literature is built on another, and was written in another script. Far from the coast, Swahili spread as a second language and changed again: in Congo, and among the young of Nairobi.

This lesson is a map, not a course. It names the varieties, gives a few forms of each from open sources so that you can recognise them, and shows one stanza of the oldest dated Swahili poem. It teaches no new forms to use.

## How it is built

### The standard: Zanzibar town speech

Polomé (1967, ch. 1, dialect 13) says that the dialect of Unguja (Zanzibar island), *Kiunguja*, "has now been widely accepted as the basis of 'standard' Swahili". Wikipedia's article on the language (wplang) is more precise: modern standard Swahili "is based on Kiunguja, the dialect spoken in Zanzibar City". Glottolog lists Unguja as a dialect of Swahili.

How was it fixed? Polomé credits the **Inter-Territorial Language Committee**, founded in 1930 for the British territories of East Africa, with spreading the Zanzibar-based standard through schoolbooks and dictionaries. Wplang adds the later history: after the union of Tanganyika and Zanzibar in 1964, the Institute of Swahili Research (*Taasisi ya Uchunguzi wa Kiswahili*, TUKI) was created from that committee. Swahili became Tanzania's national language after independence (1961); in Kenya it has been the national language since 1964 and an official language since 2010 (wplang).

One fact you have already met depends on this choice. Lesson 7.4 used wpgrammar's note that the Zanzibar-based standard passivises *-aa* verbs with *-liwa* (*-zaliwa*), where other dialects can say *-zawa*.

### The old literary dialects

Wplang: "Swahili literature and poetry, traditionally written in Swahili Ajami, is based on Kiamu, the dialect of Lamu on the Kenyan coast." The dialects of the Lamu group (Kiamu, Kipate, Kingozi) "are the linguistic base of the oldest (c. 1600 CE) Swahili manuscripts and poems". **Kimvita**, the dialect of Mombasa, is "the other major dialect alongside Kiunguja", also with a classical literature in Arabic script.

### Ajami: Swahili in Arabic script

**Ajami** is the name for African languages written in Arabic letters. Wplang: Swahili "used to be primarily written in the Ajami script", and "much literature was produced in this script"; the Latin alphabet came with Christian missionaries and colonial administrators. The Arabic sounds of lesson 1.4 (dh, th, gh, kh) are traces of that history.

### The utenzi

The **utenzi** (or *utendi*) is the classic narrative verse form. Wikipedia's article on it: four-line stanzas, eight syllables to a line; the first three lines rhyme with each other, and "the fourth line has a rhyme that is constant throughout the whole of the epic". The name comes from *-tenda* "do": a poem of deeds (Wiktionary: *utenzi* "action; narrative poem, usually describing heroic deeds").

The oldest dated work is the *Utendi wa Tambuka* "the epic of Tambuka", by Bwana Mwengo wa Athman, written at Pate in 1728, in Arabic script and in Kiamu (Wikipedia, "Utendi wa Tambuka"). Its first stanza, as romanised there:

- *Bisimillahi kut̠ubu*
- *yina la Mola Wahhabu*
- *Arrah̠amani eribu*
- *na Arrah̠imu ukyowa*

What can be read with this guide's sources:

1. *Bisimillahi* is Arabic *bismi llāh* "in the name of God" (Wiktionary *bismillahi*).
2. *Mola* is "God, the Lord", from Arabic *mawlā* (Wiktionary). *Wahhabu*, *Arrahamani* and *Arrahimu* are Arabic names of God: the Bestower, the Merciful, the Compassionate.
3. *Yina la Mola* is probably "the name of the Lord": *la* is the class 5 "of" of lesson 3.11, which fits standard *jina* "name" (class 5). That *yina* is the Kiamu form of *jina* is this guide's inference from the agreement, not a sourced fact: **uncertain**.
4. *Kut̠ubu*, *eribu* and *ukyowa* could not be checked in the sources opened. They are left unglossed.

The form, at least, is plain: eight syllables to each line (*Bi-si-mil-la-hi ku-tu-bu*), *-bu* rhyming in lines 1 to 3, and the *-a* of line 4, which Wikipedia says ends every stanza of the poem.

### Kenyan and coastal forms in modern Swahili

Wpgrammar (after Gromova 2008) reports Kenyan forms *jengine* and *lengine* for standard *jingine* (class 5) and *zingine* for *nyingine* (class 10), and calls them non-standard. Mombasa *pahali* for *mahali* is, per Wiktionary, *pa-* + the older *mahali*. Lesson 7.12's regular commands *kuja!*, *enda!* are heard "particularly in Kenya" (wpgrammar).

### Congo Swahili

Wplang: Swahili is a national language of the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and "the local dialects of Swahili in Congo are known as Congo Swahili and differ considerably from Standard Swahili". Wikipedia's "Congo Swahili" article distinguishes two main varieties, Kingwana (Maniema, older, from nineteenth-century trade) and Katanga Swahili (younger, from the mining towns), and lists features such as near and remote past tenses, and, in Katanga, the loss of the locative *-ni* (*ku soko* for standard *sokoni*). Wiktionary has a Congo habitual *-aga*, with the example *wanakwendaga* "they often go"; compare the *-ga* of lesson 7.12.

### Sheng

Wikipedia's "Sheng slang" article: Sheng is "a Swahili and English-based cant, slang, perhaps a mixed language or creole", from the urban youth of Nairobi; the name blends **S**wa**h**ili and **Eng**lish, and is "first recorded in 1965". Its grammar is largely Swahili; its vocabulary mixes English, Kenyan languages and others, and changes fast. Wpgrammar's examples of *-ga* and Wiktionary's "(Sheng)" labels (lesson 7.2's *-pepea* has one) are where you will meet it in reference books. This guide teaches no Sheng.

## Where it applies

- **Standard Swahili** (Zanzibar-based): schools, print, broadcasting and government in Tanzania and Kenya; every form in this guide unless labelled.
- **Kiamu and the Lamu dialects**: classical poetry, the *utenzi* tradition, and the *Utendi wa Tambuka*. Recognise; part 8 does not read them.
- **Kimvita** (Mombasa): the other great literary and spoken dialect; *pahali* and some Kenyan coastal forms.
- **Kenyan usage generally**: *jengine*, *zingine*, regular imperatives; Sheng among the young in towns.
- **Congo Swahili**: eastern and southern Democratic Republic of the Congo; Kingwana and Katanga Swahili.
- **Arabic script**: old manuscripts and a living tradition of Ajami writing (wplang: "the language continues to have a tradition of being written in Arabic script").
- **The utenzi form**: four lines, eight syllables, three rhyming lines and a fourth that rhymes through the whole poem.

## Watch out

**Do not call the standard "pure" Swahili and the rest "broken".** The standard is one dialect chosen for a job. Kiamu poetry is older than the standard; Kimvita is a full dialect.

**Do not write dialect forms in standard prose.** *\*Hili ni jambo jengine* in an exam essay will be marked as non-standard (wpgrammar). Write *jingine*.

**Do not assume a word in a dictionary is standard.** Wiktionary marks many senses "(Sheng)", "(Congo)", "(Kenya)"; check the label before using a meaning (*-pepea* has a Sheng sense besides "fan").

**Do not try to read old poems with this guide alone.** The *Utendi wa Tambuka* uses Kiamu forms and Arabic vocabulary that the guide's sources do not cover; even its first stanza has three words this lesson could not check.

**Do not confuse Ajami with Arabic.** Ajami writes Swahili words in Arabic letters; the language is still Swahili.

**Do not take Sheng for Swahili with English words in it.** Its vocabulary changes fast and differs between neighbourhoods (Wikipedia, "Sheng slang"). Recognise it; do not learn it from a book.

## Connections

Standard Swahili's relation to Kiunguja is like standard Italian's to Florentine or standard French's to the speech of Paris: one city's usage made the national norm, while older literary centres kept their own traditions. Ajami has close relatives in Hausa and Wolof Ajami, and in Aljamiado, Spanish written in Arabic letters, which this app treats as a companion to Old Spanish.

## Exercises

1. Which dialect is Standard Swahili based on, and which is the base of classical poetry?
2. Rewrite *Nilinunua kitabu kingine na zingine nyingi* in standard Swahili. (*zingine* stands for class 10 here.)
3. Count the syllables in *yina la Mola Wahhabu* and say what makes it a correct utenzi line.
4. What does the *-ga* of *wanakwendaga* show about where the sentence comes from?
5. Translate *Utenzi una beti nyingi.*
6. Why does this lesson mark *yina* = *jina* as uncertain?
7. Translate *Mshairi huyu aliandika tenzi nyingi.*

## Worked answers

1. Kiunguja, the dialect of Zanzibar City (Polomé; wplang); classical poetry is based on Kiamu, the dialect of Lamu (wplang). Lesson 7.14.
2. *Nilinunua kitabu kingine na nyingine nyingi.* *zingine* is the Kenyan form of class 10 *nyingine* (wpgrammar). (A natural sentence would name the class 10 noun.) Lesson 7.14.
3. *yi-na-la-Mo-la-Wah-ha-bu*: eight syllables, with *-bu* rhyming with lines 1 and 3 (Wikipedia, "Utenzi"). Lessons 1.5, 7.14.
4. *-aga* is the Congo habitual (Wiktionary): the sentence is Congo Swahili, not the standard (lessons 7.12, 7.14).
5. "An utenzi has many stanzas." *beti* is class 10, so *nyingi* (lesson 7.14).
6. The agreement *la* fits a class 5 noun like *jina*, but no source opened says that Kiamu has *y* for standard *j*; the identification is an inference (lesson 7.14).
7. "This poet wrote many epics." *tenzi* is the class 10 plural of *utenzi*, so *nyingi* (lessons 3.7, 7.14).
