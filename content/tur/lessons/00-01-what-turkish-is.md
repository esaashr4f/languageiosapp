## A language that travelled

Turkish is the language of the Republic of Turkey, where it is the official language, and of the Turkish community of northern Cyprus. Its speakers call it *Türkçe* and their country *Türkiye*. There are also old Turkish-speaking communities in the Balkans, in Bulgaria and Greece above all, left behind by the Ottoman Empire, and newer ones in western Europe, about half of them in Germany. A 2005 reference grammar estimated that some 65 million people speak it as a first language or with native fluency. That figure is now old and should be read as an order of size.

Turkish was not born where it is spoken now. It belongs to the **Turkic** family, a group of related languages spoken in a long belt from the Balkans across Central Asia to western China. Uzbek, Kazakh, Tatar and Uyghur are members. Within the family, Turkish sits in the south-western branch, called **Oghuz**. Its closest relatives are Azerbaijani, Turkmen and Gagauz, a language of Orthodox Christians in southern Moldova.

Oghuz Turks settled in Anatolia, the peninsula that is now most of Turkey, between the eleventh and thirteenth centuries. The language they brought with them is the ancestor of modern Turkish. Many of its everyday words go back further still, to **Proto-Turkic**, the reconstructed parent of the whole family: *ev* "house", *göz* "eye", *su* "water" and *iki* "two" all have Proto-Turkic ancestors, and relatives across the family.

## A family of its own

You may read that Turkish is related to Mongolian, Korean and Japanese, in a family called **Altaic**. Glottolog, the standard catalogue of the world's languages, does not accept that family: it lists Turkic as a family with nothing above it. Turkish, Korean and Japanese do share a striking number of features, as you will see in this guide: the verb comes last, small endings do the work of English prepositions, and a description comes before the thing it describes. These are shared habits of structure. They are not proof of a shared ancestor, and this guide never treats them as one.

## Ottoman Turkish

The Ottoman Empire lasted from about 1300 to 1922. The written language of its state is called **Ottoman Turkish**. It was Turkish in its grammar, but it was written in the Arabic script, and educated writers filled it with Arabic and Persian. Arabic was the language of religion, law and scholarship. Persian was the model for poetry and fine prose. An educated Ottoman was expected to command all three languages, and in formal writing the Turkish base could almost disappear under borrowed words and borrowed constructions.

Ordinary speech was different. It kept Arabic and Persian words that had become part of daily life, such as *kitap* "book" (from Arabic) and *cam* "glass" (from Persian), but little of the borrowed grammar. This plainer Turkish also had a literature of its own. The poet Yunus Emre, who died around 1320, wrote in it, and this guide ends by reading one of his poems.

Ottoman Turkish has its own guide in this app. It teaches the Arabic script and the Arabic and Persian layers that this guide leaves aside. If you finish this guide, you will have the grammar you need for that one.

## The break of 1928

The Ottoman Empire ended after the First World War, and the Republic of Turkey was founded in 1923. Its leader, Mustafa Kemal, later called Atatürk, made the language part of a programme of radical reform. Two changes followed.

The first was the alphabet. In 1928 Turkish was given a specially adapted form of the Latin alphabet, and the Arabic script was banned from teaching and publishing in Turkish. The new alphabet fits the sounds of Turkish closely, with very nearly one letter for each sound, and part 1 of this guide teaches it.

The second was the vocabulary. From 1932 an official language society, today the Türk Dil Kurumu (Turkish Language Association), led a campaign to replace Arabic and Persian words with Turkish ones. Some replacements came from dialects, some from old texts, and many were new words built from Turkish roots and suffixes. The campaign largely achieved its aims. The vocabulary of Turkish today is very different from that of a century ago, so a text of the 1920s can be hard going for a modern reader even when it is printed in Latin letters. Borrowings from French and other European languages, such as *otobüs* "bus" and *tren* "train", were hardly opposed and are everywhere.

## Which Turkish this guide teaches

This guide teaches modern standard Turkish: the language of schools, newspapers, television and almost all writing in Turkey, as spoken by educated people in Istanbul. Spelling follows the dictionary of the Türk Dil Kurumu.

Where speech and writing differ, the guide teaches the written standard and gives the spoken form in the note, labelled colloquial. Forms used mainly in formal writing are labelled formal. Regional dialects, and the Turkish of Cyprus and the Balkans, get one lesson in part 7. Older Turkish appears only in the readings of part 8, always explained, never taught as modern.

## What kind of language it is

Three facts shape everything that follows.

Turkish builds words by adding suffixes, small pieces placed after a stem, one after another. Each suffix adds one meaning, and the suffixes always come in the same order. A single word can do the work of an English phrase. The next lesson is about this, because it is the idea the whole grammar hangs on.

The vowels of those suffixes change to match the vowels of the word they are added to. This is called vowel harmony, and part 1 teaches it before any grammar, because you cannot write or say a single suffix correctly without it.

Turkish has no grammatical gender, not even in its pronouns: one word, *o*, covers "he", "she" and "it". It has no word for "the". And it puts the verb at the end of the sentence. If you know Japanese or Korean, much of the sentence shape will feel familiar. If you know only European languages, the shape will feel strange for a week, and then logical.
