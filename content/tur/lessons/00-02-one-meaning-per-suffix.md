## The idea

Every language needs a way to add meanings to a word: more than one, mine, inside, not, in the past. Languages solve this in different ways.

English mostly uses separate words placed in front: "in my houses". Latin and Spanish use endings, but one ending often carries several meanings at once, and you cannot cut it into a piece for each.

Turkish uses a chain of **suffixes**. A suffix is a small piece added after a word. The word you start from is the **stem**. In Turkish each suffix carries one meaning, and the suffixes stand in a fixed order. You build a word by adding them one at a time, like beads on a string.

Languages built like this are called **agglutinative**, from the Latin for "glued on". The name fits: the pieces are glued on and stay visible. Turkish adds all its grammar at the end of the word. It has no grammatical prefixes.

That is the organising idea of this guide: **one meaning per suffix, in a fixed order**. Almost every grammar lesson teaches a new suffix, the place it takes in the chain, or a new use for one you already know.

## How it works in one word

Here is a Turkish word, built up in four steps. Do not try to learn the suffixes yet. They are taught properly in parts 2 and 3. For now, watch the chain grow.

:::example
orig: ev
morphemes: ev
gloss: house
translation: house, a house, the house
note: The stem. It is a complete word on its own.
source: wiktionary
confidence: verified
:::

:::example
orig: evler
morphemes: ev-ler
gloss: house-PL
translation: houses
note: Preview. Add -ler and the word means more than one.
source: wiktionary
confidence: verified
preview: PL
:::

Under each Turkish word you see it cut at the joins, and under that a label for each piece. This is called a **gloss**. The label PL means plural, "more than one". The next lesson explains how to read glosses in full.

:::example
orig: evlerim
morphemes: ev-ler-im
gloss: house-PL-1SG.POSS
translation: my houses
note: Preview. Add -im and the houses are mine. The plural stays where it was.
source: wiktionary
confidence: verified
preview: PL, 1, SG, POSS
:::

:::example
orig: evlerimde
morphemes: ev-ler-im-de
gloss: house-PL-1SG.POSS-LOC
translation: in my houses
note: Preview. Add -de and the word means "in". One Turkish word, four English ones.
source: wiktionary
confidence: verified
preview: PL, 1, SG, POSS, LOC
:::

Look at the order. English says "in my houses": place first, then owner, then the noun with its plural. Turkish says *ev-ler-im-de*, "house-s-my-in": the noun first, then number, then owner, then place. The order is not a matter of style. The plural always comes before the owner, and the owner always before the place.

Take a piece away and the others keep their places.

:::example
orig: evimde
morphemes: ev-im-de
gloss: house-1SG.POSS-LOC
translation: in my house
note: Preview. Without the plural, the owner and the place stay in the same order.
source: wiktionary
confidence: verified
preview: 1, SG, POSS, LOC
:::

This guide calls each fixed position a **slot**. A slot can be empty, as the plural slot is here, but its neighbours never swap.

## The suffixes change shape

Now build the same chain on a different noun, *araba* "car".

:::example
orig: arabalarımda
morphemes: araba-lar-ım-da
gloss: car-PL-1SG.POSS-LOC
translation: in my cars
note: Preview. The same four meanings in the same order, but -ler is now -lar, -im is -ım, and -de is -da.
source: wiktionary
confidence: verified
preview: PL, 1, SG, POSS, LOC
:::

The suffixes are the same suffixes. Their vowels have changed to match the vowels of *araba*. This is **vowel harmony**: the vowel of each suffix is chosen to agree with the vowel before it. The plural is always *l*, a vowel and *r*, but the vowel is *e* after a word like *ev* and *a* after a word like *araba*.

Harmony is why this guide teaches sounds before grammar. A Turkish suffix has no single spelling. It has a rule that chooses its spelling, and part 1 gives you that rule before you meet a single suffix in earnest.

## The verb does it too

Verbs are built the same way, and their chains are longer.

:::example
orig: geliyorum
morphemes: gel-iyor-um
gloss: come-PROG-1SG
translation: I am coming
note: Preview. Stem gel- "come", then a suffix meaning "in progress", then one meaning "I".
source: wiktionary
confidence: verified
preview: PROG, 1, SG
:::

:::example
orig: gelmedim
morphemes: gel-me-di-m
gloss: come-NEG-PST-1SG
translation: I did not come
note: Preview. "Not" comes straight after the stem, then the past, then the person.
source: wiktionary
confidence: verified
preview: NEG, PST, 1, SG
:::

:::example
orig: yapmadım
morphemes: yap-ma-dı-m
gloss: do-NEG-PST-1SG
translation: I did not do it
note: Preview. A different stem, yap- "do", with the same chain. Harmony turns -me into -ma and -di into -dı.
source: wiktionary
confidence: verified
preview: NEG, PST, 1, SG
:::

English needs a pronoun, a helper verb and a separate "not". Turkish puts all of it in one word, and the person suffix at the end makes a pronoun unnecessary.

## Why Turkish is like this

Turkish did not invent this system. It inherited it. Many of its suffixes have relatives across the Turkic family and can be traced back to Proto-Turkic, the reconstructed ancestor of the family. Wiktionary's etymologies, for instance, trace the "in, at" suffix to a Proto-Turkic suffix that already had a front-vowel form and a back-vowel form, as *-de* and *-da* do today. So harmony is old too: it was already working on suffixes in the parent language. Why the parent language worked this way is not known.

## What this means for you as a reader

Three habits follow.

Read every word from left to right, piece by piece. The stem tells you what the word is about. Each suffix after it adds one fact, and you can read the facts off in order.

When you build a word, ask the questions in slot order. Which stem? More than one? Whose? Where? The answer to each question is one suffix, and the next question picks up where the last one left off.

Expect long words, and do not fear them. A Turkish word of eight syllables is usually a short stem and five or six small, regular suffixes, each of which you will already know. Japanese and Korean readers have met this way of working with verb endings and particles. Turkish applies it to nouns and verbs alike, and harmony binds the whole chain together.
