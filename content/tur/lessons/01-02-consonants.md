## The idea

Most Turkish consonants sound as they do in English. A few letters have values you must learn, and three letters, *k*, *g* and *l*, each have two pronunciations that depend on the vowels around them.

The alphabet comes close to one letter for each sound. English needs two letters for the sounds of "ch", "sh" and the "j" of "jam". Turkish gives each its own letter, by adding a mark below or reusing a letter English spends on something else.

- **c** is [dʒ], the "j" of "jam": *cam* "glass".
- **ç** is [tʃ], the "ch" of "church": *çay* "tea".
- **ş** is [ʃ], the "sh" of "ship": *şey* "thing".
- **j** is [ʒ], the "s" of "measure". It occurs only in loanwords, mostly from French and Persian: *jilet* "razor blade".
- **y** is [j], always a consonant, as in "yes".
- **r** is a tap [ɾ]: the tongue tip touches the ridge behind the teeth once, as in Spanish *pero*. At the end of a word it loses its voice and can sound almost like a soft "sh".
- **h** is pronounced at the end of a word too: *sabah* "morning".

Each consonant also belongs to a pair. Put your fingers on your throat and say "zzz", then "sss". The first hums and the second does not. A consonant that hums is **voiced**, one that does not is **voiceless**. Seven pairs of letters differ only in this way, voiced first: *b/p*, *d/t*, *g/k*, *c/ç*, *v/f*, *z/s*, *j/ş*. The letter *h* is voiceless and has no partner; *m*, *n*, *l*, *r* and *y* are voiced. Keep this division in mind. Lesson 6 shows that suffixes switch between the members of a pair.

**Front and back consonants.** The tongue that makes *k* and *g* is the same tongue that makes the vowels. Before or after a front vowel (*e i ö ü*) it is already forward, so *k* and *g* are made further forward, against the hard palate: [c] and [ɟ]. To an English ear these sound like "k" and "g" with a faint "y" after them, as in "cute". Next to a back vowel (*a ı o u*) they are made at the back, [k] and [ɡ].

*L* does the same. Next to a front vowel it is the clear [l] of English "leaf". Next to a back vowel it is the dark [ɫ] of English "full".

In native words you never have to choose. The vowel decides, and the spelling does not show the difference. But the difference is real, and it is the first sign of a pattern that runs through the language: **front goes with front, back goes with back**. Vowel harmony, in lesson 4, is the same pattern at the level of suffixes.

## How it is built

To read a consonant, take three steps.

1. Check the letter. If it is *c*, *ç*, *ş*, *j*, *y*, *r* or *h*, use the values above. Otherwise start from English.
2. If it is *k*, *g* or *l*, look at the nearest vowel. Front vowel: front version. Back vowel: back version.
3. If it is *p*, *t*, *k* or *ç* before a vowel or at the end of a word, add a small puff of air, as English does in "pin". Before another consonant it has no puff.

Two more facts. A doubled consonant is held for twice as long: *elli* "fifty" has a long [lː], as in Japanese *kitte* or Italian *anno*. And *v* between vowels, next to a rounded one, is often made with both lips, close to [w]: *tavuk* "chicken".

## Watch out

**c is never "k" or "s".** English and Spanish habits pull you towards *cam* as "kam". Read Turkish *c* as English *j*, every time.

**ç and ş are two letters, not decorated c and s.** In dictionaries they have their own places, after *c* and *s*.

**The r is light.** Do not use the English "r" with the tongue curled back, and do not roll it: one quick tap. The word *bir* "one" is often said [bi] in quick speech when unstressed, a colloquial habit you will hear constantly.

**No new clusters.** A native Turkish word never begins with two consonants. Loanwords that do, such as *tren* "train", are often said with a helping vowel. Lesson 8 returns to this.

## Connections

Korean readers know the puff of air: Turkish *p t k ç* before a vowel sound like ㅍ ㅌ ㅋ ㅊ. Turkish has no separate set without the puff, only the weaker versions before a consonant. The Turkish *r* is the tap of Spanish *pero*. *Çay* is a loan from Chinese, according to the Türk Dil Kurumu.

## Exercises

1. Write the sounds of *gece* and *kapı* in brackets.
2. In *kedi* and *kar*, the *k* is pronounced differently. Explain why, using the vowels.
3. Which letter would you use to write the first sound of English "church", and which for "jam"?
4. Is the *l* of *göl* clear or dark? And of *bal*? What decides it?
5. Name the voiceless partner of *b*, *d*, *c* and *z*.
6. A learner says *sabah* as "saba". What is missing, and is it ever dropped in Turkish?
7. *Elli* and *eli* "his hand" differ in one way. How?

## Worked answers

1. [ɟedʒe] and [kapɯ]. In *gece* the g is front because e follows it, and c is [dʒ]. In *kapı* the k is back because a follows it, and the last vowel is dotless ı.
2. *K* is made where the tongue already is for the next vowel. In *kedi* that vowel is front e, so the k is front [c]. In *kar* it is back a, so the k is back [k].
3. *ç* for "ch" and *c* for "j".
4. Clear after ö in *göl*, dark after a in *bal*. The vowel next to the l decides: front vowel, clear l; back vowel, dark l.
5. *p*, *t*, *ç* and *s*.
6. The final h. Turkish pronounces h at the end of a word, here as a breathy [x] after a. It can fade before a consonant, as in *kahve*, but not at the end of *sabah*.
7. Length of the l. *Elli* has a double l, held twice as long; *eli* has a single one.
