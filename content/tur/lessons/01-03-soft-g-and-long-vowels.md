## The idea

One Turkish letter has no sound of its own. It is **ğ**, called *yumuşak g*, "soft g". It never begins a word. In standard speech it is not pronounced as a consonant at all. What it does is change the vowels around it: it lengthens the vowel before it, or it marks a boundary between two vowels, or it slips in as a light [j].

Why write a letter you do not say? Because it was once a consonant, and in some dialects it still is, a soft fricative made at the back of the mouth. Wiktionary's etymology of *dağ* "mountain" traces it to Proto-Turkic *\*tāg*, with a real *g*. Standard Turkish has lost that sound, but the spelling keeps the letter, and the grammar still treats it as a consonant. A vowel-initial suffix attaches to *dağ* exactly as it would to a word ending in *k* or *t*, with none of the extra letters that are needed after a vowel (lesson 6). So *ğ* is silent in the mouth but present in the system. Linguists disagree whether that makes it a true unit of sound; this guide treats it as a letter with three behaviours.

This lesson also covers **long vowels**. Native Turkish words do not distinguish long and short vowels; the long vowels you hear in them come from a silent *ğ* (or, as in *kahve*, a fading *h*). Loanwords from Arabic and Persian do have long vowels, and the spelling mostly does not show them. A few words mark them with a **circumflex**, the hat on *â*, *î*, *û*.

## How it is built

To read *ğ*, look at the vowels on each side.

1. **At the end of a syllable, after a back vowel:** lengthen the vowel and say nothing else. *Dağ* is [daː], and *yağmur* "rain" is [jaːmuɾ].
2. **Between two identical vowels:** say one long vowel, or two with the lightest break: *ağaç* "tree" is [aːtʃ].
3. **Next to *e* or *i*:** it may be a light [j], as in "yes": *değil* "not" is [dejil].
4. **Between a rounded vowel and another vowel:** usually silent, sometimes a faint [w]: *soğuk* "cold" is [souk] or [sowuk].

When *ğ* stands between two vowels, they belong to two syllables, not one. They do not merge into a glide.

To read a **circumflex**, ask which of its two jobs it is doing.

1. It can mark a **long vowel**: *hâlâ* "still" is [haːlaː].
2. After *k*, *g* or *l*, it can mark that the consonant is the **front** version, although a back vowel follows. You saw in the last lesson that *k* before *a* is normally back. In *kâr* "profit" it is front: [caɾ]. Without the hat, *kar* is "snow", [kaɾ].

Often it does both: in *hâlâ* it marks the long vowels and the clear l.

The circumflex is not a separate letter, and its use is declining. Many writers leave it out where the context is clear. This guide writes it exactly where the dictionary of the Türk Dil Kurumu writes it in the headword.

## Watch out

**Never say ğ as g.** *Dağ* is not "dag". The temptation is strongest for readers who know the letter g from every other language.

**A long vowel from ğ is still two letters' worth of word.** *Dağ* sounds like a long "da", but it is not the same word as *da*, and suffixes treat it differently (lesson 6).

**The spelling hides most long vowels.** *Tarih* and *mavi* carry no mark. Learn the length with the word, as the vocabulary notes give it.

**A missing circumflex does not change the word.** Printed texts often leave it out. Read by context, and write as the dictionary does.

## Connections

Arabic readers will recognise *saat* (*sāʿa*), *tarih* (*tārīkh*) and *dakika* (*daqīqa*): the Turkish long vowels are the Arabic ones, kept in speech though not in spelling. The double a of *saat* is where Arabic has the consonant ʿayn, which Turkish dropped. Japanese readers are used to long vowels that change a word; in Turkish, length alone seldom separates two words.

## Exercises

1. Write the sounds of *yağmur* and say what the *ğ* does.
2. Every behaviour of *ğ* involves a vowel before it. What does that tell you about where it can stand in a word?
3. *Kar* and *kâr* differ in one sound. Which one, and what does the circumflex tell you?
4. How many syllables has *soğuk*? And *ağaç*?
5. A friend writes *hala* in a message meaning "still". Is that a spelling mistake, and how would you know what was meant?
6. *Saat* is written with two a's. What do they stand for, and where does the spelling come from?
7. Which of these words contain a long vowel that the spelling does not show: *tarih*, *kedi*, *mavi*, *gece*?

## Worked answers

1. [jaːmuɾ]. The *ğ* closes the first syllable after a back vowel, so it is silent and lengthens the a.
2. It can never stand first. It lengthens the vowel before it or links it to the next one, and at the start of a word there is no vowel before it. That matches the fact that no Turkish word begins with *ğ*.
3. The first sound: [c] in *kâr*, [k] in *kar*. Before *a*, *k* would normally be the back [k]; the circumflex says it is the front [c] instead.
4. Two each: so·ğuk, and a·ğaç. The *ğ* links two vowels that belong to separate syllables. In fast speech *ağaç* can sound like one long [aː] plus [tʃ], but it still counts as two.
5. It is the common practice of leaving out the circumflex, not a real error, though the dictionary writes *hâlâ*. The sentence tells you: an aunt is a person, and "still" goes with a verb or a state.
6. One long vowel, [aː]. The word is from Arabic *sāʿa*, which had a consonant between the vowels. Turkish lost the consonant, and the two vowels merged into a long one.
7. *Tarih* and *mavi*: [taːɾih], [maːvi]. *Kedi* and *gece* have only short vowels; the dictionary marks no length in either.
