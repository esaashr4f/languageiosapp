## The idea

**Stress** is the extra prominence one syllable of a word gets over the others. In Turkish that prominence is mainly a higher pitch, with some extra loudness. Turkish spelling never marks it. In this lesson only, the guide marks the stressed vowel with an acute accent, *arabá*, and the IPA marks the stressed syllable with [ˈ]. Neither is part of Turkish spelling.

The basic rule is the simplest possible one: **stress the last syllable**. *Arabá* "car". *Masá* "table". And because a suffix becomes the new last syllable, the stress moves right as the word grows: *arabá*, *arabalár*, *arabalarımdá*. A long Turkish word has a rising, forward-leaning shape, with its peak at the end.

This fits the organising idea of the guide. A word is a stem plus suffixes, and by default the stress marks where the word stops. Two kinds of exception break the pattern, and both are worth knowing because they carry meaning.

**Words with fixed stress.** Some stems carry their own stress on an earlier syllable, and keep it there whatever is added. The main groups are place names (*Ánkara*, *İstánbul*, *Tǘrkiye*), many loanwords from European languages (*lokánta* "restaurant", *bánka* "bank"), and a group of adverbs (*şímdi* "now"). A few pairs differ by stress alone: *bebék* "baby" but *Bébek*, a district of Istanbul.

**Suffixes that refuse stress.** A small set of suffixes and particles never take stress themselves and push it onto the syllable before them. Göksel and Kerslake call them **unstressable**. They include the question particle *mI* (*güzél mi?*), the negative suffix *-mA* (part 2), and the endings that mean "is" or "am" after a noun (part 2). One suffix, *-(I)yor* (part 2), takes the stress on its own first syllable. Each of these is flagged when it is taught.

Is this stress, like English stress, or a pitch accent, like the high and low pitches of Japanese? Researchers disagree. Levi (2005) found pitch to be the main cue and some writers call the system a pitch accent; Kabak (2016) finds no clear evidence for a pitch-accent system. The position of the accent, which is what you need, is not in dispute.

## How it is built

To stress a word, take three steps.

1. **Is the stem one of the fixed-stress words?** Place names, European loans, some adverbs: the vocabulary lists mark them with an acute. If so, stress that syllable and stop.
2. **Otherwise, go to the last syllable.**
3. **Is the last piece unstressable?** If the word ends in *mI* or another unstressable piece, step back one syllable and stress that.

**Sentences.** In a statement the voice rises a little and then falls at the end. In a yes-no question with *mI*, the syllable before *mI* is pitched high and the voice then falls. In a neutral sentence the strongest stress of all goes on the word just before the verb. Part 2 builds on this: it is why Turkish puts new information there.

## Watch out

**Do not carry English stress over.** English speakers often stress the last syllable of "Istanbul"; Turkish stresses the second. Learn the stress of a loanword or a name with the word.

**A suffix does not always mean a stress shift.** It does after a regular stem: *arabalár*. After a fixed-stress stem it does not: *Ánkara'da*. Before an unstressable piece, the shift stops one syllable short: *güzél mi*.

**Compounds have their own rule.** Two words written as one, such as *bugün* "today" (*bu* "this" + *gün* "day"), are usually stressed on the first part: *búgün*. You will meet the word later.

**Stress rarely carries meaning on its own,** but in pairs like *bebék* and *Bébek* it does.

## Connections

Japanese readers know a word accent that is mainly pitch and can separate words (*háshi* "chopsticks" against *hashí* "bridge"). Some researchers describe Turkish the same way; the comparison is one of type only. Spanish readers know stress that separates words too, but Spanish marks the exceptions with an accent in writing and Turkish does not.

## Exercises

1. Mark the stress on *masa*, *masalar* and *masalarda*.
2. Why is *Ankara'da* not stressed on its last syllable?
3. Mark the stress on *ordu* "army" and on the city *Ordu*. Which follows the basic rule?
4. Where does the high pitch fall in *Büyük mü?*
5. A friend says *İstanbul* with the stress on the last syllable. What is the Turkish stress, and why can you not work it out from the rule?
6. What do researchers disagree about in Turkish stress, and what do they agree on?

## Worked answers

1. *Masá*, *masalár*, *masalardá*. Each suffix becomes the new last syllable and takes the stress.
2. *Ankara* is a place name with fixed stress on its first syllable, *Án-*. Fixed stress stays where it is when suffixes are added.
3. *Ordú* "army" follows the basic rule, last syllable. *Órdu* the city is a place name and has fixed stress on the first.
4. On *-yük*: *büyǘk mü*. The particle is unstressed, so the syllable before it gets the stress and the high pitch of the question.
5. *İstánbul*, on the second syllable. Place names are exceptions to the last-syllable rule, and the exact syllable has to be learnt with the name.
6. They disagree whether the accent is stress or a pitch accent: Levi (2005) finds pitch the main cue, Kabak (2016) finds no clear evidence of a pitch-accent system. They agree on where the accent falls.
