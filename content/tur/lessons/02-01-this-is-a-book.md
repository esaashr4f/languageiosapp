## The idea

Every sentence has two parts. The **subject** is what the sentence is about. The **predicate** is what the sentence says about it. In "The house is old", the subject is "the house" and the predicate is "is old".

In English the predicate always contains a verb, even when the verb means almost nothing. "Is" in "the house is old" only links the two parts. Turkish does not need the link. It puts the subject first, the predicate second, and stops:

*Ev eski.* "House old." The house is old.

A sentence like this, whose predicate is a noun or an adjective instead of a verb, is a **nominal sentence**. Göksel and Kerslake call it the normal way to say "x is y" when the time is the present and the subject is "he", "she", "it" or a noun (goksel2005 §12.1.1.2). There is a marker for "was" and one for "apparently is", both taught in part 4. For plain "is" there is nothing.

This is not a shortcut or a casual style. It is the standard sentence, written and spoken. Arabic readers know the same thing: *al-baytu kabīrun*, "the house (is) big", has no verb either.

Two more English words have no Turkish equivalent here.

**There is no word for "the".** Turkish has no definite article. *Ev* is "house", "a house" or "the house", and the situation tells you which. A subject at the start of a sentence is usually understood as something both speakers know about: *Ev eski* is "the house is old".

**"A" is *bir*.** The word you met in part 1 as "one" also serves as "a, an". *Bu bir kitap*: "this is a book". Turkish uses *bir* less often than English uses "a", as the next section shows.

## How it is built

To build a nominal sentence, take four steps.

1. **Name the subject.** A noun, a name or the word *bu* "this": *ev*, *Ali*, *bu*.
2. **Name the predicate.** A noun or an adjective: *eski* "old", *öğretmen* "teacher".
3. **Put the subject first and the predicate last.** Add nothing between them: *Ev eski.*
4. **Decide whether the predicate noun needs *bir*.**
   - When you say what job, nationality or kind of person someone is, leave *bir* out: *Ali öğretmen*, "Ali is a teacher". Göksel and Kerslake note that this bare form is the regular choice for occupation, nationality and social status (goksel2005 §22.3).
   - When you identify a thing, use *bir*: *Bu bir kitap*, "this is a book".
   - When an adjective describes the predicate noun, put *bir* after the adjective and before the noun: *Ayşe iyi bir doktor*, "Ayşe is a good doctor". Göksel and Kerslake give this order as much the more common one (goksel2005 §15.9.2).

**Order decides between a phrase and a sentence.** An adjective in front of a noun describes it, and the two together make a **phrase**, a group of words that is not yet a sentence: *eski ev* "the old house". An adjective after the noun is a predicate, and the two together make a sentence: *Ev eski* "the house is old". The words are the same. Only their order differs.

**Where the stress goes.** In a nominal sentence the strongest stress of the sentence falls on the predicate (goksel2005 §23.1.2.1): *Ali öğretMEN*. If an adjective describes the predicate noun, the stress falls on the adjective: *Ayşe İYİ bir doktor*. Capitals here, as in Göksel and Kerslake, show sentence stress; they are not Turkish spelling. Inside each word the stress follows the rules of tur-01-07. The voice falls at the end, as in any statement.

## Where it applies

The nominal sentence is the form for every "x is y" in the present with a third-person subject:

- **What something is**: *Bu bir kitap.*
- **What job, nationality or role someone has**: *Ali öğretmen*, *Ali Türk*. No *bir*.
- **What someone or something is like**: *Ev eski*, *Adam hasta*. Turkish uses the same pattern for a lasting quality and a passing state.
- **A predicate noun with its own adjective**: *Ayşe iyi bir doktor*.
- **The weather**: *Hava sıcak*, *Hava kötü*. *Hava* "weather, air" is the subject.
- **Things in general**: a bare noun can mean a whole kind. *Kitap pahalı* can be "the book is expensive" or "books are expensive"; the situation decides (goksel2005 §14.3.1.3).
- **Two statements in a row**, separated by a comma or joined by *ve* "and": *Kitap pahalı ve kalem ucuz*.
- **Where something is**: the same pattern with a place word, *Ali evde* "Ali is at home". This needs the locative case (tur-02-06).
- **"I am", "you are", "we are"**: the same pattern plus a person ending (tur-02-02).
- **"Is not" and "is it?"**: the same pattern with *değil* (tur-02-04) and *mI* (tur-02-05).
- **"Was", "will be", "apparently is"**: these need markers or the verb *ol-* "be, become" (part 4). The bare pattern is present only.

## Watch out

**Do not look for a word for "is".** *\*Ali var öğretmen* is not Turkish. *Var* means "there is" (tur-02-06), like Spanish *hay*, not *es*. Say *Ali öğretmen*.

**Spanish *ser* and *estar* are one pattern here.** Spanish uses *es* for "Ali is a doctor" and *está* for "the man is ill". Turkish uses no word for either: *Ali doktor*, *Adam hasta*.

**Do not put the adjective after the noun to mean "the old house".** Spanish *la casa vieja* and French *la maison vieille* tempt *\*ev eski* for "the old house". In Turkish that order is a sentence, "the house is old". The phrase is *eski ev*.

**Do not put *bir* after the noun.** English "a house old" is not a model, nor is *\*eski ev bir*. *Bir* stands just before the noun: *eski bir ev*. The order *bir eski ev* also exists, with a slightly different shade (goksel2005 §15.9.2), but *eski bir ev* is the usual one.

**Do not add *bir* to every job.** *Ali bir öğretmen* is possible, but the regular way to give someone's job or nationality is the bare noun: *Ali öğretmen*, *Ali Türk*. *Bir* returns as soon as an adjective comes in: *Ali iyi bir öğretmen*.

**Do not use *o* for "the".** *O* means "that" or "he, she, it" (part 3). *O kitap* is "that book", not "the book". For "the book" say *kitap*.

**Do not use a pronoun for weather.** English "it is hot" has an empty "it". Turkish names the weather: *Hava sıcak*. *\*O sıcak* would mean "that thing is hot" or "he is warm".

***Eski* before a person means "former".** *Eski* is "old" for things, but *eski öğretmen* is "former teacher" (wiktionary, *eski*). For an old person Turkish uses another word, taught in the next lesson.

## Connections

Arabic has the same sentence type: a subject and a predicate with no verb in the present, *al-kitābu jadīdun* "the book is new", while past time needs the verb *kāna*. Turkish works alike: no verb now, a marker for the past. Korean and Japanese, which Turkish often resembles in word order, do use a copula here: Korean 이다, Japanese だ or です.

## Exercises

1. Say "the man is tall" and "the tall man". Explain the difference.
2. Say "Ayşe is a student" and "Ayşe is a good student".
3. Take apart *Bu ev çok pahalı*: subject, predicate, and the job of each word.
4. Translate *Hava kötü, Türkçe zor.*
5. Say "this is a new book". Where does *bir* go?
6. A learner writes *\*Ali bir öğretmen var* for "Ali is a teacher". Correct it and give two reasons.
7. Which word in *Ali öğretmen* carries the sentence stress, and which syllable of it?
8. What does *eski doktor* mean, and why is it not "the old doctor" in the sense of an elderly man?

## Worked answers

1. *Adam uzun* is the sentence "the man is tall": the adjective comes after the noun, so it is the predicate. *Uzun adam* is the phrase "the tall man": the adjective comes first, so it describes the noun. The phrase still needs something said about it to become a sentence.
2. *Ayşe öğrenci*: a role, so no *bir*. *Ayşe iyi bir öğrenci*: an adjective comes in, so *bir* appears, after the adjective and before the noun.
3. The subject is *bu ev* "this house": *bu* points, *ev* names. The predicate is *çok pahalı* "very expensive": *pahalı* is the adjective, and *çok* strengthens it. No word means "is". The sentence means "this house is very expensive".
4. "The weather is bad; Turkish is difficult." Two nominal sentences, each subject then predicate.
5. *Bu yeni bir kitap.* *Bu* is the subject. The predicate is *yeni bir kitap*, with the adjective first, then *bir*, then the noun.
6. *Ali öğretmen.* First, *var* means "there is", not "is"; a nominal sentence has no word for "is". Second, a job is normally given without *bir*.
7. The predicate *öğretmen*, on its last syllable: *öğretMEN*. In a nominal sentence the sentence stress falls on the predicate, and inside the word the stress follows the last-syllable rule.
8. "The former doctor" or "a former doctor". Before a noun for a person, *eski* means "former". For an elderly person Turkish uses a different adjective (next lesson).
