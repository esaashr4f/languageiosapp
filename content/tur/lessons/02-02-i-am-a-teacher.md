## The idea

Lesson 1 built "Ali is a teacher" with nothing between subject and predicate. For "I am a teacher" Turkish adds one small piece to the end of the predicate:

*Öğretmenim.* "Teacher-I." I am a teacher.

The piece *-im* says who the subject is. It is a **personal ending**: a suffix on the predicate that names the subject's **person** (the speaker, the person spoken to, or someone else) and **number** (one or more than one). Grammar calls the speaker the first person, the person spoken to the second, and everyone and everything else the third.

There are six endings, one for each combination of person and number. The third person singular, "he, she, it", has no ending at all, which is why *Ali öğretmen* needed nothing. That gap is not an accident of this lesson. It runs through the whole verb system: the third person singular is usually the bare form.

**Why the endings look the way they do.** Several of them resemble the personal pronouns, the words for "I", "you" and so on. "I" is *ben*, and the ending is *-(y)Im*; "you" is *sen*, and the ending is *-sIn*. Wiktionary's etymologies trace both endings to these pronouns in Proto-Turkic, the ancestor of the family. Turkish has, in effect, glued a worn-down "I" or "you" onto the end of the predicate. Spanish readers know the result: *soy*, *eres*, *somos* carry the person in the ending, and the pronoun is optional.

**The pronoun is optional; the ending is not.** Because the ending already names the subject, Turkish leaves the pronoun out unless it wants to contrast or stress it. Göksel and Kerslake state that with a first or second person subject the ending is required whether or not the pronoun is present (goksel2005 §12.2.1). *Öğretmenim* alone is a complete sentence. *Ben öğretmenim* adds weight to "I": "*I* am a teacher".

## How it is built

The endings are suffixes, so they obey the harmony and buffer rules of part 1. In this guide's notation they are:

- 1st person singular "I": **-(y)Im**
- 2nd person singular "you", one person, familiar: **-sIn**
- 3rd person singular "he, she, it": nothing
- 1st person plural "we": **-(y)Iz**
- 2nd person plural "you", several people or one person politely: **-sInIz**
- 3rd person plural "they": **-lAr**, often left out (see below)

To add one, take four steps.

1. **Find the predicate.** The noun or adjective at the end: *yorgun* "tired".
2. **Choose the ending for the subject.** "We": *-(y)Iz*.
3. **Check the last sound of the predicate.** After a vowel, *-(y)Im* and *-(y)Iz* take the buffer *y*: *hasta-yım*, *hasta-yız*. After a consonant they do not: *yorgun-um*. The endings that start with *s* or *l* never need a buffer.
4. **Apply harmony.** *I* is a four-way vowel, *A* a two-way vowel. After the *u* of *yorgun*, *I* is *u*: *yorgunuz* "we are tired". In *-sInIz* both vowels harmonise: *yorgunsunuz*, *hastasınız*.

The consonant rules of lesson 6 of part 1 apply as well. A noun whose final *k* softens before a vowel softens before *-(y)Im* and *-(y)Iz*: *çocuk* "child", *çocuğum* "I am a child".

**Who is "you"?** Turkish, like French, has two words. *Sen* is for one person you know well, a child, a friend. *Siz* is for several people, and also for one person you address politely: a stranger, an older person, someone senior (goksel2005 §18.1.1). The ending follows the pronoun: *-sIn* with *sen*, *-sInIz* with *siz*, even when *siz* means one person.

**"They".** The ending *-lAr* is the same as the plural suffix you met in previews (lesson 3 teaches it on nouns). On a predicate it is optional in many cases. Göksel and Kerslake give the main rule: when no subject word is said and the subject is people or animals, *-lAr* is required: *Yorgunlar* "they are tired". When the subject is said, for example *onlar* "they", *-lAr* is common but can be dropped: *Onlar yorgun* or *Onlar yorgunlar* (goksel2005 §§12.2.1, 12.2.2.1).

**Stress.** The personal endings never take stress. They push it onto the syllable before them (goksel2005 §4.3.2): *yorgúnum*, *hastáyım*, *öğretménsiniz*. This is the behaviour tur-01-07 promised. It matters at once, because *öğretmenim* has a second meaning, "my teacher", with a possessive suffix that does take stress: *öğretmením* "my teacher" against *öğretménim* "I am a teacher" (goksel2005 §§4.3.1, 4.3.2). Part 3 teaches the possessive. Until then, read *-(y)Im* on a predicate as "I am".

## Where it applies

The personal endings of this lesson mark the subject of every predicate except a few verb forms. In this guide they are called the **z-set**, after the *z* of *-(y)Iz*.

- **After a noun**: *öğretmenim*, *öğrenciyiz*, *komşuyuz*.
- **After an adjective**: *yorgunum*, *hastayım*, *mutlusun* "you are happy".
- **After a noun with its adjective**: the ending goes on the last word, *iyi bir arkadaşsın*.
- **After a nationality**: *Türküm*, *Japonsunuz*, *İngilizim*.
- **With or without the pronoun**: *Öğretmenim* and *Ben öğretmenim* are both correct. Use the pronoun to contrast or to stress the person (goksel2005 §18.1.5.1): *Ali yorgun, ben hazırım* "Ali is tired, (but) I am ready".
- **With a joint subject**: "Ali and I" takes the "we" ending; "you and Ali" takes the "you" plural ending (goksel2005 §12.2.2.4).
- **Polite "you" to one person**: *Siz yorgunsunuz* can be said to one older person.
- **"They", out of respect**: Göksel and Kerslake note that a single respected person can be spoken of with the plural *-lAr* (goksel2005 §12.2.1). You will hear it with titles.
- **After a place word**: *evdeyim* "I am at home" (tur-02-06).
- **After *değil* "not"**: *değilim* "I am not" (tur-02-04).
- **After the verb suffix *-(I)yor***: *geliyorum* "I am coming" (tur-02-08). The same six endings serve verbs and nouns alike.
- **Not after the past *-DI***: that tense has its own set, *geldim* "I came" (tur-04-03).

## Watch out

**Do not leave the ending off because the pronoun is there.** *\*Ben öğretmen* does not mean "I am a teacher". The ending is required: *Ben öğretmenim*.

**Do not write a separate word for "am".** The ending is a suffix, written joined: *\*Ben im öğretmen* and *\*Öğretmen im* are wrong. Say *Öğretmenim*.

**Do not forget the buffer after a vowel.** *\*Hastaım* breaks the rule that two vowels do not meet. Say *hastayım*. And *hastam* is a different word: "my patient" (possessive, part 3).

**Do not make the noun plural for "we" or "you".** Spanish says *somos estudiantes*, plural noun and plural verb. Turkish keeps the noun bare and puts the plural in the ending: *Biz öğrenciyiz*. A plural noun in the predicate means "the students", a particular group (goksel2005 §22.3).

**Do not use *sen* and *-sIn* to a stranger.** French *tu* and *vous* are a good guide. *Sen hazırsın* to someone you have just met is too familiar. Say *Siz hazırsınız*.

**Do not let the second vowel of *-sInIz* escape harmony.** *\*Hastasiniz* mixes front and back. Both vowels follow the vowel before them: *hastasınız*, *yorgunsunuz*, *öğretmensiniz*.

**Do not add *-lAr* twice.** If *onlar* is said, *-lAr* on the predicate is optional; if not, it is needed for people. It is never doubled: *\*yorgunlarlar*.

**Do not stress the ending.** *\*Yorgunúm* puts the stress where Turkish does not. The ending is unstressable: *yorgúnum*.

**Do not use *yaşlı* and *eski* the wrong way round.** For people, "old" is *yaşlı*: *Adam yaşlı* "the man is old". *Eski* is for things (tur-02-01).

## Connections

Spanish works the same way: *soy médico*, with the person in the ending and *yo* only for contrast. Japanese and Korean also leave out "I" and "you", but they have no person ending to recover them from; the listener relies on context. Turkish states the person every time, in the ending, and drops only the pronoun.

## Exercises

1. Say "I am ready", "you (one friend) are ready" and "we are ready".
2. Say "you are engineers" to two people, and "you are an engineer" politely to one person.
3. Take apart *mutlusunuz* and *gençsin*: stem, ending, person, number.
4. Build "I am a child" from *çocuk*. What happens to the *k*, and why?
5. Translate *Ben açım, Ali yorgun.* Why does *yorgun* have no ending?
6. A learner writes *\*Biz öğrenciler* for "we are students". Correct it and give two reasons.
7. Say "they are married" with no subject word, then with *onlar*. What changes?
8. Where is the stress in *hazırım*, and what rule puts it there?

## Worked answers

1. *Hazırım*, *hazırsın*, *hazırız*. *Hazır* ends in a consonant, so no buffer. Its last vowel is *ı*, back and unrounded, so every *I* becomes *ı*.
2. *Siz mühendissiniz* in both cases. *Siz* covers several people and also one person addressed politely, and its ending is *-sInIz*. After *i* the vowels are *i*: *mühendis-siniz*. The double *s* is the stem's *s* followed by the ending's *s*.
3. *Mutlu-sunuz*: stem *mutlu* "happy", ending *-sInIz*, second person plural, "you are happy" (several people, or politely). *Genç-sin*: stem *genç* "young", ending *-sIn*, second person singular, "you are young".
4. *Çocuğum*. The ending *-(y)Im* starts with a vowel after a consonant, and *çocuk* is a word of two syllables ending in a vowel plus *k*, so the *k* becomes *ğ* (part 1, lesson 6). Harmony after *u* gives *-um*.
5. "I am hungry; Ali is tired." *Açım* has the first person ending. *Yorgun* belongs to *Ali*, a third person singular subject, which takes no ending.
6. *Biz öğrenciyiz.* First, the predicate needs the "we" ending *-(y)Iz*, with the buffer *y* after the vowel. Second, the noun stays singular; a plural noun would mean "the students".
7. *Evliler*: with no subject word and people meant, *-lAr* is required. *Onlar evli* or *Onlar evliler*: once *onlar* is said, the ending becomes optional.
8. *Hazírım*, on the syllable before the ending. Personal endings are unstressable and push the stress onto the syllable before them.
