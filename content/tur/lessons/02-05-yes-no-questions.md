## The idea

A **yes-no question** is one that can be answered "yes" or "no": "Is Ali ill?" English builds it by moving the verb to the front. Spanish and French can keep the order and change the voice, or add a question word such as *est-ce que* at the start.

Turkish keeps the statement exactly as it is and adds one small word, ***mI***, after it:

*Ali hasta.* "Ali is ill." *Ali hasta mı?* "Is Ali ill?"

Nothing moves. Nothing is added at the front. The question is the statement plus *mI*.

**mI is a clitic.** A **clitic** is a small word that is written separately but is pronounced as part of the word before it, leaning on it. *MI* shows this in two ways. Its vowel follows four-way harmony with the word before, as if it were a suffix: *hasta mı*, *evli mi*, *yorgun mu*, *Türk mü* (wiktionary, *mi*, usage notes). And it never takes stress: it pushes the stress onto the syllable before it, as tur-01-07 promised (goksel2005 §4.3.2). Göksel and Kerslake call it an unstressable clitic (goksel2005 §19.1).

**mI marks the word in question.** Japanese か and Korean -까 close the whole sentence. Turkish *mI* attaches to whichever part of the sentence you are asking about. Placed after the predicate, it asks about the whole statement: *Ali hasta mı?* "Is Ali ill?" Placed after another word, it asks about that word: *Ali mi hasta?* "Is it Ali who is ill?" (goksel2005 §§19.1.1.1, 19.1.4). Because the predicate comes last, a question about the whole sentence ends in *mI*, and looks like the Japanese and Korean pattern. A question about one word does not.

**The voice.** The syllable just before *mI* gets a high pitch, and the voice falls after it (goksel2005 §19.1.1.1). The rise is before the particle, not at the very end as in an English question.

## How it is built

To ask a yes-no question about a whole nominal sentence, take four steps.

1. **Build the statement**, without its personal ending: *rahat* "comfortable".
2. **Put *mI* after the predicate**, as a separate word.
3. **Harmonise *mI* with the last vowel before it.** The last vowel of *rahat* is *a*, so *mı*: *rahat mı*.
4. **Put the personal ending on *mI*, not on the predicate.** "You (polite)" is *-sInIz*: *Rahat mısınız?* "Are you comfortable?" After *mI*, which ends in a vowel, *-(y)Im* and *-(y)Iz* take the buffer *y*: *Rahat mıyım?* "Am I comfortable?", *Rahat mıyız?* "Are we comfortable?"

The six forms after *hasta* are *hasta mıyım*, *hasta mısın*, *hasta mı*, *hasta mıyız*, *hasta mısınız*, *hastalar mı* (wiktionary, *mi*; goksel2005 §11.1.1.5).

**Why the ending moves.** It moves for the same reason it moved onto *değil* in the last lesson: the personal ending goes on the last piece of the predicate, and *mI* is now that last piece. Göksel and Kerslake state that *mI* comes immediately before these person endings (goksel2005 §11.1.1.5).

**The one exception is "they".** The "they" ending *-lAr* comes before *mI*, not after it: *Hastalar mı?* "Are they ill?" Göksel and Kerslake give the same order for every predicate (goksel2005 §11.1.1.5). So *mI* sits before *-(y)Im*, *-sIn*, *-(y)Iz* and *-sInIz*, but after *-lAr*.

**Negative questions.** Put *mI* after *değil*, and the ending after *mI*: *Hasta değil misin?* "Aren't you ill?" *Değil* ends in *i*, so the forms are *değil miyim*, *değil misin*, *değil mi*, *değil miyiz*, *değil misiniz*, *değil(ler) mi* (wiktionary, *değil*).

**Questioning one word.** Put *mI* straight after that word, and keep the predicate last: *Ali mi hasta?* The stress falls on the word before *mI*, on its last syllable: *Alí mi hasta?* Göksel and Kerslake note that such a word normally stands just before the predicate, and never after it (goksel2005 §19.1.1.1).

**Stress inside the word.** Because *mI* refuses stress, the syllable before it takes it: *hastá mı*, *yorgún mu*. Endings after *mI* do not change this: *hastá mısınız*. A word with fixed stress may keep it or let it move to the syllable before *mI*: Göksel and Kerslake give both *İstánbul mu?* and *İstanbúl mu?* (goksel2005 §4.3.2.1).

**Answering.** *Evet* "yes" or *hayır* "no", usually followed by the statement or its denial: *Evet, rahatım.* *Hayır, rahat değilim.*

## Where it applies

- **Asking about a whole nominal sentence**: *Ali hasta mı?*, *Bu kitap pahalı mı?*
- **Every person**: *hasta mıyım*, *mısın*, *mı*, *mıyız*, *mısınız*, *hastalar mı*.
- **Polite "you" to one person**: *Meşgul müsünüz?* is the normal way to ask a stranger.
- **Negative questions**: *Hasta değil misin?* "Aren't you ill?"
- **Tag questions**: a statement, a comma and *değil mi?* asks for agreement, like "isn't it?" or "aren't you?". Göksel and Kerslake call it the ordinary tag, used when you believe the statement is true; it follows positive and negative statements alike (goksel2005 §19.1.1.2): *Yorgunsun, değil mi?*, *Hasta değilsin, değil mi?*
- ***Öyle mi?*** "Is that so? Really?", a tag or a reply to news, especially surprising news (goksel2005 §19.1.1.2).
- **Questioning one part**: *Ali mi hasta?* "Is it Ali who is ill?"; *Çok mu pahalı?* "Is it *very* expensive?"
- **A noun phrase**: *mI* goes after the noun, the head of the phrase, even if you are asking about its adjective; stress shows which part you mean (goksel2005 §19.1.3): *İyi bir öğretmen mi?*, with stress on *iyi*, asks "Is he a *good* teacher?".
- **Either-or questions**: *mI* after each alternative, with optional *yoksa* "or" (goksel2005 §19.1.2): *Ayşe Fransız mı, yoksa İspanyol mu?*
- **With *acaba* "I wonder"**, at the start or the end: *Acaba Ali hasta mı?*, *Ali hasta mı acaba?* "Is Ali ill, I wonder?" (goksel2005 §19.5.2).
- **"Is there?"**: *var mı?*, *yok mu?* (tur-02-06).
- **On verbs**: *Geliyor musun?* "Are you coming?" (tur-02-09), with the same placement of the ending as on nouns.
- **Not with question words**: *ne* "what" makes its own question, *Bu ne?* (more in tur-03-12).

## Watch out

**Do not write *mI* joined to the word.** It harmonises like a suffix, but it is spelt as a separate word: *\*Hastamı?* is wrong. Write *Hasta mı?* An ending that follows it is joined to it: *mısın*, *misiniz*.

**Do not take the vowel from the wrong place.** *MI* harmonises with the last vowel of the word just before it, not with the subject: *\*Ali yorgun mi?* is wrong. *Ali yorgun mu?*

**Do not leave the ending on the predicate.** *\*Yorgunsun mu?* keeps "you are tired" and adds *mI* after it. Except for "they", the ending goes on *mI*: *Yorgun musun?*

**Do not put "they" after *mI*.** *\*Mutlu mılar?* follows the pattern of the other endings, but *-lAr* comes first: *Mutlular mı?*

**Do not start the sentence with the question marker.** French *Est-ce que* and Spanish *¿* invite a front-loaded question. *\*Mı Ali hasta?* is impossible: *mI* needs a word before it to lean on. Say *Ali hasta mı?*

**Do not put *mI* after the predicate to question another word.** To ask "Is it *Ali* who is ill?", *mI* goes after *Ali*: *Ali mi hasta?* A *mI*-phrase cannot come after the predicate (goksel2005 §19.1.1.1): *\*Hasta Ali mi?* is wrong.

**Do not put *mI* between an adjective and its noun.** *\*İyi mi bir öğretmen?* is wrong. *MI* goes after the noun: *İyi bir öğretmen mi?*, with stress on *iyi* if the adjective is the point.

**Do not add *mI* to a question word.** *\*Bu ne mi?* doubles the question. *Bu ne?*

**Do not confuse a negative question with a tag.** *Ali öğretmen değil mi?*, said as one phrase, is "Isn't Ali a teacher?". *Ali öğretmen, değil mi?*, with a comma and a pause, is "Ali is a teacher, isn't he?" Göksel and Kerslake note that writing separates them by the comma, speech by the intonation (goksel2005 §19.1.1.2).

**Do not stress *mI* or raise the voice at the end.** *\*Hasta mí?* is wrong. The high pitch goes on the syllable before *mI*, *hastá mı*, and the voice then falls.

## Connections

Japanese か and Korean -까 also leave the statement intact and add one piece. The difference is placement: they close the whole sentence, while Turkish *mI* attaches to the questioned word and only lands at the end when that word is the predicate. To question one part, Turkish moves the particle. The resemblance is typological, not a sign of shared ancestry.

## Exercises

1. Turn into questions: *Ayşe öğrenci*, *Sen hazırsın*, *Biz emin değiliz*.
2. Ask a stranger politely: "Are you a tourist?"
3. Take apart *misafir misiniz*: which words, which pieces, and why does the ending come after *mi*?
4. Ask "Are they married?" without a subject word.
5. Ask "Is it the *teacher* who is tired?" and say where *mI* goes and why.
6. A learner writes *\*Rahatsın mı?* for "Are you comfortable?". Correct it.
7. Ask "Is your father Turkish or German?" using *Türk*, *Alman* and *yoksa*, with *baban* "your father".
8. What is the difference between *Müdür emin değil mi?* and *Müdür emin, değil mi?*
9. Why is *\*Bu ne mi?* wrong?

## Worked answers

1. *Ayşe öğrenci mi?* After *i*, *mi*. *Sen hazır mısın?* The ending *-sIn* moves from *hazır* to *mI*, which harmonises with the *ı* of *hazır*: *mısın*. *Biz emin değil miyiz?* *MI* follows *değil*, and *-(y)Iz* takes the buffer *y* after the vowel of *mi* (tur-02-04, this lesson).
2. *Turist misiniz?* Polite "you" to one person is *siz*, so the ending is *-sInIz*; after *mi* it is *-siniz*. A nationality or role takes no *bir* (tur-02-01).
3. Two words: *misafir* "guest", and *mi-siniz*, the question particle plus the second person plural ending. The ending follows *mi* because the personal ending always sits on the last piece of the predicate, and *mI* is now that piece. "Are you guests?" or, politely, "Are you a guest?"
4. *Evliler mi?* With no subject word and people meant, *-lAr* is required (tur-02-02), and it is the one ending that comes before *mI*.
5. *Öğretmen mi yorgun?* *MI* goes straight after the word in question, the subject *öğretmen*, and the predicate *yorgun* stays last. The stress falls on *öğretmen*.
6. *Rahat mısın?* The ending goes after *mI*, not on the predicate. *MI* harmonises with the *a* of *rahat*: *mı*, and the ending follows it: *mısın*.
7. *Baban Türk mü, yoksa Alman mı?* Each alternative gets its own *mI*, harmonised with its own word: *mü* after *ü*, *mı* after *a*. *Yoksa* "or" is optional (goksel2005 §19.1.2).
8. *Müdür emin değil mi?*, one phrase, asks "Isn't the director sure?". *Müdür emin, değil mi?*, with a comma, states "The director is sure" and adds a tag asking for agreement: "isn't he?" (goksel2005 §19.1.1.2).
9. *Ne* "what" already makes the sentence a question; a yes-no particle cannot be added to a question-word question. *Bu ne?*
