## The idea

So far every sentence has had two or three parts: a subject, sometimes a place, and a predicate at the end. This lesson states the rules behind that order, and extends them to sentences with a verb and an object.

Two terms first. The **verb** is the word for an action or a state: "read", "drink", "write". The **object** is the thing the action is done to: in "Ali reads a book", "a book" is the object.

**The neutral order is subject, object, verb.** *Ali kitap okuyor*: "Ali book reads", Ali is reading a book. Göksel and Kerslake call this the **unmarked** order: the order of a sentence that opens a conversation, where nothing is assumed and nothing is singled out (goksel2005 §23.1). Nominal sentences follow the same principle, subject then predicate: *Hava çok soğuk*. WALS classifies Turkish as SOV, like Japanese and Korean (wals 81A).

**The order is free, but not meaningless.** Much of the work English does with position, Turkish does with endings: a place carries *-DA* wherever it stands, and part 3 adds endings for specific objects, goals and sources. So Turkish can move words around. It uses the freedom to show what is *new* and what is *already known*. Göksel and Kerslake describe three areas in the sentence (goksel2005 §§23.1, 23.3):

1. **The start** holds the topic: what the sentence is about, usually known already.
2. **The position just before the predicate** holds the most important new information, and it carries the main stress of the sentence.
3. **The area after the predicate**, in speech only, holds what is already known or added as an afterthought. Nothing there is stressed.

**Why the verb comes last.** Turkish puts the word that is described or governed after the words that depend on it, again and again: the adjective before its noun (*eski ev*), the place word before *var*, the object before the verb, and later the owner before the thing owned and the description before the noun it describes. Linguists call a language that does this consistently **head-final**: the main word of each phrase, its **head**, comes at the end. The verb last is one case of a general design, which is why the same "last word decides" habit runs through the whole guide.

The examples in this lesson that contain a verb use the form *-(I)yor*, which is taught in the next lesson. They are marked as previews. Read *okuyor* as "is reading" and *-um* as "I" for now.

## How it is built

To build a neutral sentence, put the parts in this order:

1. **The subject**, if it is said at all: *Ayşe*.
2. **Time words**, either before or after the subject: *bugün* "today". Göksel and Kerslake note that time comes before place, and the broader time before the narrower one (goksel2005 §23.2.4).
3. **Place words**: *kütüphanede*.
4. **A bare object**, straight before the verb: *kitap*.
5. **The predicate**, last: *okuyor*.

*Ayşe bugün kütüphanede kitap okuyor.* "Ayşe is reading (a book) in the library today."

**The slot before the verb is special.** Some things must stand there, with nothing between them and the predicate except a particle such as *mI*. The main ones, in Göksel and Kerslake's list (goksel2005 §23.2.1):

- **A bare object**, one with no case ending: *kitap okuyor*, *çay içiyor*, *mektup yazıyor*. Such an object is not a particular book or letter; it fuses with the verb into one idea, "book-reading", "letter-writing".
- **The predicate noun or adjective** of a sentence with a verb of being, met in part 4.
- **A new, indefinite subject**, which tends to stand there (goksel2005 §23.2.1); in an existential sentence the thing that exists takes this slot (goksel2005 §23.1.2.2): *Bahçede bir kedi var*, where *bir kedi* is the new thing and sits before *var*.

**Where the stress goes.** In a neutral sentence with a verb, the main stress falls on the word just before the verb (goksel2005 §23.1): *Ayşe kütüphanede kiTAP okuyor*. In a positive existential it falls on the thing that exists, which is the same slot; in "x is y" sentences on the predicate; in negatives on *değil* or *yok* (tur-02-04, tur-02-06).

**To give a word extra weight**, which Göksel and Kerslake call **focus**, move it into the slot just before the predicate and stress it, or stress it where it is (goksel2005 §23.3.1). A bare object never gives up that slot, so for now practise focus in sentences without one: *Bahçede ALİ var* "it is *Ali* who is in the garden".

**To background a word**, in speech, put it after the predicate: *Çok güzel bu bahçe* "It's lovely, this garden." Göksel and Kerslake note this is a spoken and informal device; formal writing keeps the predicate last (goksel2005 §23.3.2).

## Where it applies

- **Every sentence ends with its predicate** in neutral speech and in all formal writing: a verb (*okuyor*), a noun or adjective (*güzel*), a place word (*bahçede*), *var*, *yok* or *değil*.
- **The subject opens the sentence**, when it is said: *Ali kitap okuyor*.
- **Time before place**: *Akşam evde*. Time words can also precede the subject: *Yarın Ali okulda değil*.
- **A bare object directly before the verb**: *kitap okuyor*, *çay içiyor*.
- **A new, indefinite thing directly before *var***: *Bahçede bir kedi var*.
- **The topic first, the news last**: *Kedi bahçede* (known cat, new place) against *Bahçede bir kedi var* (known place, new cat).
- **Focus by position and stress**: *Bahçede ALİ var*.
- **Questions**: a word carrying *mI*, or a question word, takes the focus slot, never the area after the predicate (goksel2005 §§19.1.1.1, 23.3.2): *Ayşe mektup mu yazıyor?*
- **Afterthoughts in speech**: *Çok güzel bu bahçe*. If the predicate itself comes first, it carries the stress (goksel2005 §23.3).
- **Inside phrases, the head is last**: *eski ev*, *çok güzel*, *iki kitap*. Later lessons add more of the same: the owner before the owned (tur-03-05), postpositions after their noun (part 5), relative clauses before their noun (part 6).

## Watch out

**Do not put the verb in the middle.** English, Spanish and French are subject-verb-object. *\*Ali okuyor kitap* is not a neutral sentence; at best it is an informal afterthought, and a bare object is not normally backgrounded this way. Say *Ali kitap okuyor*.

**Do not separate a bare object from its verb.** *\*Ali kitap bugün okuyor* puts a time word into the slot that belongs to *kitap*. Time words go earlier: *Ali bugün kitap okuyor*.

**Do not put the place last as in English.** "Ayşe is drinking tea in the kitchen" invites *\*Ayşe çay içiyor mutfakta* as a neutral sentence. The place comes before the object: *Ayşe mutfakta çay içiyor*.

**Do not move a bare object to the front for emphasis.** *\*Kitap Ali okuyor* does not mean "Ali is reading a *book*". A bare object stays before the verb; to stress it, stress it there: *Ali kiTAP okuyor*.

**Do not put *mI* or a question word after the predicate.** *\*Yazıyor Ayşe mektup mu?* is wrong. The questioned word stands before the predicate.

**Do not move words after the predicate in formal writing.** *Çok güzel bu bahçe* is natural in conversation; in an essay write *Bu bahçe çok güzel*.

**Do not read word order as grammar in the English way.** Swapping *Kedi bahçede* and *Bahçede kedi* does not swap subject and place: the locative ending shows which word is the place. What changes is what counts as known and what as new.

**Do not confuse Turkish freedom with Japanese freedom.** Japanese also puts the verb last and moves the rest fairly freely, but it marks most roles with a particle. Turkish leaves the subject and non-specific objects bare, so their position matters more: a bare object must stay next to the verb.

## Connections

Japanese and Korean share the subject-object-verb order and the head-last design: 本を読む and 책을 읽다 put the object before the verb, as *kitap okuyor* does. All three use the position before the verb for the most important information. WALS records Turkish, Japanese and Korean alike as SOV with postpositions (wals 81A, 85A); this is a shared type, not proof of common descent. Spanish and French are verb-medial, so the habit to break is putting the verb second.

## Exercises

1. Put in neutral order: *okuyor / Mehmet / gazete / şimdi*.
2. Put in neutral order: *yok / bugün / kütüphanede / toplantı*.
3. Put in neutral order: *içiyor / Ali / bahçede / çay / akşam*.
4. Which word carries the main stress in *Ali mutfakta çay içiyor*, and why?
5. You know there is a dog somewhere and want to say it is in the street. Build the sentence with *köpek* and *sokak*.
6. You are looking at a street and want to say a dog is there. Build that sentence.
7. A learner writes *\*Ayşe yazıyor mektup* for "Ayşe is writing a letter". Correct it and explain.
8. Rewrite *Bu film çok ilginç* in the spoken backgrounded style, and say where the stress falls.

## Worked answers

1. *Mehmet şimdi gazete okuyor.* Subject, time word, bare object, verb. *Gazete* must touch the verb.
2. *Bugün kütüphanede toplantı yok.* Time before place, then the thing, then *yok* last. "There's no meeting at the library today."
3. *Akşam Ali bahçede çay içiyor* (or *Ali akşam bahçede çay içiyor*). The time word goes before or after the subject, the place follows, and the bare object *çay* stands against the verb. "Ali drinks tea in the garden in the evening" or "Ali is drinking tea in the garden this evening".
4. *Çay*, the word just before the verb: in a neutral verbal sentence the main stress falls there (goksel2005 §23.1).
5. *Köpek sokakta.* The dog is known, so it is the topic and comes first; the place is the new information and is the predicate.
6. *Sokakta bir köpek var.* The street is known and comes first; the new thing comes just before *var*.
7. *Ayşe mektup yazıyor.* The verb goes last, and a bare object stands straight before it.
8. *Çok ilginç bu film.* The predicate comes first and carries the stress; *bu film*, already known, follows unstressed. In writing, keep *Bu film çok ilginç*.
