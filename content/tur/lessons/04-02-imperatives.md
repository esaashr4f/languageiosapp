## The idea

The shortest finite verb in Turkish is the bare stem. *Gel!* "Come!" *Otur!* "Sit down!" *Bak!* "Look!" Nothing is added: the stem itself is the command to one person you address as *sen*.

Last lesson said that every finite verb fills slot 5, the tense slot, with two exceptions. These are the exceptions. Göksel and Kerslake note that the second-person imperative and the third-person command forms are the only finite verbs without a tense suffix (goksel2005 §8.2.3). A command is not a statement about when something happens. It asks for the action, so the action alone is enough. The person slot does the rest: a set of command endings says who is to act.

There are four forms you need:

- **bare stem**: one person, familiar (*sen*): *Gel!*
- ***-(y)In***: several people, or one person politely (*siz*): *Gelin!*
- ***-(y)InIz***: the same, but formal, as in notices and announcements: *Geliniz.*
- ***-sIn***: "let him, her, it do it", "I want him to": *Gelsin.* For "them", ***-sInlAr***: *Gelsinler.*

Göksel and Kerslake describe *-(y)In* as the standard plural and polite imperative in everyday life, and *-(y)InIz* as mainly for formal public commands (goksel2005 §21.4.4.2).

**Forbidding** uses the negative you already know. *-mA* goes in slot 3, as always, and the command ending follows: *Gelme!* "Don't come!", *Gelmeyin!* "Don't come (polite)!", *Gelmesin* "He's not to come." There is no separate negative command form, unlike the Spanish switch from *ven* to *no vengas*.

A fifth form is common in speech: ***-sAnA*** and ***-sAnIzA***, a nudge rather than an order: *Otursana!* "Why don't you sit down!" Göksel and Kerslake analyse it as the conditional *-sA*, a person ending and an exclamation *A* fused together, and note that it often carries impatience that the hearer has not already done the obvious thing (goksel2005 §§8.4, 21.4.4.2).

## How it is built

To give a command, ask three questions.

1. **To whom?** One friend: bare stem. Several people, or anyone you call *siz*: *-(y)In*. A sign or an announcement: *-(y)InIz*. Someone not present: *-sIn* or *-sInlAr*.
2. **Positive or negative?** For a negative, add *-mA* after the stem first.
3. **What does the stem end in?** The buffer *y* of *-(y)In* appears only after a vowel.

**The polite form -(y)In** has four-way harmony:

- after a consonant: *gel-in*, *otur-un*, *bak-ın*, *gör-ün*;
- after a vowel, with *y*: *bekle-yin*, *oku-yun*, *söyle-yin*;
- after the negative: *gel-me-yin*, *bak-ma-yın*.

The usual consonant changes apply before the vowel: *git-* gives *gidin*, *et-* gives *edin*: *Yardım edin!* "Help!" The two one-letter stems raise their vowel, as they did before *-yor*: *de-* gives *diyin* and *ye-* gives *yiyin* (Wiktionary's tables for *demek* and *yemek*).

**The third person -sIn** harmonises four ways too and needs no buffer, since it starts with a consonant: *gel-sin*, *otur-sun*, *bak-sın*, *bekle-sin*.

**Stress.** The bare stem is stressed on its last syllable: *otúr*. *-(y)In* and *-(y)InIz* cannot take stress, so it stays on the stem: *otúrun*, *gélin*. Third-person *-sIn* can: *gelsín*. With *-mA*, the stress falls before the negative, as always: *gélme*, *gélmeyin* (goksel2005 §4.3.2). *-sAnA* is stressed on its first syllable: *otursána* (goksel2005 §4.3.1).

A worked chain: "Please don't wait for us" to a stranger. Stem *bekle-*. Negative: *bekle-me-*. Polite: *-(y)In*, with *y* after the vowel: *beklemeyin*. *Lütfen bizi beklemeyin.*

## Where it applies

- **Ordinary commands and instructions** to one friend: *Gel*, *Bak*, *Dur!* "Stop!", *Sus!* "Be quiet!"
- **Politeness to one person**: *-(y)In* with anyone you would call *siz* (tur-03-10): a stranger, a shopkeeper, an older person. Adding *lütfen* makes it softer still: *Lütfen bekleyin.*
- **Several people**, whatever your relationship: *Çocuklar, yatın!* "Children, go to bed!"
- **Formal and written instructions**: notices, recipes, forms, announcements, exam papers use *-(y)InIz*: *Lütfen dikkat ediniz.*
- **Prohibitions**: *-mA* plus the command ending: *Dokunma*, *Acele etmeyin* "Don't hurry."
- **Wishes and instructions about a third person**: *-sIn*, *-sInlAr*: *Ali gelsin* "I want Ali to come, have Ali come." The plural *-sInlAr* is used when the subject word is absent: *Gelsinler.*
- **Asking what someone wants done**: *-sIn* with *mI*: *Taksi kapıda mı beklesin?*
- **Fixed social phrases**: *Kolay gelsin* to someone working; *Buyurun* "here you are; come in; please go ahead", originally the polite imperative of *buyurmak* "command" (Wiktionary); *Bakın* "excuse me, listen" to get attention.
- **Nudges** among friends and family with *-sAnA*, *-sAnIzA*: *Otursana!*, *Söylesenize!* "Tell us, then!"
- **With noun + etmek verbs**, the ending goes on *etmek*: *Yardım edin*, *Merak etmeyin*, *Acele et.*

## Watch out

**Do not use the infinitive as a command.** English signs say "No smoking" and Spanish recipes say *mezclar*. In Turkish *\*Gelmek!* or *\*Kapıyı kapatmak!* is not a command. Use the imperative: *Gel!*, *Kapıyı kapatın.*

**Do not use the bare stem with strangers.** *\*Bekle!* to a shop assistant sounds like an order to a child. Use *Bekleyin* or *Lütfen bekleyin.* The same goes for *-sAnA*: *\*Otursana* to your boss is rude. Say *Oturun* or *Buyurun, oturun.*

**Do not read -sIn as "you".** *Gelsin* means "let him come", not "come (polite)". English "let" misleads here in another way too. *-sIn* means "I want him to", not "allow him to": "let him go out" in the sense of permission is a different construction (*izin vermek*, part 6).

**Do not use -sIn for "let me" or "let's".** *\*Gelsinim*, *\*gelsiniz* for "let us come" do not exist. "Let me" and "let's" are the optative (tur-04-14): *geleyim*, *gelelim*.

**Do not put the negative after the ending.** *\*Gelinme* or *\*gelin değil* treat "not" as an add-on. *-mA* is slot 3, right after the stem: *Gelmeyin.*

**Do not forget the buffer after a vowel, or add one after a consonant.** *\*Bekleın*, *\*okuun* lack the *y*: *bekleyin*, *okuyun*. *\*Gelyin*, *\*bakyın* add one where none belongs: *gelin*, *bakın*.

**Do not keep the vowel of ye-.** *\*Yeyin* follows the spelling of the stem. Before a suffix beginning with *y*, the Turkish Language Association writes *i* in the stems of *demek* and *yemek* (*diyen*, *diyelim*, *yiyen*, *yiyelim*; tdk spelling rules, "Ünlü daralması"), and Wiktionary gives *yiyin* and *diyin*. For *de-* you will also see *deyin*, which some usage writers prefer; the rule does not list the imperative, so this guide follows Wiktionary's *diyin*. The negative keeps the vowel, because *-mA* is next to the stem: *demeyin*, *yemeyin*.

**Do not forget gid- and ed-.** *\*Gitin*, *\*yardım etin*: before the vowel of *-(y)In* these stems change: *gidin*, *yardım edin*. Before a consonant they do not: *gitsin*, *gitme*, *etmeyin*.

**Do not stress the polite ending.** *\*Gelín*, *\*oturún* put stress on an unstressable suffix. Keep it on the stem: *gélin*, *otúrun*.

## Connections

Japanese and Korean also have a command form without tense: 見ろ (miro) and 봐라 (bwara) are bare, blunt commands, and politeness is added by other endings (〜てください, -세요), much as Turkish adds *-(y)In*. French has the same split as Turkish *sen*/*siz* in *viens*/*venez*, though French uses *venez* both for several people and for one person politely, exactly like *gelin*.

## Exercises

1. Make the polite command "Wait here", and the familiar one.
2. Make "Don't throw it away!" to a friend, and to a stranger.
3. Take apart *Dikkat etmeyin* and say why it is a strange thing to say.
4. Make "Let Ayşe pay the bill" and "Should Ayşe pay the bill?"
5. Make "Eat (polite)!" and "Say it again (polite)!" Explain the stem vowel.
6. A learner says *\*Lütfen bekle* to a waiter and *\*Gelmek!* to a child. Correct both.
7. Make the formal notice "Please don't touch."
8. What is the difference between *Kalksana!* and *Kalkın*?

## Worked answers

1. Polite: *Burada bekleyin.* Familiar: *Burada bekle.* *Bekle-* ends in a vowel, so *-(y)In* takes its *y*.
2. To a friend: *Atma!* To a stranger: *Atmayın!* The negative *-mA* comes first; after its vowel, *-(y)In* takes *y* and harmonises back: *-yın*.
3. *Dikkat et-me-yin*: attention, *et-*, negative, polite command: "Don't be careful!" The form is correct, but the meaning is odd. The ending goes on *etmek*, and *etmek* stays *et-* before the consonant of *-mA*.
4. *Hesabı Ayşe ödesin.* "Should Ayşe pay the bill?" asks the hearer's wish: *Hesabı Ayşe mi ödesin?* Stem *öde-*, *-sin*; *mI* after the word in question.
5. *Yiyin!* and *Tekrar diyin!* The stems *ye-* and *de-* write their vowel as *i* before the *y* of the suffix, as before *-yor* (tur-02-08). For "say it again" Turkish more often uses *söyle-*: *Tekrar söyleyin.*
6. To a waiter: *Lütfen bekleyin.* The bare stem is for people you call *sen*. To a child: *Gel!* The infinitive is never a command.
7. *Lütfen dokunmayınız.* Formal *-(y)InIz*, after the negative, with *y*.
8. *Kalksana!* is a nudge to one friend: "Get up, will you!", often impatient. *Kalkın* is a plain command to several people, or a polite one to one person.
