What Georgian is

Place Georgian in time, space and family, and know which Georgian this guide teaches

A language of the South Caucasus

Georgian is the language of Georgia, the country between the Black Sea and the Caspian, south of the main range of the Caucasus mountains. It is the official language of the state and the first language of most of its people. A recent estimate puts its native speakers at about 3.8 million (Ethnologue, as reported by Wikipedia's article on the language). Small Georgian-speaking communities also live outside the country: in north-eastern Turkey, in Fereydan in Iran, and in the Zaqatala region of Azerbaijan, where the dialect is called Ingilo. The Iranian community descends from people deported from eastern Georgia by Shah Abbas in the seventeenth century.

Georgians call their language kartuli, their people kartveli and their country sakartvelo, "the place where Georgians live". All three words are built on the name of Kartli, the central region of the country, which Greek and Roman writers called Iberia.

A family of four

Georgian is not Indo-European. It is the largest member of a small family of its own, called Kartvelian or South Caucasian. The other members are Mingrelian (also spelt Megrelian), Laz and Svan. Mingrelian and Svan are spoken in western Georgia, Laz mainly on the Black Sea coast of Turkey. Some linguists, especially in Georgia, treat Mingrelian and Laz as two dialects of one language, Zan.

The four are related to each other as Spanish, French and Italian are, but far more distantly. Svan split off first; later Georgian and Zan separated.

No one has proved that Kartvelian is related to any other family. The Caucasus is home to two or three other native families, all in the north, and the idea that Georgian is related to them, or to Basque, has been argued many times without success. Treat Kartvelian as a family on its own. Its speakers have lived for millennia beside Greeks, Persians, Arabs, Turks and, more recently, Russians, and Georgian has borrowed words from all of them.

Fifteen centuries of writing

Georgian has been written since the fifth century AD. No other native language of the Caucasus has a literary tradition of anything like that length.

The kingdom of Kartli adopted Christianity in the fourth century, in the 330s. The alphabet was most probably created soon after, to write the Bible and the liturgy. The oldest surviving inscriptions date from about 430, at a Georgian monastery in the Holy Land, and from 494, at the Sioni church of Bolnisi, south of Tbilisi. Who made the alphabet, and on what model, is debated: lesson 1-8 sets out the evidence.

The first original work of Georgian literature, The Martyrdom of Saint Shushanik, was written between 476 and 483. The great work of the Middle Ages is Shota Rustaveli's epic The Man in the Panther's Skin (vepxisṭq̇aosani), about 1,600 four-line stanzas composed around 1200 in the reign of Queen Tamar.

Historians of the language divide its written history into three periods.

  • Old Georgian, from the fifth century to the eleventh or twelfth. Its texts are mostly religious: Bible translations, saints' lives, hymns.
  • Middle Georgian, from the twelfth century to the eighteenth. Rustaveli belongs here.
  • Modern Georgian, from the nineteenth century on.

The sources disagree slightly about where to draw the lines. Hewitt ends Old Georgian in the eleventh century, and Bolkvadze and Kiziria in the twelfth. Hewitt also warns that the language changed gradually and that the periods are a convenience, not clean breaks.

The modern standard was shaped in the second half of the nineteenth century. By then the church's written norm was far from how people spoke. Writers led by Ilia Chavchavadze (1837–1907) set out to write the language as it was spoken, as part of a national revival. The Georgian you will learn is the result.

Which Georgian this guide teaches

This guide teaches modern standard Georgian: the language of schools, newspapers, books, signs and educated speech. It is based on the speech of Kartli, the region that contains Tbilisi, the capital. There are three reasons for the choice.

First, every grammar and dictionary describes it, so every form in this guide can be checked.

Second, it is what you meet in Georgia, in print and on screen.

Third, the older language and the dialects are easier to learn from it than the other way round.

Everyday Tbilisi speech differs from the standard in small, regular ways. Where an example uses a spoken form, its note says "colloquial". Old and Middle Georgian appear only in part 7 and in the last readings of part 8, clearly labelled. Dialects are described once, in part 7.

What kind of language it is

Four facts shape what follows.

The sounds are new, the spelling is easy. Georgian has five plain vowels and consonants English lacks, including a whole series made with a closed throat. They are often grouped in clusters that look unpronounceable and are not. The alphabet has 33 letters, one sound each, and no capitals. Part 1 teaches it all.

Nouns are regular. A noun has seven case endings, which say what it is doing in the sentence. There is no grammatical gender: the same word, is, means "he", "she" and "it". The one distinction nouns care about is between people and everything else, the difference between the question words vin "who" and ra "what". There is no word for "the".

The verb is the centre. A single Georgian verb names its subject and its objects, says where the action goes, whom it is for, and when it happens. And the tense of the verb decides which case the nouns around it take. This is the organising idea of the language. The next lesson shows it at work.

Word order is free, with the verb often last. A common order is subject, object, verb, but others are possible, because the endings and the verb carry the meaning. A change of order changes the emphasis.

What you already know

You come with real advantages. Japanese and Korean have taught you to put the verb last, to attach small endings to nouns instead of using prepositions, and to leave out "I" and "you" when the verb makes them clear. Georgian does all three. Korean also has a three-way contrast in its stops, which is the nearest thing in your languages to Georgian's.

Spanish gives you the idea of a person in the dative who "receives" a feeling: in me gusta the thing liked is the subject and the person liking is the indirect object. Georgian builds a whole class of verbs, and a whole tense series, on this pattern. French gives you tu and vous; Georgian has the same distinction.

Arabic gives you less than you might expect. Georgian has words from Arabic and Persian, but its grammar is unrelated, and its throat consonants are not the emphatic consonants of Arabic.

None of this is a shortcut past the verb. The verb has to be learnt on its own terms, and the next lesson shows why it deserves the effort.
