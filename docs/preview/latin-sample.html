The organising idea: endings carry the grammar

See how one word changes its ending to do the jobs English gives to word order and helper words

The idea

Every sentence has to tell you who did what to whom. Languages solve this problem in different ways.

English uses position. In "the girl sees the sailor", you know the girl is doing the seeing because she comes before the verb. Swap the two nouns and the meaning flips.

Japanese and Korean use small tags placed after each noun. The tag says "this is the doer" or "this is the thing acted on".

Latin puts the tag inside the word. Each noun ends in a short piece that states its job. Each verb ends in a short piece that states who is acting. Change the job and the ending changes. This guide calls that final, changeable piece the ending, and the part that stays the same the stem.

That is the organising idea of Latin: endings carry the grammar. Almost every lesson in this guide teaches either a new set of endings or a new use for a set you already have.

What word order is for

If order does not say who did what, what does it do? It carries emphasis and flow. A Latin writer puts first what the sentence is about or what he wants you to notice, and tends to keep the verb for the end. The difference between Puella nautam videt and Nautam puella videt is like the difference in English between "The girl sees the sailor" and "It's the sailor the girl sees".

So Latin word order is free, and it is never random. Part 2 gives you the neutral order. Later lessons show what authors do by departing from it.

The verb does it too

The verb ending names the doer, so a separate word for "I", "you" or "she" is usually left out.

Spanish works the same way (ve, vemos), and for the same reason: it inherited the habit, and several of the endings, from Latin.

Why Latin is like this

Latin did not invent this system. It inherited it. The parent language of the Indo-European family already marked jobs with endings, and many of its descendants, old and modern (Greek, Sanskrit, Old English, Russian), do the same. English had such endings a thousand years ago and has lost nearly all of them. The pairs he/him, she/her and who/whom are the clearest of what is left: he is a "doer" form and him an "acted on" form, the same kind of contrast as nauta and nautam.

What this means for you as a reader

Three habits follow, and the sooner you form them the better.

Read the end of every word. The start of a word tells you what it means. The end tells you what it is doing.

Do not read Latin by rearranging it into English order. Take each word as it comes, note its job from its ending, and hold it until the sentence resolves. This feels slow for a week and then becomes the faster way.

Expect one ending to pack several facts together. The -am of nautam says "acted on" and "one" at once. You cannot cut it into a piece for each meaning. Languages built like this are called fusional. It is the main difference from Japanese, Korean or Turkish, where each tag has one meaning and tags are stacked in a row.

The price of the system is that endings must be learnt. The reward is that a Latin sentence can be very short, can put any word in the spotlight, and is rarely ambiguous about who did what.

Stress: the second-from-last rule

Stress any Latin word correctly from its spelling alone

The idea

Stress is the extra force given to one syllable of a word. In English and Spanish you have to learn it word by word, and it can separate words: Spanish término, termino and terminó are three different things.

Latin stress carries no meaning of its own. It is computed from the shape of the word, and the same sum works for every word. That is why the Romans never needed to write it. Once you can weigh syllables, you can stress any word you can spell.

The sum counts from the end of the word and looks at one syllable only. The penult is the second-from-last syllable. The antepenult is the third-from-last.

  • A word of two syllables is stressed on the first.
  • In a longer word, weigh the penult. If it is heavy, it takes the stress.
  • If the penult is light, the stress moves back one place, to the antepenult.

Stress never falls further back than the antepenult, and in a word of more than one syllable it does not fall on the last.

Why this shape? There is a way of stating the three lines as one. Set the last syllable aside. From there, walk back two beats. The stress falls on the syllable where the second beat lands. A heavy penult holds both beats, so the walk ends inside it. A light penult holds only one, so the second beat lands in the syllable before, whatever that syllable weighs. This is a modern description, not something a Roman grammarian said, but it shows that the rule is a count of time and not an arbitrary list.

Roman grammarians state the rule, and the Romance languages confirm it.

Latin had not always worked this way. The usual account is that very early Latin stressed the first syllable of every word. Vowels in the syllables after it were weakened. That is why amīcus "friend" has a, and inimīcus "enemy", built from in- "not" and amīcus, has i. The classical rule had replaced the old one before the classical period.

How it is built

  1. Divide the word into syllables. Use the prose division: a stop with l or r goes to the next syllable.
  2. Count them. One syllable: stress it. Two: stress the first, and stop.
  3. Three or more: find the penult. Ask whether it has a long vowel or a diphthong, or ends in a consonant.
  4. If yes, stress the penult. If no, stress the antepenult.
  5. Ignore everything else. The weight of the last syllable, and of any syllable before the antepenult, plays no part.

Below, the stressed syllable is written in capitals.

Examples

Watch out

A long vowel does not attract the stress. Only the penult is weighed. Cicerō ends in a long vowel and is stressed CI·ce·rō, on a light syllable.

Stress belongs to the form, not to the word. Latin words change their endings, and a longer form has a different penult. Imperātor is im·pe·RĀ·tor. Its form imperātōris, which you have seen in the word list, is im·pe·rā·TŌ·ris.

Words with something attached. A few small words, such as -que "and", are never said alone. They are attached to the end of the word before. Ancient grammarians say the stress then falls on the syllable next to the attachment: po·pu·LUS·que. In that word the ordinary rule gives the same result, because lus is heavy. The rule makes a difference only when that syllable is light: fēmina with -que becomes fē·mi·NA·que, not fē·MI·na·que. This is the traditional rule. Some scholars doubt that it held when that syllable was light.

A handful of words stress the last syllable. They once had a further syllable and kept their stress when it was lost. Illīc "there" (from illīce) is one.

Connections

Spanish and French kept the Latin stressed syllable and lost its unstressed neighbours. A·MĪ·cus gives Spanish amigo and French ami. PO·pu·lus gives pueblo and peuple, and O·cu·lus gives ojo and œil: the light penult has gone. The stress rule usually taught for Modern Standard Arabic is close to the Latin one: unless the last syllable is extra heavy, a heavy penult is stressed, otherwise the antepenult.

Exercises

  1. Divide fortūna and dominus and place the stress. Say which syllable you weighed.
  2. Do the same for imperium. How many syllables has it?
  3. Place the stress in inimīcus and in animus.
  4. Senātus and exercitus both end in -tus. Why are they stressed on different syllables counting from the end?
  5. Use the two-beat walk to explain why lībertās is stressed on ber.
  6. Spanish ojo comes from oculus. Which Latin syllable was lost, and why that one?
  7. A friend stresses Cicerō on the last syllable "because the vowel is long". Correct him.

Worked answers

  1. for·TŪ·na. Three syllables, so the penult tū is weighed. It has a long vowel, so it is heavy and stressed. DO·mi·nus: the penult mi ends in a short vowel and is light. The stress moves back to the antepenult do.
  2. im·PE·ri·um, four syllables. The i and u are separate vowels, so the penult is ri. It is light, and the antepenult pe is stressed.
  3. i·ni·MĪ·cus: the penult has a long vowel. A·ni·mus: the penult ni is light, so the antepenult is stressed.
  4. The ending is the same, but the penults differ. In se·NĀ·tus the penult has a long vowel and is heavy. In ek·SER·ci·tus the penult ci is light, so the stress goes one place further back.
  5. Set aside tās. Walk back two beats. Ber is heavy: its short e is one beat and the r that closes it is the other. Both beats are inside ber, so the stress falls there.
  6. The cu. The word was stressed O·cu·lus, because the penult cu is light. Unstressed syllables were the ones that wore away, and the stressed o survived.
  7. Only the penult is weighed, and the last syllable is never stressed in a word this long. Its penult ce is light, so the stress falls on the antepenult: CI·ce·rō. The final ō is long and unstressed.
