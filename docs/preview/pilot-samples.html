kat-01-05 · Part 1

Mkhedruli, stage one: vowels and sonorants

Read and write the vowel letters and m, n, l, r, v, s, h, and words made of them

The idea

Modern Georgian is written in an alphabet called Mkhedruli (mxedruli). The name comes from mxedari "horseman, knight", and Wiktionary notes it was coined in contrast to the older church script; lesson 1-8 tells that story. Mkhedruli has 33 letters. Hewitt calls the script "fully phonemic": each letter stands for one sound and each sound has one letter. There are no silent letters, no letters with two values, and no spelling rules of the English or French kind. Once you know the letters, you can say any word you read and write any word you hear.

Two more facts make the script easy to start.

It is written from left to right, like English, with spaces between words.

It has no capital letters. Aronson stresses this: every letter has only one form. Sentences and names begin with the same letters as everything else.

What takes practice is that many letters are round and look alike. The key to telling them apart is their height. Imagine the two lines of a school exercise book. Aronson sorts the letters into four groups:

  • letters that sit between the two lines;
  • letters that rise above the upper line;
  • letters that hang below the lower line;
  • letters that do both.

The same round shape can be two different letters depending on where it sits. Always read a letter together with its position.

This lesson teaches twelve letters: the five vowels and seven consonants you already know how to say. With them you can read real words at once. Lessons 1-6 and 1-7 add the rest.

How it is built

Here are the twelve letters, each with its sound, its position and its name. Letter shapes vary between typefaces, so learn each from the examples in your own app as well as from these descriptions.

The vowels

  • ა a: a small hook sitting between the lines. Name: an.
  • ე e: an arch whose right side drops below the lower line and curls back. Name: en.
  • ი i: a plain arch, like a Latin n, between the lines. Name: in.
  • ო o: a rounded arch with its left side curled in, between the lines. Name: on.
  • უ u: like ე with a second arch attached on its left; hangs below the line. Name: uni.

The consonants

  • მ m: a round bowl with a stem rising above the line on the right. Name: man.
  • ნ n: a round bowl with a stem rising above the line on the left, like a 6. Name: nar.
  • ლ l: a wide letter of small arches whose left end sweeps below the line. Name: las.
  • რ r: like ო, but its curl rises above the upper line. Name: rae.
  • ვ v: a 3 that hangs below the line. Name: vin.
  • ს s: a U whose left arm rises above the line. Name: san.
  • ჰ h: a 3 shape that rises above the line. Name: hae.

The letter names come from Wiktionary, which gives each with its IPA. They are useful labels when you talk about spelling. Most of them end in -n.

To read a word, take three steps.

  1. Find each letter's height first, then its shape.
  2. Say each letter's sound in turn. Nothing is silent.
  3. Count the vowels to know the syllables, as in lesson 1-1.

To write a word, reverse it: say the word slowly, write one letter for each sound, and check the heights.

Examples

Each example is a word made only of this lesson's letters, glossed by meaning.

Watch out

ი is not n. The arch ი looks like a Latin n and is the vowel i. The Georgian n is ნ, the bowl with a tall stem.

ო and რ. They differ only in height: ო stays between the lines, რ climbs above. In fast handwriting, check the top.

ვ and ჰ. Both are 3-like. ვ hangs below the line; ჰ rises above it.

No capitals, so no help with names. nino (a woman's name) is written with the same letters as any other word. Aronson warns that you must learn to recognise names from context.

Connections

Korean Hangul and Georgian Mkhedruli are both alphabets where letter and sound match almost perfectly, so Korean readers know the feeling: a week of effort, then you can sound out anything. Unlike Hangul, Mkhedruli writes letters in a row, not in syllable blocks. Arabic readers will find it easier than Arabic script: no joining forms, and vowels are always written.

Exercises

  1. Read aloud and transliterate: ლომი, ნავი, მელა, ერი.
  2. Write in Mkhedruli: mama, ena, suli, salami.
  3. Sort these letters by height into the four groups you know so far: ა ე ი ო უ მ ნ ლ რ ვ ს ჰ.
  4. A learner reads ნისლი as "insli". Which letter has he misread, and how?
  5. How do you tell ო from რ, and ვ from ჰ?
  6. Spell marili aloud using the letter names.
  7. How many syllables has მარილი, and how can you tell from the letters alone?

Worked answers

  1. lomi "lion", navi "boat", mela "fox", eri "nation".
  2. მამა, ენა, სული, სალამი.
  3. Between the lines: ა ი ო. Above: მ ნ რ ს ჰ. Below: ე უ ლ ვ. None of these twelve letters goes both above and below; you meet those in the next lessons.
  4. He has read the first letter, ნ (n), as if it were ი (i), and the ი as n: he has taken the Latin look-alike for the sound. ნ is the bowl with a tall stem; ი is the plain arch.
  5. ო stays between the lines and რ rises above. ვ hangs below the line and ჰ rises above it.
  6. man, an, rae, in, las, in.
  7. Three: it has three vowel letters, ა, ი and ი, and every vowel letter is one syllable.
hbo-01-01 · Part 1

The consonant letters and the direction of writing

Recognise the 22 letters and their five final forms, and read a line from right to left

The idea

The Hebrew alphabet has twenty-two letters, and every one of them is a consonant. There is no letter for a, e, i, o or u. A Hebrew scribe wrote the skeleton of each word and left the reader to supply the vowels from knowledge of the language. That works better than it sounds: the consonants carry the root, and a reader who knows the patterns can usually hear the vowels.

The vowel signs you see in printed Bibles came a thousand years later (lesson 3). This lesson is about the consonants alone. The examples below are printed with their vowel marks, as all Hebrew in this guide is. Ignore the marks for now and look at the letters. The transliteration shows you the whole word.

Hebrew is written and read right to left. A book opens from what an English reader thinks of as the back, and each line starts at the right margin.

Each letter has a name, and in most cases the name begins with the letter's own sound. Yod, the letter for y, has the same consonants as yāḏ "hand"; ʿayin has those of ʿayin "eye"; resh is close to rōʾš "head". Gesenius thought the shapes began as rough pictures of these objects, but warned that some names may be later guesses.

Where the letters come from

The alphabet was invented in Canaan in the second millennium BC, and all its early users wrote only consonants. Greek, and through Greek the Latin alphabet you are reading now, descend from the same Phoenician source, which is why alpha beta echoes alef bet.

The letters in this guide are not the shapes the earliest biblical scribes used. Until the Babylonian exile, Hebrew was written in an older script, now called Palaeo-Hebrew. You can see it on the Siloam tunnel inscription in Jerusalem (eighth century BC) and, in a closely related form, on the stone of the Moabite king Mesha (ninth century BC). After the exile, Jews gradually switched to the script of Aramaic, the language of the Persian empire. Out of that Aramaic hand grew the square script (in Hebrew kəṯāḇ ʾaššûrî, "Assyrian writing"), which was settled by about the third century BC.

So is the square script the "real" Hebrew script? Both are. The older script is the native one, and it lived on: the Samaritans kept a form of it, it was put on Jewish coins in the second century BC, and some of the Dead Sea Scrolls use it. But every surviving manuscript of the Hebrew Bible, including the Leningrad Codex, is written in the square script, and Jewish scribes have copied the Bible in it for over two thousand years. The two scripts have the same twenty-two letters in the same order, so the change of shapes did not change the letters of the text. Part 7 teaches you to read the old script.

How it is built

To read a written Hebrew word, take four steps.

  1. Start at the right. The first letter of the word is the rightmost one.
  2. Name each letter in turn, moving left. Many letters differ by one small stroke, so look closely (see Watch out).
  3. At the left end, look for a final form. Five letters change shape when they end a word: כ becomes ך, מ becomes ם, נ becomes ן, פ becomes ף, צ becomes ץ. In four of them the tail is drawn straight down instead of bending to the left. Final mem closes into a box. The final form is the same letter with the same sound.
  4. The letter ש has two values, š and ś. A dot on its right or left arm says which (lesson 2).

Examples

Watch out

Look-alike letters. The square script has groups of letters that differ by one stroke. Learn them as pairs from the start:

  • ב bet and כ kaf (bet has a foot at the bottom right);
  • ד dalet and ר resh (dalet has a sharp corner);
  • ה he, ח ḥet and ת taw (he has a gap at the top left; ḥet is closed at the top; taw has a foot at the bottom left);
  • ו waw, ז zayin and final ן nun (zayin has a crossbar head; final nun runs below the line);
  • ג gimel and נ nun (gimel has a foot that kicks out to the left);
  • ט ṭet, ס samekh and final ם mem (ṭet is open at the top; samekh and final mem are closed, samekh round and mem square);
  • ע ʿayin and צ ṣade (ʿayin's tail runs to the left; ṣade has a flat base).

Final forms are not extra letters. There are twenty-two letters, not twenty-seven. ך and כ are both kaf.

Connections

The Arabic alphabet is another descendant of the same ancestor, and keeps the old order in its numerical sequence (abjad: alif, bāʾ, jīm, dāl). Like Hebrew, Arabic gives some letters a different shape at the end of a word, and writes right to left. The Greek letter names alpha, beta, gamma, delta are the Semitic names, borrowed with the letters.

Exercises

  1. Write the letters of meleḵ in reading order, naming each one.
  2. In which direction does the word כֶּסֶף begin, and which of its letters is a final form?
  3. A beginner reads בֵּן as kēn. Which letter has he misread, and how do you tell the two apart?
  4. Write the five letters that have final forms, with their final shapes.
  5. Copy the words יוֹם and שֵׁם and circle (or name) the final form in each. What would the letter look like in the middle of a word?
  6. Someone tells you the Bible "was really written" in the square script. Correct the claim in two sentences.
  7. Name the letter in each pair: ד or ר in דָּבָר (first letter); ה or ח in the word for "darkness", חֹשֶׁךְ (first letter).

Worked answers

  1. מ mem, ל lamed, ך final kaf. Reading from the right: mem first, final kaf last.
  2. It begins at the right, with כ kaf. The last letter, ף, is the final form of pe.
  3. The first letter. It is ב bet, with a small foot jutting out at the bottom right. Kaf (כ) is round at the bottom with no foot. So the word is bēn "son", not kēn "so".
  4. Kaf כ ך, mem מ ם, nun נ ן, pe פ ף, ṣade צ ץ.
  5. In יוֹם the final form is ם, final mem; in the middle of a word it is מ, open at the bottom left. In שֵׁם the last letter is also final mem.
  6. The earliest biblical texts were written in the older Palaeo-Hebrew script, which Jews replaced with a script of Aramaic origin after the exile. The square script grew from that Aramaic hand and has been used for copying the Bible for over two thousand years, including in every surviving manuscript; it has the same twenty-two letters as the older script.
  7. ד dalet: it has a sharp corner at the top right. ח ḥet: it is closed across the top, while he (ה) has a gap at the top left.
